Health information exchanges (HIEs) are new organizational forms in the United States that facilitate digital exchange
of health data across participants. Many believe that these multi-sided digital platforms will become key enablers of
the digital transformation of healthcare in the United States. However, like other entrepreneurial ventures, HIEs face chal-
lenges to achieve operational maturity and ﬁnancial viability. Entrepreneurial ventures often fail to attain ﬁnancial viabil-
ity due to the initial design of the business model and the inability to evolve the model over time. We focus on the
inﬂuence of service offerings, revenue models, and their evolution on the viability of HIEs. Using a unique archival data-
set constructed from surveys of HIEs in the United States from 2008 to 2010, we ﬁnd that providing an extensive service
bundle may lead to higher operational maturity with the HIE’s increasing age, but this may deter the HIE’s potential to
achieve ﬁnancial viability. The ﬁndings also show that the inﬂuence of service offerings on ﬁnancial viability is mediated
by operational maturity. Qualitative evidence corroborates these ﬁndings and suggests that HIEs need to move from pro-
viding a set of foundational services to offering more advanced services as they evolve and that they need to appropri-
ately bundle the services using transaction-, subscription- or mixed-fee models. We discuss how these ﬁndings contribute
to research at the intersection of operations management and information systems and offer important implications for
HIEs with regard to crafting and evolving appropriate service offerings and revenue models.
Key words: digital platforms; health information exchange; service offerings; revenue model; ﬁnancial viability; operational
maturity

1. Introduction
Health information exchanges (HIEs) are a key com-
ponent of healthcare operations and delivery that
span multiple entities, including doctors, hospitals,
pharmacies, and laboratories. These multi-sided dig-
ital platforms offer the promise of streamlining
information integration (i.e., the exchange, sharing,
and consolidation of data from different sources)
across players to reduce costs and improve out-
comes in the complex U.S. health care system (Brill
2013). Although the capability to integrate informa-
tion is key to enabling better coordination in the
value chain of any product or service (Devaraj et al.
2007, Lee and Whang 2004), it is even more critical
in healthcare settings, such as clinical operations
and care delivery, where access and consolidation
of shared health information enables physicians and
hospitals to make accurate diagnoses and recom-
mend effective treatment.Beginning with Roth and Van Dierdonck (1995),
several studies in operations management have sug-
gested that the tracking and exchange of information
in health care operations, workﬂow, and delivery has
the potential to inﬂuence critical outcomes (Anderson
et al. 2014, Kraiselburd and Yadav 2013, Senot et al.
2016, Zepeda and Sinha 2016). The robust exchange
of information across health care operations and
delivery can facilitate higher quality communication
and coordination between different levels of care
and among different entities (e.g., between a hospital
and a physician’s practice). HIEs offer the promise of
enabling critical health information to ﬂow seamlessly
across the multitude of players involved in healthcare
service operations and improving patients’ diagnostic
and treatment coordination process (Demirezen et al.
2016).
Recognizing the value potential of information inte-
gration across the health care sector, several HIEs and
entrepreneurial initiatives have emerged, with the

2.1 Prior Literature
Before there was widespread adoption of IT in the
U.S. healthcare system, doctors, hospitals, and labora-
tories exchanged patient information by sending
paper-based records through the mail, couriers, orfacsimile communications. With the accelerated adop-tion of health information technologies (HITs), suchas the electronic medical records (Agarwal et al.2010b, Romanow et al. 2012), some have argued thatdigital information exchange can potentially increasethe value derived from HITs in the form of reduced
costs, and improved productivity and quality (Setia
et al. 2011), ultimately helping advance the widelysought goal of improving healthcare practice throughthe enhanced use of information systems (Feldmanand Horan 2011, Unertl et al. 2012).
However, healthcare entities have concerns about
data sharing and exchange. For example, hospitalsare often unwilling to share data due to competitive
reasons, and individual physicians are reluctant to
share data to avoid the complexities involved withdata digitization and exchange (Grossman et al. 2008,Sherer 2010). While such concerns might plausiblydeter the active participation of certain entities in vari-ous HIE initiatives and retard the growth of HIEs, oneexplanation for the less-than-stellar progress of HIEsis that they been unable to construct the correct busi-
ness model. Indeed, the strategy literature invokes the
business model as a key construct for explaining ﬁrmsurvival and performance (Chesbrough 2007, Teece2010). Prior studies suggest that business modelscan help deﬁne new organizational structures andcustomer-centric value propositions to achieve com-petitive advantage (El Sawy and Pereira 2013, Oster-walder and Pigneur 2010, Shafer et al. 2006), thereby
enabling the viability and success of the ﬁrm.
There is some disagreement in the literature about
the signiﬁcance of business models for new ventures,partly due to lack of a widely accepted deﬁnition ofwhat constitutes business model, and how it is differ-ent from related terms such as strategy or revenuemodel (El Sawy and Pereira 2013). Some researchersclaim that the growth and success of new organiza-
tions is largely attributable to the crafting of appropri-
ate business models during the planning stage(George and Bock 2011, Hedman and Kalling 2003),while others argue that business models are simplydemonstration activities to satisfy investors and pro-moters (Lange et al. 2007). Researchers point to incon-clusive empirical results pertaining to the inﬂuenceof business models on ﬁrm success (Gruber 2007,
Karlsson and Honig 2009), underscoring the need for
further work on this relationship. In a review ofKhuntia, Mithas, and Agarwal: Viability of Health Information Exchanges

2.2. Hypotheses Development
We build on Teece’s (2010) arguments to suggest that
the customer value proposition instantiated in serviceofferings and the revenue model will inﬂuence theﬁnancial viability and operational maturity of HIEs.
The former outcome reﬂects the extent to which the
ﬁrm is able to generate revenue that is greater than itscosts (i.e., if the HIE has achieved ﬁnancial breakevenas a result of operational revenue), and the latterreﬂects the progress of the ﬁrm through differentstages, independent of its ﬁnancial achievements.

Prior research suggests that a business model is not astatic construct. McGrath (2010, p. 248) argues that
“business models often cannot be fully anticipated in
advance. Rather, they must be learned over time.”Other work reinforces the dynamic nature of businessmodel evolution that occurs through learning andexperience in response to the changing environmentand customer needs (Rindova and Kotha 2001, Zolloand Winter 2002). Experimentation and learning arearguably even more critical for entrepreneurial ven-
tures that seek to create a new market and fulﬁll a
new customer need that previously did not exist inthe environment.
We contextualize these ideas in the HIE setting and
investigate the moderating effects of HIE age on therelationship between business model componentsand ﬁnancial viability. In addition, we argue that theeffects of business model components on ﬁnancial
viability are mediated by operational maturity. Note
that age and maturity are two different concepts here.Age of a ﬁrm captures the number of years of exis-tence since initiation, whereas operational maturityreﬂects the organization’s evolution of outcomes withrespect to achieving speciﬁc goals. In other words,while age provides a time-dependent learning effectfor a ﬁrm to identify and leverage service and revenue
opportunities, higher levels of operational maturity
reﬂect a ﬁrm’s ability to respond to environmentalconditions and changes and position its services andrevenue opportunities appropriately to improveﬁnancial performance.
Deciding what to bring to market (i.e., the ﬁrm’s
products or services) is arguably the most criticalaspect of the ﬁrm’s business model. We consider ﬁveservice offerings that are central to HIEs’ mission,
and are aligned with the meaningful-use criteria for-mulated by the U.S. federal government to stimulatethe adoption and use of electronic health records(EHRs) by eligible professionals and hospitals (Blu-menthal and Tavenner 2010, Hogan and Kissam2010): (1) connectivity to EHRs, or the HIE’s capabil-ity to connect to hospitals, physicians, and clinics; (2)
exchange of health summaries for continuity of care,
which enables customers to access patients’ priorclinical information (medication history, eligibilityinformation, lab history, immunization history andclinical care documents) through the HIE interface;(3) electronic prescribing (e-prescribing) functionwhich transmits prescription-related informationdigitally between prescribers, dispensers, and phar-
macy managers through the HIE; (4) alerts to health-

These ﬁve
service offerings can enable the HIE to offer a richer
portfolio of services in the future by implementingadditional functionalities such as a clinical datarepository, a master patient index, a record locatorservice, telemedicine, medication management, anddisease or immunization registries (Overhage et al.2005, Vest and Jasperson 2010). Providing all the ser-vices in the initial periods may not be feasible for all
HIEs (Vest and Gamm 2010), and as such, there is
signiﬁcant variation in offerings across differentHIEs even when we consider the ﬁve servicesmentioned above.

Although isolating an association between exten-
siveness of service offerings and ﬁnancial viability ofHIEs would be informative by itself, our focus is onan arguably more important unanswered questionrelated to how the evolution of service offerings inﬂu-

Absorptive capac-ity has been deﬁned as “a ﬁrm’s ability to recognizethe value of new information, assimilate it, and applyit to commercial ends” (Cohen and Levinthal 1990,p. 128). This is particularly relevant in the U.S. health-care context, which is rapidly undergoing signiﬁcant
transformations triggered by changes in technology,
customer expectations, and regulations. Policy initia-tives such as the Health Information Technology forEconomic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act of 2009and the Patient Protection and Affordable CareAct (PPACA) of 2010 are changing the nature of com-petition and are increasingly requiring healthcareproviders to modify their delivery practices. This
ever-changing environment requires a ﬁrm to use its
learning and absorptive capacities to respond to theseexternal conditions (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Laneand Lubatkin 1998). Studies argue that acquiringabsorptive capacity is a dynamic process that unfoldswith a ﬁrm’s evolution over time. Through the evolu-tion process, a ﬁrm learns to manage its interactionswith partners and customers for greater value cre-
ation, and accordingly changes its strategic behavior
to reduce uncertainties associated with the valuecreation process (Zollo and Winter 2002).
Because the value proposition of HIEs in the U.S.
healthcare setting is not clearly established, the learn-ing process over time is likely to play an importantrole in their maturity. Evidence suggests that HIEs arestill experimenting with and transforming their oper-
ational strategy to better align with varying customer
needs, and they are still learning how to best orienttheir resources and capabilities for optimal valuecreation. This is true in any nascent market in whichcustomer needs and product/service offerings co-evolve with each other. For example, early HIEs hadperceived EHR connectivity to be the best and sim-plest process for data exchange and therefore focused
on improving their EHR integration capabilities. Sub-
sequently, HIEs learned that EHRs could be costlypropositions for physicians (Shapiro et al. 2007) andbegan to explore alternatives for data exchange.
Over time, customers participating in HIEs have
demanded a wider and richer service portfolio thanwas initially expected by many of the early HIEs (Vestand Jasperson 2010). Many early HIEs expendedsubstantial time and effort on technology evaluations
at the expense of focusing on community buy-in,stakeholder relationships, data sharing agreements,and operational processes. Although new HIEs havethe opportunity to learn from the mistakes and expe-rience of older HIEs, enacting the learning requires a
certain degree of maturity on the part of the newHIEs. Convincing HIE stakeholders to participate and
pay for data exchange and other services involves
understanding stakeholders’ needs and meeting themwith an appropriate service offering (Zhang et al.2015). Not only does age reﬂect the number of yearsan HIE has been in existence, it is also a proxy for thetime it takes to develop an improved ability to under-stand customers’ circumstances and environmentalchanges. The adoption of strategies to meet the needs
of the environment and its related dynamics requires
preparedness on the part of the ﬁrm. We suggest thatalthough information may be available, a ﬁrm’s abil-ity to grasp and digest the information and act on it isdependent on its age. service bundle for hospitals and a less extensive
service option for physicians). Older HIEs also havehad more opportunities to receive feedback fromcustomers and further reﬁne their services. This maynot be the case for a relatively younger HIE that doesnot have complete knowledge of customers’ evolvingneeds. Although the foregoing reasoning may suggest that
a transaction-based revenue model is associated with
lower viability in early stages and higher viability in
later stages, we do not posit a directional hypothesisbecause customers may still prefer a subscription-based revenue model due to the relative ease ofadministration. Given the lack of theoretical orempirical work in this area, we do not formalize anya priori expectations. Instead, we explore how the
choice of revenue model inﬂuences the viability of the
HIE, contingent on its age, as an empirical question.
As with basic and advanced services, we discuss thechoices and evolution of revenue models using quali-tative insights from four HIEs.

We begin our qualitative analyses with IHIE (www.
ihie.org). Launched in 2004 with the objective to con-nect healthcare providers in the state of Indiana, IHIEhas become the ﬁrst successful HIE. During its forma-tive years, IHIE offered a minimal service of sharing afew elements of patient records with participatingdoctors and hospitals through a secure network; inother words, it started with one foundational service.
The sustainability strategy of IHIE was based on the
principle “to support the concept of service evolution
and operational maturity ” (Overhage 2009). Over time,
IHIE offered a growing portfolio of advanced services(which IHIE referred to as “value-added services”) tovarious stakeholders, including exchange of currentdata about admissions, discharges, and transfers, andhad a strong collaboration with health plans and
accountable care organizations to help them realize

IHIE was grant-funded to create a clinical reposi-

In later years, IHIEtransitioned its repository services to a fee-basedservice that linked clinical messaging and repositoryservices into bundled services and offered these bun-dles to hospitals and health systems with fees in
proportion to their business volume (Weaver 2012).
The current fee structure is a layered monthly sub-scription-based model, where charges range by thetype and volume of service and are paid by healthcare providers, physician ofﬁces, and medical institu-tions. Thus, over time, IHIE moved from foundationalto advanced services and evolved the structure of itsservice offering from initial fee-based models to more
sophisticated and ﬂexible subscription-based models.
In addition, a collaboration plan with providersacross Indiana provided IHIE enough stakeholderbuy-in to pay for its services and achieve ﬁnancialviability.
The second HIE we examine is HealthBridge

HealthBridge started with only patient data access
(e.g., a foundational service) and then graduallyoffered more value-added and advanced services,such as patient health records, aggregation and ana-lytical data capabilities, and quality improvementprojects. In terms of its revenue model, HealthBridgehas evolved to offer a hybrid approach (McCarthyet al. 2014). Currently, it does not charge any fees for
clinician access to data and has designed a mix of

In recent years, HealthBridge earned approx-imately 85% of its operational revenues from sub-scription fees charged to health systems using the
exchange, with the remaining 15% obtained from fees

5. Discussion
5.1. Findings and Contributions
The goal of this study has been to understand how
the evolution of service offerings inﬂuences the opera-tional maturity and ﬁnancial viability of HIE. We ﬁndthat the age of the HIE positively moderates the inﬂu-ence of number of service offerings on operational
maturity; but has a negative moderation effect on
ﬁnancial viability. The ﬁndings indicate thatKhuntia, Mithas, and Agarwal: Viability of Health Information Exchanges

compared with new entrants, older HIEs are better
able to leverage a larger number of services throughtheir experience gained and can bundle and structureservices for customers to achieve higher operationalmaturity. However, merely providing extensive ser-vices may not be effective in achieving ﬁnancialbreakeven as time progresses, because the ﬁndingsindicate that although service offerings have stronger
positive effects on operational maturity with age,
more service offerings may have a negative impact onﬁnancial breakeven. A plausible explanation for thisunexpected ﬁnding is that service offerings may leadto maturity, but earning sufﬁcient revenue to achieveﬁnancial breakeven may require service structuringand a bundling focus rather than merely servicediversiﬁcation.
The results also indicate that the power of the trans-
action-based revenue model on the ﬁnancial breake-ven of HIEs decreases with time. We suggest that thetransaction-based revenue model generates lowerrevenue potential for older HIEs, because customerswant a more established HIE to offer differing servicebundles through subscription models, thus allowingthem to pick and choose service bundles and sub-
scriptions based on their speciﬁc needs. Although in
principle, an HIE can earn revenue via both transac-tions and subscriptions, relying on transactions aloneis not recommended for viability. At an early stage,given the limitations of minimal service offerings,transaction fees are more appropriate to earn revenue,but in long run, subscription-based models areneeded to accrue the higher revenue necessary to
achieve viability.
These results are counter-intuitive and challenge
conventional wisdom in at least two ways. First, atransaction-based revenue model may be more attrac-tive to generate revenue for HIEs because it equates topayment for every unit of service delivered. However,our results suggest that an established and incumbentHIE runs a higher risk of failure by exclusively adopt-
ing a transaction-based revenue model. Second, even
though transaction-based fees for minimal servicesoffered by younger HIEs may be attractive for cus-tomers, over time, customers may prefer to subscribeto services that they use regularly rather than payingfor single transaction-based fees. Thus, HIEs need toassess their service offerings vis-/C18a-vis revenue poten-
tial carefully.
Although some of these decisions are also context
dependent, our results suggest that conﬁguring ser-vices to deliver actionable and usable data, and devel-oping a revenue generation strategy for viabilitycontribute more to the success of a ﬂedgling HIE thansimply focusing on having the most advanced tech-nology platform, which many HIEs have erroneouslytended to concentrate on. Although making goodchoices about the technology that will power an HIE
is undoubtedly important, creating a viable revenuemodel and delivering value to stakeholders mustreceive signiﬁcant and appropriate attention.
Finally, the effect of operational maturity on ﬁnan-
cial viability indicates that without functional andoperational progress, long-term viability may not befeasible for an HIE. While the organization may be
able to earn high revenue from a handful of key cus-
tomers in the beginning, continued revenue genera-tion depends greatly on the functional operationalprogress and evolution of the HIE.
This study makes several contributions to existing
operations management and information systems lit-erature. We extend prior work that explores the via-bility of new ventures and entrepreneurial initiatives
(Audretsch 2003, Brinckmann et al. 2010, Delmar and
Shane 2003, 2004, Osterwalder et al. 2005) by investi-gating how service offerings and revenue modelsinﬂuence operational maturity and ﬁnancial viability.We contribute to the emerging literature on theappropriateness of foundational vs. advanced servicebundles in the context of HIEs, a new IT-enabledorganizational form in the U.S. healthcare ecosystem
that seeks to facilitate information exchange across
ﬁrms (Zott et al. 2011). Furthermore, in examiningrevenue model evolution and its effect on HIE viabil-ity, we extend discussions in the recent literatureabout how business models need to evolve dynami-cally in response to market demand, environmentalchanges, and organizational learning with passage oftime (Demil and Lecocq 2010). The ﬁndings of this
study, in terms of service focus vs. diversiﬁcation and
revenue models, are applicable to other ﬁrms thatserve as platforms for information exchange amongdifferent players or sides.
5.2 Limitations and Implications for Research
We acknowledge the following limitations of the
study that future research should seek to overcome.
First, because of data limitations and the use of a
cross-sectional design, our results should be consid-ered associational; that is, they do not imply anycausal relationship. Longitudinal data or quasi-experimental studies can help assess the causal effectof focal variables on HIE survival in a better way(Mithas and Krishnan 2009).
Second, while our sample includes all the HIEs in
existence over a 3-year study period, including those
that have failed to break even, it excludes those thatdid not appear across the 3 years or completely failedand shut down before or after the 3 years. While thesample we used was appropriate for our researchquestions, as it exhibited sufﬁcient variability inﬁnancial viability, we were unable to address thequestion of what factors determine HIE survival.

Several other opportunities exist to extend the
work presented here. While our results shed lighton how some components of business models inﬂu-ence the viability of new ventures engaged withexchange activities, there is still a need to under-stand the ﬁner-grained processes through which
these speciﬁc factors inﬂuence viability. For exam-
ple, how do different service bundles maximizerevenue to inﬂuence viability? What is the optimalstrategy for service differentiation? Further, a quan-tiﬁcation of the value that an HIE participant gainsneeds to be established. For instance, HIEs todaysuch as the Chesapeake Regional Information Sys-tem for our Patients (CRISP) that serves Maryland
and the District of Columbia offer advanced ser-
vices such as a prescription drug monitoring pro-gram. The extent to which such offerings improveefﬁciency and health outcomes can be examined.Opportunities also exist to study more macroimpacts of IT in health care to assess the extent towhich IT and related digital innovations can helpto improve overall outcomes such as life expec-
tancy (Mithas et al. 2009), and overcome what is
referred to as the Baumol’s cost disease. Exploringthese impacts of HIEs, as well the value customersderive from using and interacting with sustainableHIEs, will help inform the economic rationalebehind the value potential, attractiveness of partici-pation, and diffusion of these ﬁrms in the U.S.healthcare system.
5.3 Managerial Implications
Several managerial and policy implications follow
from our ﬁndings. First, we ﬁnd that a set of robustservice offerings are important to establish a ﬁrm’svalue proposition and foundation for operationalmaturity in the formative years and continues to besigniﬁcant as the ﬁrm establishes itself in the market.An early offering of a services bundle helps the ﬁrm
achieve viability during its initial inception years,though as time passes, merely providing a servicebundle without careful attention to the types of ser-vices being bundled is not effective for achievingﬁnancial viability. HIE managers need to be proactivein researching customers’ needs and considerationswhen crafting their services portfolio and organiza-
tional capabilities because research suggests that
managers’ intuition regarding customer expectationsis not always reliable (Hult et al. 2017). This is espe-cially important when there are diverse customer seg-ments with different needs, as is the case for HIEorganizations. Moreover, if the service offerings arealigned with the proper incentives for customers,there is a higher likelihood of customer participation
in the HIE.
Our qualitative analyses suggest that as the HIE pro-
gresses, viability based only on foundational servicesdiminishes. With the passage of time, it is important tooffer more advanced services that reﬂect customers’evolving needs. Thus, HIEs need to focus on speciﬁcservice portfolios in response to market demand, whilemaintaining the adeptness to deliver a richer service
bundle to meet the needs of all customers. HIEs
need to closely monitor the meaningful-use criteriareleases and other requirements mandated by theU.S. federal government regarding digital capabilitiesthat hospitals and physicians need to demonstrate,as this will help them determine how the servicebundle should evolve in response to environmentalchange.
Second, HIEs should exercise care when planning
their revenue models. In the early stages, transac-tions may be helpful in conducting business withcustomers. However, transaction-based revenuemodels are not likely to support ﬁnancial viabilityat a later stage. Managers need to think creativelyabout how to design appropriate revenue structuresand choose the right revenue model using both
bundles and subscriptions, and how to evolve these
over time based on the structure and characteristicsof their ﬁrms.
Finally, operational maturity is an inﬂuential factor
in shaping the capability to earn revenue throughoperations and related efﬁciency gains. The moderat-ing role of age and the mediating role of operationalmaturity indicate that HIE managers need to
approach viability as an ongoing process that requires
making changes in components of the business modelover time. Once the HIE is operationally mature, itcan offer enhanced service bundles that create valuefor its stakeholders. For instance, an operational HIEshould frequently reach out to hospitals and provi-ders to identify challenges and opportunities toimprove workﬂows or create efﬁciencies through

In conclusion, this study examines how the evolu-
tion of various components of a business model (e.g.,number and type of service offerings, revenue model)inﬂuences the viability of HIEs. We draw on and
extend prior literature on the design and evolution of
business models in a consequential and understudiedsetting —namely, the use of IT-enabled data and infor-
mation exchange in the healthcare sector. We ﬁnd thata higher number of service offerings increases theoperational maturity potential of older HIEs, but thismay not be useful for achieving ﬁnancial viability.Over time, the HIE needs to offer more advanced
services than the foundational services initiated in itsearly years. In addition, as the age of an HIE
increases, transaction-based revenue models are notappropriate for achieving viability. These ﬁndingsshould help policymakers and managers plan anddesign viable HIEs as they seek to deliver affordable,accessible, and quality healthcare.
