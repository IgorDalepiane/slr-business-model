Converting free users to paid subscribers in the SaaS context:
The impact of marketing touchpoints, message content, and usage
Hongshuang (Alice) Li
Department of Marketing & Logistics, Ohio State
University, Columbus, Ohio, USA
Correspondence

Handling editor : Fred FeinbergAbstract
As software-as-a-service (SaaS) becomes an increasingly popular business model, free-
trial acquisition, which allows prospective consumers to explore the service at no
charge, also becomes a norm for SaaS companies. However, empirical research on
the role of marketing communications, consumers’ free-trial usage, and their interac-
tions is still lacking. This study utilizes the granular data from a leading SaaS ﬁrm
that provides prospects with free trials and examines the impact of and interactions
among various marketing touchpoints, different types of message content, and the fre-
quency and variety of free-trial usage on consumers’ subscription decisions. Regarding
advertising effectiveness, this study ﬁnds that consumer-initiated touchpoints with all
message content enhance conversion, but the ﬁrm-initiated touchpoints conveying per-
suasive messages discourage conversion. Meanwhile, free-trial usage does not always
follow the old adage “the more, the merrier.” When usage is unpacked as frequency and
variety, more frequent free-trial usage encourages conversion, but exploring a greater
variety of software leads to a lower conversion rate. For the focal ﬁrm, advertising and
free-trial usage attenuate each other in leading to conversion, except for the persuasive
messages sent to frequent users and featured messages to variety-seeking users. The
robustness of these ﬁndings is tested, and two simulations demonstrate how the model
helps ﬁrms determine when to contact consumers with additional display ads or emails.
This research provides guidance to SaaS managers on the impact of advertising, free-
trial usage, and the interplay between both on conversion, which facilitates decisions
on marketing strategies and resource allocations.
KEYWORDS
free-trial acquisition, marketing touchpoints, message content, software usage, software-as-a-service
1 INTRODUCTION
Software-as-a-service (SaaS) is a popular business model that
centrally hosts and licenses software on a subscription basis.
Many software companies such as Microsoft and Salesforce
have adopted the SaaS model. Under the SaaS model, cus-
tomers pay a monthly or annual subscription fee that is usu-
ally less expensive than the price of a perpetual license. Con-
sumers can access the software via the Internet, while the
SaaS vendors offer the software remotely on cloud-based
hardware. SaaS companies widely use free trials to acquire
new customers by providing free access to full-ﬂedged ser-
vice during a limited time window. Prospects can sign up
for free trials, usually by providing emails and/or other con-

Then, the paramount next step for SaaS ﬁrms is to convert
these free-trial users to paid users.
Many SaaS ﬁrms are concerned with the low conversion
rate at the end of a free trial and invest tremendous market-
ing resources in engaging customers and encouraging their
software usage. Despite the pervasive presence of the SaaS
model, and a projected $172 billion global market by 2022
(Statista, 2021 ), it is still unclear to what extent consumers’
engagement in marketing communication and free-trial usage
inﬂuences the likelihood of their conversion. Is a type of mes-
sage content more effective via certain marketing media than
others? Are busier free-trial users more likely to convert? Do
marketing efforts and free-trial usage accentuate or attenuate
each other in leading to a higher conversion rate? This study
will address these questions and shed light on the impact of

marketing communications, free-trial usage, as well as the
interactions between them.
SaaS managers send marketing communications through
multiple media with varied content in order to convince
prospective consumers to make a purchase. Nowadays, many
ﬁrms have access to rich touchpoint data left by consumers
on their path to purchase. Managers can track advertis-
ing impressions and responses at the consumer level with
improved precision. However, as marketing communications
are usually evaluated independently from customers’ usage
behavior, this one-sided interpretation could be biased. Mean-
while, the extant research on message content is usually
focused on a single advertising medium, such as display
ads (Bruce et al., 2017 ), emails (Sahni et al., 2018 ), or
search ads (Ghose & Yang, 2009 ). The observation of mes-
sage content across multiple media enables this study to
examine the relationship between different types of message
content and marketing media in the presence of free-trial
usage.
Apart from engagement with various forms of marketing
communication, consumers’ free-trial usage also reﬂects their
engagement with the ﬁrm. As consumers explore the product
or service during the free trial, they may use it frequently to
reduce their uncertainty about the utility of a long-term sub-
scription. Since the free-trial window is the same for all users,
usage intensity becomes an important indicator of consumer
engagement. Usage behaviors, such as the variety of products
explored by the same user and how often those products are
used, are key indicators of a user’s learning and assessment
of the subscription utility. As the service is full-featured and
the access is unlimited during the free-trial window, usage
is mainly driven by true underlying demand. If the usage
intensity has a positive and signiﬁcant relationship with con-
versions, then it is in the ﬁrm’s interest to encourage usage
among free-trial users. However, consumer behavior research
ﬁnds that consumers usually fail to estimate their usage fre-
quency before making a purchase (Goodman & Irmak, 2013 ),
and usage experience with a higher level of variety can lower
satisfaction and purchase likelihood (Etkin & Sela, 2016 ).
Thus, whether usage matters and how it inﬂuences conver-
sions is still unclear for SaaS managers.
The empirical research on usage in a contractual setting is
insufﬁcient (Ascarza & Hardie, 2013 ), with even fewer stud-
ies focused on free-trial usage. Some researchers have investi-
gated the relationship between postpurchase usage and reten-
tion (Ascarza & Hardie, 2013 ) or the postpurchase usage by
consumers acquired from different routes (Datta et al., 2015 ).
However, the research on usage behaviors during the free-
trial period and how the free-trial usage affects purchase is
still scarce. Moreover, the usage intensity tracked by ﬁrms
usually includes multiple metrics, such as the variety of ser-
vices a user has tried or the frequency a user explores the ser-
vice, but the research categorizing consumer engagement into
various types of usage behaviors has been inadequate (Lee
et al., 2017 ). This research adds to the literature by examining
two dimensions of the consumer’s free-trial usage—variety
and frequency—in a granular manner, and whether eitheror both dimensions of usage would increase consumers’
conversion.
Previous work has documented a mixed relationship
between advertising and usage experience (Vakratsas &
Ambler, 1999 ; Wright & Lynch, 1995 ). On the one hand,
Bruce et al. ( 2012 ) examine the advertising and sales of a
soft drink brand and ﬁnd that usage increases sales via cog-
nition and affect. On the other hand, Smith ( 1993 ) shows that
advertising reduces brand cognition and brand attitude with
lab experiments. Ackerberg ( 2001 ) also ﬁnds that the effec-
tiveness of advertising on experienced consumers who have
made purchases previously is less than that on inexperienced
consumers. These ﬁndings are all conditional on past pur-
chases, which differ from the SaaS setting, where prospects
gain experience with the product prior to the very ﬁrst pur-
chase during the free trial. Therefore, this study enriches the
understanding of the interactions between free trial usage and
marketing communication.
In summary, this research adds to the extant litera-
ture through an empirical study that examines the impact
of and interactions among various marketing touchpoints,
types of message content, and free-trial usage. The results
show a positive cumulative impact of advertising in vari-
ous marketing touchpoints and message contents. Interest-
ingly, the consumer-initiated touchpoints with all message
content enhance conversion, but the ﬁrm-initiated touchpoints
conveying persuasive messages discourage conversion. For
usage, the cumulative impact of usage frequency is positive,
whereas that of usage variety is negative. Regarding the ad-
usage interactions, the focal ﬁrm’s advertising and free-trial
usage attenuate each other, but persuasive messages sent to
frequent users and featured messages to variety-seeking users
still help convert prospects. These ﬁndings provide guidance
to SaaS managers in assessing consumers’ engagement in
terms of marketing responsiveness and free-trial usage, which
can facilitate better decision-making in their marketing strate-
gies and resource allocation.

2.1 Conceptual framework
Many SaaS ﬁrms offer free trials to attract prospective cus-
tomers. The free trial enables a unique opportunity to observe
the prospects’ usage behavior prior to purchase. During and
after the free trial, prospects may also be exposed to the ﬁrms’
advertising and interact with the ﬁrm’s marketing communi-
cations. In keeping with the empirical context of this study,

2.2 Marketing touchpoints
Marketing communication can provide information for free-
trial users to reduce their uncertainty about a product and
thus affect conversions, as represented in Figure 1by the
arrow pointing from advertising to conversion. A wealth of
literature provides valuable insights into consumers’ expo-
sures and responses to a ﬁrm’s marketing communication and
how advertising inﬂuences their conversion propensity. This
research focuses on six types of marketing touchpoints—the
impressions and clicks of display ads and emails, and organic
and paid search clicks.
Extant empirical studies have revealed a mixed impact of
display ads. Drèze and Hussherr ( 2003 ) ﬁnd that display ads
inﬂuence the preattentive level of information processing and
increase ad and brand recall. Manchanda et al. ( 2006 )s h o w
that more display ad impressions and more sites and pages
containing the display ads improve the repeat purchase prob-
ability. However, several ﬁeld experiments also ﬁnd insigniﬁ-
cant effects of display ads on purchases, possibly due to small
advertising effects or small sample sizes (Zantedeschi et al.,
2017 ). When investigating the heterogeneous consumer seg-
ments, Chae et al. ( 2019 ) even ﬁnd a negative marginal effect
of additional display impressions on website visits for about
a quarter of consumers.
Email marketing is found to be very effective in generating
sales. As the costs of producing email campaigns are very low
or even negligible, researchers report high returns on invest-
ment (ROIs) for email marketing (e.g., Danaher et al., 2020 ,
ﬁnd an average ROI of 2693%, and Zhang et al., 2017 , reporta 3700% ROI of emails). Marketing intensity also inﬂuences
the timing of consumers’ opt-in and opt-out decisions of the
ﬁrm’s email marketing (Kumar et al., 2014 ).
Both display ads and emails are initiated by ﬁrms, which
differ from online search marketing that consumers vol-
untarily initiate. A large volume of research supports the
effectiveness of search engine marketing. Researchers ﬁnd
that consumers may consider ﬁrm-initiated marketing has
low credibility and thus discount it (Foubert & Gijsbrechts,
2016 ). Chan et al. ( 2011 ) ﬁnd that customers acquired by
paid search ads have higher customer lifetime value than
customers acquired from other channels. In addition, paid
search advertising is found to be more effective than off-line
advertising (Dinner et al., 2014 ) and has a more enduring
impact than emails (Wiesel et al., 2011 ).
A large volume of research has examined the impact
of marketing on conversions in a multimedia environment
(Ansari et al., 2008 ; Lin et al., 2013 ; Naik & Raman, 2003 ;
Naik & Peters, 2009 ). Naik and Raman ( 2003 ) were among
the ﬁrst to deﬁne interactive media synergy as the combined
effect of multiple marketing media exceeds the sum of the
individual effects of each medium. Within this stream of lit-
erature, some research focuses on the synergy between online
and off-line media. For example, Naik and Peters ( 2009 ) eval-
uate the synergy between off-line and online media groups,
ﬁnding banner ads can increase direct website visits. Some
research on the synergy effect focuses on highly related
media such as email and catalog (Ansari et al., 2008 ; Zante-
ceschi et al., 2017 ) and organic search and paid search (Ghose
& Yang, 2009 ). As data-tracking technology advances, a large
volume of multimedia data such as touchpoint data becomes
available. More empirical studies are conducted about the
cross-media synergy (e.g., Dinner et al., 2014 ; Joo et al.,
2014 ; Zantedeschi et al., 2017 ) and synergy in a staged con-
sumer purchase funnel (e.g., Li & Kannan, 2014 ;T o d r ie ta l . ,
2020 ). This research extends the literature stream on the mul-
timedia consumer purchase journey by examining the various
interactions between touchpoints and message content and
between touchpoints and free-trial usage.
2.3 Message content
Early research on advertising message content mainly orig-
inated from psychology and consumer behavior studies
focused on the emotional versus rational aspects of the ads
(e.g., Bass et al., 2007 ; Chandy et al., 2001 ; MacInnis et al.,
2002 ). There are relatively few ﬁeld studies on the effects of
ad content, especially for digital ads (Bruce et al., 2017 ;L e e
et al., 2018 ).
Researchers have unpacked the message content of dis-
play ads on a few dimensions. For example, Hupfer and Grey
(2005 ) ﬁnd that display ads that offer a free sample have
higher click-through rates than those without. Bruce et al.
(2017 ) categorize display ads into price-related messages and
product-related messages and ﬁnd the former is more effec-
tive, and only price-related ads are effective for retargeted

ads. Lee et al. ( 2018 ) classify ads into brand-personality-
related content and directly informative content and asso-
ciate the content attributes with consumers’ likes, comments,
and shares on Facebook. They ﬁnd that messages contain-
ing deals and promotions are associated with higher click-
through rates. However, some directly informative content,
such as mentions of prices and deals, is associated with lower
levels of engagement when included in messages in isolation.
Additionally, more diversiﬁed ads can help reduce annoyance
elicitation (Todri et al., 2020 ).
The research examining email content is primarily focused
on the impact of personalization on email effectiveness
(Ansari & Mela, 2003 ). For example, Sahni et al. ( 2018 ) ﬁnd
that when the emails include noninformative content such
as the names of email recipients, consumers’ probability of
opening emails increases by 20%, and the opt-out probability
is reduced by 17%. On the contrary, Wattal et al. ( 2012 ) ﬁnd
that such personalization negatively impacts the advertiser.
In search engine marketing, researchers have explored the
characteristics of search keywords, including brand names,
retailer names, geo-locations, the popularity of keywords, and
the speciﬁcity of keywords (Ghose & Yang, 2009 ; Li et al.,
2016 ; Rutz & Bucklin, 2011 ). Recent applications of machine
learning techniques extract latent semantic meanings from
search phrases and evaluate the keyword ambiguity (Gong
et al., 2018 ), consumers’ content preference (Liu & Toubia,
2018 ), and the impact of latent themes on purchase propen-
sity (Li & Ma, 2020 ).
Different from the existing content categorization in the lit-
erature, which is usually attached to the presence of certain
keywords, such as prices, brands, and consumers’ personal
information, this study enriches the literature with a new cat-
egorization wherein the ads are grouped into awareness, per-
suasive, and featured products, depending on the wholistic
message delivered in an ad. The rich set of message content
renders substantive ﬁndings of awareness, persuasive, and
featured content and their effectiveness via different market-
ing channels and their interactions with the free-trial usage.
2.4 Usage of free trials
Free trial is a popular sampling strategy widely adopted by
SaaS ﬁrms. While the research on sampling (Bawa & Shoe-
maker, 2004 ) provides valuable insights for understanding the
free-trial promotion, this study is related to a smaller and
newer group of studies on information goods offered in the
SaaS settings (Jones & Mendelson, 2011 ; Yoganarasimhan
et al., 2019 ). The insights from sampling research on con-
sumer goods may not be directly applicable to the free trials
in the SaaS setting.
Through usage, consumers learn to resolve uncertainty
and update their valuation of the service (Bolton & Lemon,
1999 ; Foubert & Gijsbrechts, 2016 ; Sriram et al., 2015 ).
Extant research has revealed a positive relationship between
usage and retention or repeat purchases (e.g., Ascarza &
Hardie, 2013 ; Lemon et al., 2002 ). Thus, in Figure 1,t h efree trial usage affects consumers’ conversion decisions, as
represented by the straight arrow pointing from usage to con-
version. The curved arrows reﬂect the positive relationship
between current usage and future usage, as supported by
numerous empirical studies (e.g., Bolton & Lemon, 1999 ;
Datta et al., 2015 ).
During the free-trial period, usage intensity is a key indi-
cator of consumers’ learning and assessment of the sub-
scription utility. Consumers have heterogeneous and dynamic
learning rates regarding product quality (Sriram et al., 2015 ).
Researchers have explored the impact of the free-trial acqui-
sition on customer retention and behaviors after conversion
(Datta et al., 2015 ; Lee et al., 2019 ). Despite the popularity
of free trials as an acquisition tool, few empirical studies have
been conducted to understand how the usage of a free trial
affects conversions. One exception is by Foubert and Gijs-
brechts ( 2016 ), who study the timing and usage intensity of
free trials on purchases, where consumers learn the quality
of the service from usage, word-of-mouth, advertising, and
direct marketing. They ﬁnd the overall effect of free trials is
positive, but it also depends on the timing and consumers’
learning.
In all these studies, usage is measured either by the inter-
purchase time (e.g., Borle et al., 2008 ) or the frequency in
a given period (e.g., Ascarza & Hardie, 2013 ; Sriram et al.,
2015 ). In fact, usage intensity can be composed of multiple
metrics. Besides the usage frequency, the variety of usage is
also an important factor in consumers’ free-trial engagement.
However, empirical studies that quantify multiple usage
behaviors are still lacking (Lee et al., 2017 ). Recent research
in consumer behavior reveals that a less varied usage experi-
ence leads consumers to overestimate their usage frequency
and thus increase satisfaction and repeat purchases (Etkin &
Sela, 2016 ). Etkin and Mogilner ( 2016 ) also ﬁnd more varied
activities make people feel less productive, echoing the ﬁnd-
ing of feature fatigue (Thompson et al., 2005 ), which occurs
when consumers are overwhelmed by products overloaded
with too many features. This study unpacks the usage behav-
iors into both frequency and variety, as shown in Figure 1,
and adds substantive ﬁndings to the literature on the role of
usage frequency and variety of the free trial on conversions.
2.5 Interactions between advertising and
free-trial usage
The interaction between advertising and free-trial usage is
represented by the two horizontal arrows in the center of Fig-
ure1. Both positive and negative interactions between adver-
tising and usage experience are documented in the literature.
Bruce et al. ( 2012 ), in the context of soft drink products, ﬁnd
that usage enhances the cognition and affect of the product,
leading to sales growth. On the other hand, consumer behav-
ior research has shown that when consumers are provided
with both advertising and a free trial, the former is no longer
the only indicator of product quality, and thus its effects on
brand cognitions and attitude are reduced as compared to

consumers’ usage experience is more impactful in revealing
quality than word of mouth, adverting, and direct market-
ing, as it reduces uncertainty and updates consumers’ quality
belief at a faster pace. However, neither Datta et al. (2015)
nor Foubert and Gijsbrechts ( 2016 ) capture the interaction
between marketing and usage. This research ﬁlls in this gap
in the literature with empirical ﬁndings of the impact of con-
sumers’ free-trial usage and its interplay with marketing com-
munication in converting free users to paid users.
In summary, this study contributes to the literature with
empirical ﬁndings of the impact of multimedia advertising,
message content, free-trial usage, and especially the interplay
among them. Table 1compares this research against the most
relevant articles. The potential contribution of this research
mainly lies in the new substantive ﬁndings of the interac-
tions between marketing touchpoints and message content,
the cumulative impact of and interactions between usage
frequency and variety, and the ad-usage interactions. These
new substantive ﬁndings offer managers guidance to assess
their consumers’ marketing responses and free-trial usage and
optimize their ﬁrms’ marketing communication and resource
allocation during and after the free trial.
3 DATA
3.1 Contextual background
The SaaS business model has a few unique characteristics
that distinguish it from other business models for information
goods. First, SaaS usually licenses and delivers software on a
subscription basis. Second, SaaS ﬁrms usually provide time-
locked free trials of their full-ﬂedged services. The period
of a free trial often ranges from a week to a month. Third,
in the context of SaaS, the versioning decision is less of a
concern than that for on-premise software. Because the soft-
ware maintenance and updates are continuously and instantly
done on the cloud by SaaS ﬁrms, consumers no longer makedynamic and strategic decisions to accelerate or postpone
their purchase of a newer version of the software.
It is noted that the freemium model is also widely adopted
by ﬁrms to attract prospects by providing access to free prod-
ucts and services (Arora et al., 2017 ; Wagner et al., 2014 ).
Researchers have studied the externality of the freemium
model (Shi et al., 2019 ), the conditions to provide the free
version (Lambrecht & Misra, 2017 ), and its optimal design
(Lee et al., 2019 ). Firms using the freemium model often
distinguish between the free and paid versions of their ser-
vices. The free version usually offers lower quality or limited
features (e.g., reduced computing or storage capacity), but
remains available for an unlimited time. Free trial in the SaaS
business model differs from the free version in the freemium
model, as SaaS ﬁrms usually provide access to their full ser-
vice during a free trial with the same quality as the paid ser-
vice, but for a limited time. That is, the free version in the
freemium model is quality-locked, and the free trial in the
SaaS model is time-locked. A consumer in the freemium set-
ting has to make the purchase decision in the presence of the
free version (Gu et al., 2018 ).
The SaaS model is also similar to content streaming ser-
vices, such as Spotify and Netﬂix, in subscription-based
recurring payment, time-locked free trial, and continuous
maintenance and updates. However, in the latter case, cus-
tomers are paying for the content, while the software is only
used as a tool to access the content. Learning to use software
is very different from consuming digital content. Therefore,
rather than simply imposing the knowledge or experience
from traditional software with perpetual licenses or similar
business models with free samples, managers and researchers
need to consider these differences and develop new insights
to guide the daily practice at SaaS companies.
To this end, this research collects data from a U.S.-based
multinational computer software company that uses the SaaS
business model. It offers a collection of more than 20 soft-
ware applications focused on creating and publishing con-
tent in the format of text, graphics, animation, video, etc. The
ﬁrm’s software is mainly used through desktop applications,
while tablet apps and browser-based access are also available
for a subset of software on limited functions. Consumers can
subscribe to different software, for example, one for process-
ing text and the other for images, which may be used together
in the same project.
The focal ﬁrm uses free trials as an acquisition tool. All
new consumers are provided with a free trial for the ﬁrst 30
days, and there is no limit on the number of software down-
loads1or maximal usage duration in the free-trial period.
Once the free trial expires, all downloaded software is sus-
pended for further usage. To continue using the ﬁrm’s soft-
ware, the customer must subscribe, but the subscription may
be changed or even canceled at any time. Therefore, the most
important acquisition for the ﬁrm is the ﬁrst subscription to
any software, which is treated as the dependent variable in
this study.

uously and instantaneously performed on the cloud so that
consumers do not choose among various software versions.
Additionally, unlike many start-up software ﬁrms whose most
prominent concern is the awareness of their product, the focal
ﬁrm is well established in its product category and well-
known worldwide. Awareness and reputation are no longer
a major concern for the focal ﬁrm.
3.2 Data description
The dataset is a 10% random sample of all users who cre-
ated accounts by signing up their emails at the focal ﬁrm in
September 2015, yielding a sample of 14,989 unique con-
sumers. All of these new users were provided with a 30-
day free trial. However, 2692 users (about 18% of the sam-
ple) immediately subscribed. As these users essentially opted
out of the free trial, they are removed from the sample. The
remaining sample contains 12,297 prospective customers.
Within this sample, the ﬁrst 60 days from the account cre-
ation are used for analyses, and the observations during days
61–90 are the holdout sample.
In line with the conceptual framework in Figure 1, both
marketing touchpoints and message content are observed for
advertising, whereas both frequency and variety are observed
for usage. The observed marketing touchpoints include dis-
play ad impressions and click-throughs, email exposures and
clicks, and clicks on organic and paid search ads.3Addition-
ally, the content conveyed through marketing communication
is categorized into four groups: awareness (e.g., educatory
newsletters), persuasive (e.g., campaigns intended to drive
conversions), branded campaigns, and campaigns featuring
one of the ﬁrm’s content products (produced by professionals
using the focal ﬁrm’s software). Consumers’ free-trial usage
behaviors are measured by software download and launch.
Although the focal ﬁrm does not disclose the exact software
being downloaded or launched by a consumer or the dura-
tion of usage, the daily records of usage behaviors at the
individual level allow for exploring the impact of the variety
and frequency of a consumer’s usage during the free-trial
period.The data of a consumer are aligned by the sign-up date,
marked as day 0. Consumers’ marketing touchpoints, mes-
sage content, and usage behaviors are tracked from the sign-
up date until conversion (if any) or the end of the 90-day data
window, whichever is earlier. Given the sign-up for the free
trial is the ﬁrst time a prospect provides her email, any obser-
vations before this point are excluded due to data sparsity and
low tracking accuracy. The marketing communication and
usage after conversion are also excluded as this research is
focused on the conversion of free users to paid users.

4.2 Handling endogeneity
One challenge in the investigation of advertising effects is the
potential endogeneity (Zantedeschi et al., 2017 ), wherein the
regressors are correlated with the error term. In this study,
endogeneity concerns arise from three sources: temporal
simultaneity, consumers’ decisions of marketing responses
and usage, and the ﬁrm’s selective targeting. Temporal
simultaneity can occur, for example, when managers expect
increased demand and thus increase advertising intensity, giv-
ing rise to a biased estimation of the advertising effects. The
second source of endogeneity derives from consumers’ deci-
sions of ad responses and free-trial usage. Whether and how
actively consumers react to the ﬁrm’s ads may be endoge-
nously driven by their latent needs for the product. Con-
sumers’ usage of the free trial may also be indicative of their
software needs. As such, both the consumers’ ad responses
and usage can be correlated with their latent needs included
in the error term, thus posing endogeneity concerns. The third
source of endogeneity is the ﬁrm’s targeting of certain cus-
tomers with display ads and emails, leading to selection bias.
As a result of targeting, active consumers or those with higher
purchase propensities are more likely to be exposed to adver-
tising (Dong et al., 2009 ). Meanwhile, the ﬁrm might choose
to target certain consumers based on information observed by
the ﬁrm but not by the researchers, so that consumers exposed
to the ﬁrm’s advertising are different in some respect from
those not targeted (Manchanda et al., 2004 ).
This paper tackles endogeneity in three ways. First, to
address simultaneity bias, the proposed model accounts for
unobserved demand drivers (Rossi, 2014 ; Zantedeschi et al.,
2017 ), such as the availability of the free trial, the remaining
time in the free trial, seasonality, and market-wide trend. Forthe availability of the free trial, a dummy variable is included,
the value of which is 1 during the free trial, and 0 after the free
trial expires. Consumers’ progress in the free trial is measured
with both linear and quadratic terms for the elapsed days of
the free trial to capture any changes in advertising and usage
according to the consumers’ free trial progress. Moreover,
the users’ free-trial usage, marketing responses, and purchase
are likely to differ on weekdays compared with weekends
and holidays. Meanwhile, marketing managers’ expectation
and their decision of advertising intensity may also depend
on whether a date is a weekday, weekend, or holiday. Thus,
the model includes seasonality control variables—a weekday
dummy (1 on Monday through Friday, except for national
holidays, and 0 otherwise) and a holiday dummy (1 for days
around national holidays,4and 0 otherwise), while the non-
holiday weekends are set as the baseline. These two dummy
variables also help capture seasonal impact or changes due to
nationwide campaigns. Furthermore, the model includes the
Google trend index for keywords “Software as a Service” to
control the general interest in the SaaS business model and
any unobserved trend related to SaaS in the market. All these
aforementioned variables are included in the control variable
Xitin Equation ( 1).

5 EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS
The granular observations at the cookie ID level include con-
sumers’ touchpoints, the type of message content conveyed
in the ads, and usage behaviors. There are six types of touch-
points in the data—consumers’ exposures to and clicks of the
ﬁrm’s display ads and emails, and their clicks on the ﬁrm’s
organic and paid search ads. The message content is catego-
rized into awareness, persuasive, branded (excluded due to
high correlation with paid search clicks), and featured prod-
ucts. For consumers’ free-trial usage, measures are taken on
two dimensions—download and launch of the ﬁrm’s software
applications, with the former reﬂecting usage variety and the
latter reﬂecting frequency.
The model is estimated using Gibbs Sampler by iter-
ating through random draws of each parameter from its
posterior distribution conditional on other parameters. The
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation procedures
are in Supporting Information Appendix B. Multiple chains
of 100,000 iterations are estimated, and the ﬁrst 80,000 draws
are discarded as burn-in. For the remaining 20,000 draws,
every 10th draw is kept for the analysis of posteriors. All
chains are converged according to the Geweke diagnostics
and Gelman–Rubin statistics.
5.1 Regularization
The proposed model contains 11 stock variables for touch-
points, message content, usage, and the pairwise interactions
between them, yielding a set of 66 independent variables.
Apart from the stock variables, there are six control vari-
ables and 11 copula correction terms. Regularization is thus
needed to prevent overﬁtting and multicollinearity. Speciﬁ-
cally, this study employs the variance inﬂation factor (VIF)
score, both L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization, and
principal components to achieve model parsimony and avoid
multicollinearity.
First, an independent variable is dropped if both the VIF
score (VIF >50) and Lasso regression suggest that it should
be removed. The Lasso regression, closely related to the
Bayesian variable selection (George & McCulloch, 1993 ;
Yuan & Lin, 2005 ), discards some of the correlated inde-
pendent variables. Nine out of the 66 independent variables
are dropped in this step, all of which are marked as dots in
Table 6.Second, ridge estimates are used for the remaining inde-
pendent variables, which shrinks the estimated values to pre-
vent multicollinearity. The ridge estimates in the frequentist
paradigm are equivalent to the Bayesian estimates with Nor-
mal priors used in this study (detailed in Supporting Informa-
tion Appendix B). The ridge parameter that yields the low-
est deviance is 0.447 for the proposed model. Both the Lasso
and Ridge regressions can be combined into the elastic net
penalty.
Third, for the 11 copula correction terms in the purchase
utility equation, principal components are extracted to further
reduce dimensionality. Based on the percentage of impor-
tance for each principal component in explaining the total
variance (detailed in Supporting Information Appendix C),
two principal components are included in Equation ( 7), which
account for 65% of the total variance from all copula correc-
tion terms.
In summary, after regularization, the proposed model in
Equation ( 7) contains the individualized intercept, 57 inde-
pendent variables (comprised of both stock variables and their
interactions), six control variables, and two principal compo-
nents of the copula correction terms.
5.2 Model selection
In the proposed model, the stock variables of advertising and
usage and their pairwise interactions all have associated car-
ryover parameters. For each possible value of the carryover
parameters, all stock variables need to be recomputed for the
Gibbs sampler. The large dataset of 12,297 consumers and
718,149 total observations makes the estimation of carryover
parameters very time consuming. To reduce the compu-
tational burden, the estimation of carryover parameters is
simpliﬁed by assuming a common carryover parameter for
the cumulative advertising effects (both marketing touch-
points and message content), a common carryover parameter
for the cumulative usage, and another common carryover
parameter for any interactions between the stock variables.
Similar simpliﬁcation has been used in both Bayesian (e.g.,
Chae et al., 2019 , use 0.1 increments in the range of 0.1–0.9)
and frequentist paradigms (e.g., Guadagni & Little, 1983 ,
begin with 0.75 and reﬁne the parameter). Speciﬁcally, each
carryover parameter varies from 0 to 1 with increments of
0.1, producing 113=1331 combinations. Then, grid search
is used to ﬁnd the parameters that lead to the best model ﬁt.
The 1331 speciﬁcations with varied sets of carryover param-
eters are evaluated by the log-marginal density (LMD) and
deviance information criterion (DIC). The top 20 best-ﬁtting
model speciﬁcations are reported in Supporting Information
Appendix D. The speciﬁcation with the lowest LMD and
the lowest DIC has 70% marketing carryover, 90% usage
carryover, and 10% interaction carryover.

Table 5reports the parameter estimates of the intercept, con-

The unique data on free-trial usage also render the opportu-
nity to explore the relationship between usage and marketing
touchpoints (Table 6, the yellow block, bottom two rows of
columns 1–6), between usage and message content (Table 6,
the gray block, bottom two rows of columns 7–9), and
between different usage types (Table 6, the pink triangle, bot-

usage and touchpoints are negative when signiﬁcant, indicat-
ing ads are less effective on busier free-trial users in leading
to conversion. However, the interactions between some con-
tent and usage, such as the pair of persuasive messages and
usage frequency and that of featured message and usage vari-
ety, can synergize in converting prospects. Moreover, con-
sumers’ cumulative usage frequency has a positive impact on
conversion. That is, consumers with higher usage frequency
are more likely to purchase. Such a positive correlation has
been found in the context of repeat purchases (Ascarza &
Hardie, 2013 ; Lemon et al., 2002 ) but has not been reportedfor new conversions. In terms of usage variety, both its cumu-
lative effect and interaction with usage frequency are negative
on conversion. That is, when a consumer increases the vari-
ety of her software exploration, she is less likely to convert.
Such a negative impact has been found in consumer behav-
ioral research, where more varied usage experiences cause
consumers to overestimate their usage (Etkin & Sela, 2016 )
and feel less productive (Etkin & Mogilner, 2016 ). Conse-
quently, when consumers are overwhelmed by too many fea-
tures, their satisfaction and repeat purchases may decrease
(Etkin & Sela, 2016 ; Thompson et al., 2005 ).
In summary, the cumulative impact of all marketing touch-
points and message content is positive. The cumulative
impact of usage frequency is positive, whereas that of usage
variety is negative. Many pairs of interactions between stock
variables are positive, including those between consumer-
initiated touchpoints and content and those between usage
and content. However, negative interactions are also found
between ﬁrm-initiated touchpoints and persuasive messages
and between persuasive and awareness messages. Addition-
ally, the interaction between touchpoints and usage is neg-
ative when signiﬁcant, and the interaction between usage
frequency and variety is also negative.

5.4 Robustness tests
The robustness of the ﬁndings is tested with the control func-
tion method, varied data windows, and the PSM method.
These tests generate consistent results like those from the
main model reported in Tables 5and6, validating the robust-
ness of the empirical ﬁndings. Detailed output is reported and
discussed in Supporting Information Appendices E–G.
First, the control function method is used instead of the
copula method in tackling endogeneity. Given the large
masses on zero for all types of touchpoints in Figure 3, zero-
inﬂated Poisson is adopted for the control function equations.
The marketing touchpoints, message content, and usage in
the past one day are used as the IVs. Detailed results are in
Supporting Information Appendix E.
Next, the key ﬁndings are examined again with a shorter
data window to test the sensitivity to data censoring. Left-
censoring of the data is not a concern as the sample is a
random draw from all consumers who created accounts and
were immediately provided free trials in September 2015.
However, right censoring could exist for free-trial users who
did not convert by the end of the data window. In addition to
right censoring, the observation window for buyers may be
shorter than nonbuyers as all observations after conversion
are dropped for buyers, while nonbuyers remain in the sample
until the end of the observation window. Consequently, with
a longer data window, the nonbuyers could potentially be
exposed to more marketing communications. The calibration
window is thus shortened from 60 days to 50, 45, 40, and 30
days to test the impact of data censoring. The right censoring
at the end of the data window does not change the key take-
aways of the results. However, new signiﬁcant interactions
related to email exposure emerge in shortened data windows.
Details are given in Supporting Information Appendix F.
Furthermore, ﬁrms can selectively deliver display impres-
sions or send emails to prospective customers with the highest
conversion likelihood. Such selection bias may affect con-
sumers’ reactions to marketing communications, their soft-
ware usage behaviors, and consequently their purchase deci-
sions. To alleviate such selection bias, the PSM method
(Datta et al., 2015 ) is used to estimate Equation ( 1). Two PSM
analyses are conducted regarding the ﬁrm-initiated contact—
one using samples matched by consumers’ display impres-
sions and the other matched by their email exposure. In each
case, a consumer with the ad exposure is matched with a con-
sumer without using the nearest neighbor algorithm without
replacement. The results from both, in Supporting Informa-
tion Appendix G, are consistent with those in the main model.
5.5 Simulation of Ad exposures
To illustrate how this study helps ﬁrms address the crucial
questions of when and what type of nudges they should send
to consumers to facilitate conversions, two simulations are
implemented to investigate different allocations of market-
ing effort. The simulations only focus on the two types ofadvertising exposures that the ﬁrm has the most control
over—display impression and email exposure. Moreover, the
simulations are also limited to the subsets of consumers who
have been exposed to display impressions or email exposures
during the data window. Furthermore, the cost of display and
emails ads (both monetary and their annoyance), which are
usually considered in practice, is not accounted for in the sim-
ulation due to the lack of data.
In the ﬁrst simulation, the ﬁrm is assumed to cast one
more display impression on a speciﬁc day from day 0 to
day 60 to all consumers who have been exposed to dis-
play impressions in the data. Figure 4shows the changes
in the estimated expected conversion rate, with the associ-
ated 95% highest density region shaded in gray. There are
several windows to increase conversion rate by offering one
more display impression, such as on the day of sign-up,
halfway of the free trial, around the expiration, and in the
second and third weeks after expiration, for which the ben-
eﬁt of showing one more display impression is signiﬁcantly
greater than zero. Meanwhile, there are days for which the
95% highest density region covers 0, indicating the con-
version lift from one additional display impression is not
signiﬁcantly different from 0. Although the main effect of dis-
play impression and its interactions with email exposure and
paid search are positive, its interactions with persuasive mes-
sages and usage variety are negative. The overall impact from
one additional display impression on conversion depends on
the status-quo touchpoints, content, and usage in the data.
Likewise, another simulation is conducted among con-
sumers who have received emails during the data window and
investigates the increase in the estimated expected conversion
rates if the ﬁrm sends out one more email on a speciﬁc day.
Figure 5illustrates the changes in the estimated expected con-
version rate, with the associated 95% highest density region
shaded in gray. The lift of one additional email exposure on
the day of sign-up, at the end of the free trial, and in the third
and fourth week after expiration is also statistically different
from zero. There are more days in Figure 5where the gray
regions overlap with 0, implying additional emails sent on
these days do not help conversion signiﬁcantly.
6 MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS AND
CONCLUSION
Firms widely use free trials to entice prospects by offering
them an opportunity to discover the ﬁrms’ products and ser-
vices and assess the utility of a purchase. However, the role
of consumers’ free-trial usage behavior, the marketing com-
munication in the presence of a free trial, and the interplay
between both is still an understudied topic. Especially when
focused on the SaaS business model, where the free trial has
already become the norm, many practitioners are still seeking
solutions for the low conversion rate from free-trial users to
paid subscribers.
In this paper, a unique dataset from a leading SaaS
ﬁrm is studied to explore how the ﬁrm’s advertising and

of marketing touchpoints, message content, usage behaviors,
and, importantly, the pairwise interactions among them. Reg-
ularization is imposed to avoid overﬁtting and multicollinear-
ity. Meanwhile, the potential endogeneity is addressed with
the copula method, and several robustness checks with the
control function method, varied data window, and the PSM
procedures are conducted. Finally, two simulations are imple-
mented to show ﬁrms how to leverage the dynamics in
consumers’ advertising responses and free-trial usage to
determine the timing of casting additional display impres-
sions or sending out additional emails.
The empirical ﬁndings in this study contribute to a more
precise understanding of the impact of advertising, free-trial
usage, and the interplay between them. The granular market-ing data on both marketing touchpoints and message con-
tent render a unique opportunity to examine the role of dif-
ferent types of touchpoints and message content. Consistent
with the extant literature, all marketing touchpoints and mes-
sage content have a positive cumulative impact on conver-
sions, and synergies are also found between different types
of touchpoints and message content. All types of message
content have a positive interaction with the consumer-
initiated touchpoints and usage. However, negative interac-
tions are noted between the ﬁrm-initiated touchpoints and
persuasive messages and between persuasive and awareness
messages. The negative interactions between persuasive mes-
sages and ﬁrm-initiated touchpoints and awareness messages
can result from consumers’ skepticism of ﬁrm-generated con-
tent (Mayzlin, 2006 ) and/or their reaction to unsolicited rec-
ommendations (Fitzsimons & Lehmann, 2004 ). This suggests
the focal ﬁrm ought to revisit its persuasive messages and

determine whether a lower frequency and/or milder tones
could alleviate their negative impact on conversions. Mean-
while, it is also worth exploring whether shifting persuasive
content to customer-initiated touchpoints, that is, inbound
visits via search channels or on the ﬁrm’s own website, could
mitigate the negativity due to persuasion.
The unique data of the frequency and variety of free-trial
usage allows for exploring the interaction between usage fre-
quency and usage variety, as well as the interactions between
advertising and usage. In this research, the cumulative usage
frequency positively impacts conversion, implying that the
software launch by a consumer is a good indicator of the
consumer’s interest in the ﬁrm’s service. However, the cumu-
lative impact of usage variety and the interactions between
usage variety and frequency are both negative on conver-
sion. Additionally, the interaction between touchpoints and
usage is negative when signiﬁcant, indicating the marketing
communication is less effective on busier free-trial users.
To leverage these empirical ﬁndings, the ﬁrm could develop
trackers of consumers’ usage frequency and variety to better
forecast their conversion propensity. Moreover, given the
negative impact of usage variety on conversion, the ﬁrm
could experiment with limited free trials wherein prospects
are provided access to fewer software applications to con-
clude whether they have offered too much of a good thing.
In summary, this research provides evidence and guidance
for managers to better interpret the consumer’s ad responses
and usage behaviors during the free-trial period. It is espe-
cially helpful for managers at SaaS ﬁrms, where free trials
are widely adopted. The negative cumulative impact of usage
variety and the negative interactions between persuasive mes-
sages and the ﬁrm-initiated touchpoints and between persua-
sive and awareness messages offer insights for the focal ﬁrm
on the directions of future improvements, in terms of what
messages to promote in which marketing media and what
type of messages to avoid sending together in their subse-
quent marketing campaigns. Also, the simulations of ﬁrm-
initiated exposures of display ads and emails show ﬁrms
how to utilize the proposed model to better determine the
timing of contact prospects. By examining various dimen-
sions of the ﬁrm’s advertising and consumers’ free-trial
usage, and analyzing the interactions between both, managers
can better allocate their marketing effort throughout the free-
trial period and afterward.
This research is focused on prospective consumers in the
SaaS context. It is noted for some long-standing software,
say Microsoft Ofﬁce, some prospective SaaS users may be
past owners of perpetual licenses who ﬁnd it easier to learn
the SaaS software based on their familiarity with its basic
features.5However, despite some similarities, given the rapid
advancements in the software industry, the user experience
with SaaS can be considerably different from what was
offered with perpetual licenses in the past. Moreover, in
the present study, the focal ﬁrm completely discontinued
its perpetual licenses several years before the data window.
Consequently, the previous owners of perpetual licenses didnot have any SaaS experience before buying the SaaS access
and are treated by the ﬁrm and this study as new prospects.
This research can be extended in several ways. First, the
empirical ﬁndings from observational data can be validated
and enriched by lab and ﬁeld experiments, especially to tease
out the individual impact of one type of marketing touch-
point or message content. For example, directly manipulat-
ing the timing and quantity of display impressions or emails
delivered to consumers would render more accurate measures
of the ad effectiveness in the presence of a free trial. Also,
limiting the frequency of software launches or the number
of downloads allowed during the free trial would directly
validate whether consumer’s usage frequency and variety
can be used as indicators of conversion likelihood. Second,
the dataset lacks the rich demographic information com-
monly used for segmentation analysis. Incorporating addi-
tional demographic information would help identify differ-
ent segments of prospective consumers and enable a detailed
analysis of different situations. Third, the touchpoint data
before the account creation is not used in this study due to
sparsity and low tracking accuracy. If such data were accu-
rately recorded and integrated with other touchpoints after
account creation, this study could be extended to a staged
model that also captures consumers’ journey before the free-
trial acquisition.
