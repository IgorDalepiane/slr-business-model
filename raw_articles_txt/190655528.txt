Abstract — In this paper, we show our study on a market 
model of mobile SaaS (Software as a Service) servers in a semi-
cooperative environment. We propose a decentralized SaaS 
(Software as a Service) business model and compare it with a 
common centralized model in cloud computing. First, we 
define the problem and derive some necessary conditions for 
the adoption of decentralized model. Then, using periodic user 
demand, we simulate and analyze the revenue of decentralized 
servers assuming servers will maximize their revenue. Five 
different service selection strategies are simulated and their 
results are analyzed.  
Keywords- SaaS, decentralized model, selection strategy 
I.  INTRODUCTION  
This work is an extension of previous work [1] by 
considering a different demand pattern. In addition, we 
further demonstrate the benefit of distributed software as a 
service (SaaS) business models. In current SaaS business 
models, customers connect to the service provider’s 
server(s), use its application(s) and pay it. For a medium- or 
large-size service provider, it may manage its own data 
center (applications and database) in order to control its 
service, maximize its revenue (due to economies of scale), 
and being less dependent from other intermediary 
companies. However, for a small provider, it may be more 
beneficial for it to host its application(s) on top of other 
PaaS/IaaS provider(s). Regardless where a service provider 
put its application(s), as a business entity, it directly interacts 
with its users, as depicted in Figure 1.   
 
 
 
Figure 1.  Interaction between a provider (shaded area) and its users in a 
common centralized business model 
As shown in Figure 1, in a common centralized SaaS 
business model, the headquarters (HQ) and local servers 
belong to the same business entity (shaded area), that is, the 
provider. Local servers, either owned by HQ or outsourced 
to other companies, are local data centers physically close to 
users. However, since users only interact with HQ without 
knowing whether they are accessing data center in HQ or 
local server(s), they are unaware about the business 
relationship between HQ and local servers. Hence, when 
some service errors/troubles occur, users have to contact the 
HQ for the solution. 
Two drawbacks of the centralized business model are 
obvious. First, when a growing mid-size provider broadening 
its business to global market, it may suffer from many 
investment and management issues, which are similar to 
those faced by non-IT companies that resulted in their 
adoption of a franchise business model. Second, when a 
provider suffers from a server emergency, such as the 
incident happened in Amazon AWS [2], or a security attack, 
such as DDoS, the whole chain of its market will face a 
serious problem, which is commonly known as the single-
point-of-failure problem. To avoid either one, we propose 
and analyze a distributed business model, which could be an 
alternative here.

Our work, including previous work [1], is closely related 
to others in Grid computing and multi-agent systems (MAS).

Although Grid computing and cloud computing are not the

A. Pricing Mechanisms on the Grid 
In [3], Wolski et al. considered storage capacity and CPU 
power as resources, where consumers may request a 
specified amount of them from provider in a market. Their 
results indicate that using commodities markets outperforms 
auction mechanisms for controlling Grid resources.  
Beyond a simple commodity market model, Steur et al. 
[4] allowed trading and pricing of substitutable goods. Two 
substitutable goods with different prices are considered in 
their market: low-end and high-end compute nodes (e.g., 
‘fast’ or ‘slow’ CPU). In addition, they considered 
consumers, providers, and the market itself as the basic 
market elements, where consumers/providers follow 
different buying/selling strategy. They assumed that network 
and storage are interchangeable; however, quality of service, 
available memory, and network bandwidth are ignored in 
their model. 
Both Kang et al. [5] and Buccafurri et al. [6] tried to 
improve the QoS requirement of the market. In [5], the 
authors proposed an algorithm, Highly Available Job 
Execution Service (HA-JES), to replicate jobs and assign 
them to under-utilized and under-priced resources. In [6], the 
authors provided several QoS frameworks with multiple 
layers: response time, price, availability, reputation, and data 
quality timelines, accuracy, and completeness.  
B. Load balancing in Multi-Agent Systems  
Chow and Kwok [9] discussed the load-balancing design 
choices in the area of multi-agent computing. They 
developed a communication-based load balancing algorithm 
which works on the basis of the credit value of each agent. 
They assigned a credit value to agents on the basis of its 
affinity to a machine, its current workload, its 
communication behavior, and mobility. Whenever there is 
load imbalance in the system, the credit value of all the 
agents are examined and an agent with a lower credit value is 
shifted to the lightly loaded machine. 
Bigham and Du [10] describe the cooperative negotiation 
approach of multi-agent systems for real-time load balancing 
in mobile cellular networks. They used negotiations based on 
certain number of possible local hypotheses instead of using 
a formal negotiation model of alternating offers.  Their 
system works on the basis of an agreement made between the 
agents based upon the negotiation. The negotiation in their 
system is part of the planning layer and agent system 
resource management in 3G wireless networks. 
III. PROPOSED DECENTRALIZED MODEL  
A decentralized business model of SaaS is illustrated in 
Figure 2.  
 
Figure 2.   Interaction in a decentralized business model, where local 
servers are belong to independent business entity 
Similar to that in Figure 1, the shading areas in Figure 2 
represent independent business entities. In this case, local 
servers are competing with each other to maximize their own 
profit; meanwhile, they may provide similar 
services/applications to their customers. Hereafter, the term 
“server” refers to a business entity (e.g., a URL) that 
provides services in the context of decentralized SaaS model. 
Since servers are not necessarily identical, they may have 
different capacities, located in different geographical area, 
have different operational costs, serve different customers, 
and may provide different services obtained from HQ.  
We assume the market as a brand-branch reseller and 
local servers try to maximize their own profit. A benefit for 
HQ is it can save the connection/traffic cost, use less data 
center, and concentrate on its product development. Indeed, 
local servers will get some supports from their HQ and pay 
license fee according to the number of users or usages. HQ 
may have some brand products and responsible for their 
global advertisement, R&D, and training. For example, every 
local server may be required to provide certain applications 
(e.g. the brand products), but may opt to serve other 
applications depending on their market demands. Hence 
under these settings, local servers may adjust their services 
according to their local demands in order to maximize profits 
[1]. Note here, we assume that changing service 
(installing/uninstalli ng software) does not consume much 
time/cost. 
Since HQ also wants to maximize its profit, that is, the 
total amount of license fee paid by local servers after 
deducted by its cost, it should consider a flexible pricing of 
its products and the license fee it will charge. Some factors 
that it may consider include: the operating cost of local 
servers may vary depending on their location, the service 
speed (bandwidth) of local servers may vary, and the demand 
size from customers to the servers may vary too. Hence, 
charging a flat rate to customers and local servers may not be 
an optimal strategy. In the real free market, the pricing 
mechanism is dynamic and more complicated, that is, prices 
might go up with an increase in the market demand and go 
down when the demand from users dropped (this situation is 
similar to the phenomena in the commodity market). But in 
our model, we only consider a fixed price, which may be true 
within a specific time period in an oligopolistic model.

IV. SIMULATION ANALYSIS  
Similar to that in [1], we proposed four different 
strategies for a local server to increase the QoS. Here, the 
total cost (queue length) and satisfied level (number of 
satisfied requests) are two dominant factors that affect QoS. 
For example, if N clients request the same service, which is 
only provided by a server, then the queue length will be N. 
But if two servers could provide the same service, the queue 
length reduced to N/2, which means a better QoS. In our 
experiment, we assume local servers’ consideration is not 
only QoS, but also revenue and profit. In principle, the 
underlying results will be the same, because eventually each 
server will try to provide services with the highest demand. 
In the next subsection we will discuss the simulation on the 
SaaS market in which servers may switch their services. 
A. Basic Setup 
We assume there are five local servers {S1, S2, S3, S4, 
S5} which can provide ten different customized applications 
denoted by {Item1, Item2, …, Item10}. Since the servers are 
heterogeneous with respect to their computational capacity, 
they have different capacities to provide 3, 4, 2, 6, and 1 
item(s) respectively. We assume there are one hundred 
clients {C1, C2, …, C100} requesting the applications. The 
requests in this experiment is to simulate cyclical data, says, 
on weekly data (more requests on workdays and less on 
weekend), with some random noise to resemble real data. 
Figure 3 shows the requests of 100 cycles (weeks) used in 
the simulation. This request pattern is different from a 
random request reported in [1]. Finally, we use five different 
sets of initial server conditions to observe the diverse results.  
 
 
Figure 3.   Requests (100 cycles) from 100 clients 
We apply the following method to all clients and 
applications (items): if client C1 requests item1 then it will 
search from S1. If S1 can serve Item1 then C1 will get it 
from S1. Then, the next request for Item1, say by C4, will be 
matched to S2 instead of S1, and if S2 can serve Item1, then 
it will take the item from S2. However, if S2 does not serve 
Item1, then the client will go through the others server (from 
S3 to S5) to find Item1. If none of them serve Item1 then it 
will go back to S1. In this situation, S1 will have a queue 
with the length of 2 (serving both C1 and C4).  
The simulation runs for 100 requesting cycles (a 
hypothetical time period).The clients requests may come at 
any time and not deterministic. In the reality, a cycle could be a day, or some other period of time, here, according to our 
generated data, the requesting cycle denotes a week. In every 
cycle, servers cannot change their applications and we also 
arrange the requests such that each application has been 
requested at least once by the clients. Fig. 3 shows the 
hypothetical requests used in our experiment. 
The numbers of clients, servers and their capacity, and 
even cycles in the simulation were set a little limited since 
the simulation requires quite much processing time. One will 
extend these numbers in the future when a better computing 
power is available.  
Our assumption considers as well that the cost of 
selecting each application is proportional to the queue length. 
For example, if two clients are using the same application at 
the same local server then the cost for each is higher than 
when there is only one client using the application. Hence, 
local servers cannot only care about the short term revenue 
but also should keep the regular clients and servers switch 
their applications correctly for them to anticipate client 
requests in an ideal free market mechanism, especially to 
minimize the public or social cost. It is not only for solving 
load balancing issue, but also for reducing response time and 
increasing the availability, which have been considered as 
major issues in distributed computing. 
After every cycle, the ratio of the total requests to the 
total servers providing that application is calculated. This 
ratio is nothing but the service load, which may be used by a 
server to decide its future services. For that purpose, we 
denote t-1 for the ratio of the past cycle, t-2 for that of the 
previous 2 cycles, and t-3 for the previous 3 cycles. Then, 
after every 3 cycles the demand factor, d, is calculated using 
eq. (1): 
d = 0.3 t -1 + 0.2 t -2 + 0.1 t -3  (1) 
Eq. (1) represents the service load history in the market. 
Here, the application having the highest value of d is the one 
that clients may request the most in the future. Certainly, one 
may argue to use a different equation in the forecast of future 
demand. We will leave the variation of it for future work. 
The following algorithm shows how to select a server for a 
given request. 
As mentioned previously, servers switch their 
applications on the basis of the demand factor d. In this paper 
five different strategies that may be adopted by servers have 
been studied, they are: Fixed, All move, Rich move, Poor 
move, and Poor request-rate move. The characteristics of the 
first four can be found in [1]. Below we only discuss the 
characteristic of Poor request-rate move . In this move, those 
servers whose request rate is less than the average request 
rate are defined as poor. Here, request rate is calculated after 
every fixed interval, or in this simulation is calculated after 
every 3 cycles as follow, 
For Each Server, 
Request Rate = Total Requests Received / Server Capability

Here, server capability is the number of applications a 
server can serve. The server is not poor if it has a high 
request rate even if it can only serve very few applications or 
has very low capability. But a server is considered poor if it 
receives a low request rate only. So the servers with low 
request rate are motivated to gain high request by moving 
their applications to serve the applications with high demand 
factor. 
B. Experimental Setup and Metrics 
In the simulation, all applications will be completely 
served. We use 10 different initial item conditions (called 
Trial 1 to Trial 10) for statistical purposes. The diversity of 
these initial item conditions is purportedly to include the 
following features: 
• Some applications will not be served in the initial 
state. In this case, all servers pick their own initial 
items. 
• All applications will be uniformly served at the 
beginning. In this case, the HQ arranges the initial 
items so that all items can be provided uniformly. 
• An application will be served by all the servers 
initially (trial 9 and 10). In this case, the HQ 
requests the local servers to provide a brand product 
at the initial state. 
Then, we will check performance of all the strategies 
mentioned above and compare their advantage or 
disadvantage using three metrics: total costs, total revenue, 
and satisfaction rate. The total costs depend to the length of 
queue l, that is, Cost = 0.5 l (l + 1). The total revenue is 
proportional to the number of satisfied requests or queue 
length. We assume here that all item prices are equal (= 1). 
The satisfaction rate refers to the ratio of the number of 
requests that can be served. This is the concern of HQ, 
because a higher satisfaction rate means a larger usage or 
total revenue. Note, all of these metrics related to the local 
server’s profit, but it is imperative to report them 
individually since they have some relevancies to the server’s 
and HQ’s goal.  
C. Results 
Table 1 demonstrates the experimental results using the 
four different strategies from all 100 clients in 100 cycles. 
The “Sum” columns report the sum of 5 servers’ total costs 
or revenue. The “Avg” columns report the average value per 
server (after dividing it with the capacity). We use bold fonts 
for the highest number within a group (column) and italic 
fonts for the lowest number. 
In most cases, the average revenue proportionally aligns 
with the average costs, that is, higher average revenue for 
server means higher average cost for users (longer queue). 
However, some anomalies appear, for example the lowest 
average revenue in Rich Move  group is for server S5, while 
the lowest average cost in the same group is for server S2. 
Next, we will provide some analysis on each strategy. 
Fixed strategy  is found to be the best method to earn the 
highest total revenue for all five servers, with the highest 
satisfaction rate too. Certainly, it also causes the highest 
average costs for customers. However, the top amount of profit does not mean that every server can get more revenue 
than the case of using others strategies. For example, S5 
earned 15154 with Fixed strategy , which is greater than the 
11042 profit of using Poor-request-rate-move strategy , but 
S4 shows the contrast (26331 which is a little less than 
27050). As we previously mentioned, we assume that every 
server is selfish so servers should not keep holding their 
applications all the time to indulge competitors; hence, we 
only consider Fixed strategy  as a benchmark. 
The Poor-move strategy  and Poor-request-rate-move 
strategy are similar in some aspects. All servers (S1-S5) 
show no big differences with respect to their revenue. But 
note that Poor-move strategy  gave more benefits to the 
servers with less capability and Poor-request-rate-move  
strategy  can help the ambitious and competent servers more. 
Obviously, Rich-move strategy  expressed the worst 
performance with respect to the revenue and satisfaction rate 
among all strategies. The reason is, by adopting this strategy 
all capable/rich servers may move synchronously to serve 
highly demanded applications; hence, leaving a large fraction 
of less-demanded applications unsatisfied. However, due to 
the competition among rich servers, it will bring some 
benefits for poor servers (S3 and S5), which show a larger 
average revenue than in other strategies. 
The experiment here shows the superiority of Fixed 
strategy  as well. This is not surprising, because under 
periodic requests, a slow reaction (switch items) may 
become a lag that penalizes the server; hence, Fixed strategy  
may be better here. To improve the server revenue (and also 
the satisfaction rate), a fast learning method or a method that 
can recognize the periodic pattern should be adopted, which 
are left for the future work. 
To further analyze the impact of initialization (HQ 
strategy), Table 2 shows the satisfaction rate for various 
initial values. Under some initial items, the servers gain 
100% satisfaction rate without altering their items (case 5 
and 8). Hence, altering items may not increase but reduce 
this rate. However, in some other cases where the initial 
items will not attain a high rate, altering items may help to 
improve the satisfaction rate (e.g. in case 1 and 10). 
D. Limitations of Current Simulation 
Although we try to make the simulation like the reality 
experiment, it also has some limitations. The first weakness 
is that the experiment scale (number of servers and clients) 
were limited, we took hundred clients and only five servers 
for reducing the burden of simulation, but it is common for 
millions of clients generate billions requests for several 
server clusters in the real application, so it is meaningful to 
extend the scope of the simulation in the future work. 
Secondly, in the real world, the item prices of all the 
applications may be different and servers may have different 
priorities for different applications from their business 
viewpoint. However, we assumed they are the same here. 
Hence, we need to incorporate such strategies to strengthen 
the simulation in the future. Finally, we also can add some 
assumptions such as the number of clients may decrease 
(quit) when they are waiting in the queue which is common 
in the reality. One may improve the simulation in the future

V. CONCLUSIONS  
Despite the simulation was carried out with many 
assumptions, the experimental results demonstrated the 
various effects caused by different service selection 
strategies in a semi-cooperative SaaS market, and it has 
addressed some important research issues in the context of 
SaaS market. These assumptions are based on the theoretical 
analysis in [1], in which we assume that the HQ adopts 
decentralize business model and imposes zero fixed service; 
hence, allowing all local servers to select the service.  
In our preliminary simulation [1], some adjustment 
strategies are better than just providing the same applications 
all the time ( Fixed strategy ); and we showed that providing 
the most demanded applications by all the servers is not a 
good strategy. But in our later simulation Fixed strategy  is 
better compared to others strategies. The differences are 
mainly caused by the adoption of periodic requests in our 
simulation.

problems and factors that we should consider before we can 
derive a strong conclusion or find a best strategy in the semi-
