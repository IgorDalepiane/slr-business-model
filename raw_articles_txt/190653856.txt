ABSTRACT
This paper explores the rapid growth in the public valuations of
SaaS companies and examines whether this growth can be explained
by investment theory. SaaS companies have experienced a remark-
able rise in stock market valuations in recent years. Through the
construction of a SaaS Index, constituted of 20 publicly traded
SaaS companies, the empirical study tests the efficacy of two tra-
ditional models of investment theory, The Capital Asset Pricing
Model and Fama-French’s 3 Factor Model. The Index is used as
the basis for these models and additionally is supported by Linear
Regression models. Ultimately, the assumptions which underlie
these models are shown to be unsuitable for the SaaS Industry
due its relative youth and unique characteristics. It then seeks to
expand upon existing theory and practice by building a positive
argument and highlighting additional possible factors including
Growth, Profitability and Enterprise Value/Revenue which exhibit
a greater explanatory role in share price movement. This enables
the construction of a new model, of a company’s revenue growth
and Enterprise Value relative to revenue supported by a qualitative
narrative which better accounts for the relative growth in the SaaS
Index over the observed period. Ultimately, while this new model
holds greater explanatory power, the underlying characteristics of
SaaS companies such as the subscription model, capital efficiency
and ability to scale are important and are more likely to be effective
drivers of value into the future.

This paper seeks to understand the rise of a new market phenome-
non, Software as a Service companies, which have surged in num-
ber and value in recent years. It seeks to explain the drivers of this
growth and critically engage with why market participants seem
to favour this form of company.
To achieve this, this paper combines both empirical public market
research with theoretical ideas and literature to critically examine
this performance. It contextualizes these SaaS companies through
traditional models and ideas of investment theory, including the
Capital Asset Pricing Model and Fama and French’s 3 factor model.
It then attempts to create a positive argument, by examining and
developing ideas around the specific nature of SaaS companies and
what differentiates them from traditional business models.
The latter stages of this empirical study are focused on building
the positive argument. These sections aim to provide criteria and
defining characteristics which provide the levers upon which SaaS
public valuation growth is predicated. It relies on a combination
of academic literature and industry specific research to guide the
discussion and provide relevant support.
These include characteristics related to all SaaS firms and one’s
which differentiate between SaaS firms. These are ideas such as
predictability of revenue, availability of metrics and high margins
for the former and pricing models, enterprise vs best of breed and
growth rates for the latter. This should enable the paper to close
in on the truth on drivers of pricing both on an industry and firm
level.
Ultimately, it concludes that SaaS companies seem to violate
many of the principles of traditional investment theory and the as-
sociated models. It aims to provide reasoning for this deviation and
identify the characteristics within this that hold greater explanatory
power.
2 PURPOSE
The purpose of this research paper is to examine the way in which
SaaS companies are valued. It seeks to understand the drivers of
value and how they are, or can be, reflected within valuation frame-
works.
The core hypothesis is:
SaaS business models are sufficiently different to require bespoke
models for valuation.
The argument that underpins this is as follows:
•SaaS companies have distinctly different business models to
traditional companies
•Existing asset pricing models are designed for companies
with traditional business models
•Therefore, existing pricing models do not accurately forecast
the price of SaaS companies

3 TRADITIONAL MODELS
The empirical study seeks to examine the SaaS Index in the context
of traditional valuation models used for examining the prices of
publicly traded companies.
The aim of this is to understand if SaaS companies adhere to,
or violate the supposed structures and the assumptions that they
make about valuation theory, risk appetite and expected payoff. To
do this, this paper looks at two main models:
•The Capital Asset Pricing Model
•The Fama-French Model
Capital Asset Pricing Model: It is productive to begin by provid-
ing an overview of the Capital Asset Pricing Model. The Capital
Asset Pricing Model seeks to explain the relationship between risk
and return through creating a framework for an investor appetite.
A fundamental idea of investment theory is that investors expect
the level of risk to reflect the level of return they can expect. CAPM
demonstrates the market risk premium which investors can expect
based on the level of risk being endured.
Taking the risk-free rate, the level of return one can expect when
enduring no risk and adding the market risk premium (less risk
free rate) multiplied by the individual beta of the investment, the
CAPM enables investors to calculate the fair value of the asset.
In theory, CAPM should enable investors to make decisions
about whether the risk profile makes it a worthwhile inclusion
in a portfolio. CAPM takes into account non diversifiable risk, orsystemic risk. The Sharpe ratio, taken as the measure of return
corresponding to the level of risk, demonstrates this gap. The ratio
outlined by William Sharpe, creates a simple measure of this un-
derlying assumption of investment theory. Combined, the CAPM
and Sharpe ratio represent a framework for market observers to
test the risk adjusted return of assets enabling decision making.
In order to test the principles of CAPM on the SaaS Index, it
is necessary to make some further assumptions to provide the
variables for the formula.
As the SaaS Index is based in the US, it is appropriate to take a
risk-free rate from the US economy. This study takes the 10 year
treasury note as the risk free rate. This is taken as the average
weekly rate over the outlined 3 year period. This data has been
obtained from Yahoo Finance. Along with the return of each of the
assets in the SaaS Index, the CAPM requires that the beta of each
company is also found.

4 THE RULE OF 40
One framework that is frequently employed to chart this value
proposition is the Rule of 40. It states that the percentage of prof-
itability and the growth rate of the company should add up to 40.
The main premise of this analysis is that it accounts for what are
perceived as the two main drivers of value at different points of
a company’s natural life cycle, growth and profitability. When a
company is in its infancy it may be expected to experience weaker
profitability in lieu of achieving more aggressive growth targets.
The relevance of the Rule of 40 and its purported elegance in valu-
ing or assessing SaaS companies is that it is reflective of the various
stages a SaaS company is likely to go through. [5]
SaaS companies can experience particularly high growth in their
early stages of development relative to other companies. Once this
tapers off, the onus is to deliver strong profitability, with gross
margins often greatly exceeding public market averages.
This makes the distinction between so-called value or growth
stocks as highlighted by Fama-French as being relevant to the ex-
pected performance of stocks. However, it conversely builds the
argument that this only holds at a certain level of maturity in the
Index. As evidenced previously, there is less of a pronounced reality

A Superior Business Model?: At the outset, this paper highlighted
how subscription business models differ from their traditional coun-
terparts. Subscriptions provide vendors with reliable, steady streams
of income over predefined periods.
Tien Tzou describes this phenomenon in his seminal book on
the Subscription Economy, Subscribed, detailing how historic mod-
els rely on developing channels to reach end consumers, whereas
subscription models centre on providing experiences and constant
availability to consumers. Ultimately, this means consumers remain
engaged and are more likely to stick around. [14]

must actively decide to cancel the subscription, rather than the ac-

customers are more likely to transact on a continuous basis, than

6 RESULTS
Overall, this empirical study set out to achieve two core objectives.
The first, test the SaaS Index against traditional models of Invest-
ment Theory and second, attempt to understand what the drivers
of SaaS companies’ value on public markets.
On the first of these, the study found that the SaaS Index violated
two of the primary concepts in Investment Theory. On the Capital
Asset Pricing Model, while the level of risk did correlate with the
level of return as expected, the premium paid for the risk endured
far exceeded the market risk premium paid out in the US over the
same time period.
The Sharpe ratio of the SaaS Index was significantly lower than
the corresponding benchmarks of the S&P 500 and Nasdaq com-
posite. This represents that SaaS investors sustained higher levels
of volatility on their path to higher returns.
The SaaS Index also did not correspond to the assumptions set
out by Fama and French’s model, with the larger firms outperform-
ing the small and there being little relationship between the relative
quasi-value vs growth, although the latter distinction was less ap-
propriate due the nascence of the industry. These violations posed
the question of what it was that drives these SaaS company values
as a whole and what factors distinguish them on an individual level.
The latter half of the empirical study yielded greater explanatory
results. There was an evident relationship between the Enterprise
Value of the company against its revenue, its market capitaliza-
tion, and the overall return. This model held strong explanatory
power and represents a potential new framework from which SaaS
company prices can be reviewed and expected values created.
Despite these empirical findings, a large number of factors re-
main highly qualitative. These elements are likely the more im-
portant value drivers which underpin the equation promoted. This
includes, but is not exclusive to the subscription model employed by
these companies which creates predictable, reliable revenue whilealso reducing customer acquisition cost. As demonstrated, this is
not the sole attraction with technology related benefits including
capital efficiency and scalability also important factors. Addition-
ally, the macroeconomic story in the last 12 months observed is
positive, with SaaS companies representing the beneficiaries of a
wider market slump and intervention thereafter.
7 CONCLUSION
In conclusion, the SaaS industry has enjoyed a meteoric rise in
public valuations over the past number of years. It has not only
outstripped industry benchmarks such as the S&P 500 and Nasdaq,
but has improved upon internal metrics such as EV/Revenue and
profitability as highlighted by industry research explored. [20]
The SaaS Index constructed as part of the empirical study of this
paper demonstrated that this rapid growth is seemingly contrary
to two of the core pillars of fundamental investment theory. The
Index, provided a significantly higher return profile in respect to
risk contrary to the Capital Asset Pricing Model and provided anti-
thetical outcomes to the Fama-French factors highlighted. While
it is recognised that the conditions under which the SaaS Index
was reviewed are not the ideal for either model, it is necessary that
there exists some model which can provide explanatory power for
this growth if the traditional models fail to.
This paper identified several further variables, as highlighted by
industry research and practice which seem to have a stronger rela-
tionship with the expected return of the SaaS Index. The Enterprise
Value in relation to revenue and the relative size of the company
were both positively correlated with the return of the companies
in the Index and yielded strong explanatory power in the linear
regression model.
Ultimately, the paper recognises that the current backward-
looking sample that the Index represents is not sufficient to con-
clude future predictions of expected returns. Rather, it is beneficial
to recognise that there are key underlying characteristics of SaaS
companies which make these metrics relevant to price. The latter
sections of this paper have identified some such characteristics such
as the subscription model, ability to scale, and demonstrated their
impact on the attractiveness of the sector.
The paper ultimately contested that the combination of recurring
revenue structures and the ability to scale rapidly and effectively
has helped to spur on this growth. That the qualitative factors
which underpin the SaaS industry are core drivers of quote.
Remarks
Overall, this paper has sought to add to the blossoming literature
and theoretical frameworks surrounding SaaS companies and their
valuations.
Naturally, as the industry is young, exciting, and enjoyed a spec-
tacular bull-run in the markets, most of the data is clouded by these
movements and is ultimately limited to this relatively brief period
of time. As the market matures and constituents become older com-
panies, the market will likely settle. It would be folly to believe
that the current bull run will last forever. The likelihood is that the
models employed by this paper will not be long term solutions but
rather have their place in this current stage of the cycle.
