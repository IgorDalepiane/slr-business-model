Open source is being articulated as an alternative to the intellectual property model to handle creativity. In open
source, a community of individuals makes contributions to a body of knowledge, sets up peer-review systems for

1(Wayner, 2000).
In the intellectual property model, software development is linked with the existence of an owner who controls
the software ’s production and use. Richard Stallman, who is a champion of the open source movement, criticizes the
intellectual property model by asserting that software should be free and that programmers should be motivated by
enhanced reputation and community good, rather than by remuneration or profit. Halbert (2003) opines that opensource is an alternative to the intellectual property model as it is based upon a set of fundamentally different
principles: First, the building blocks, that is, the source code, are available to everyone; second, any improvements
made by the user must be shared with the community of users; third, a business makes money not from the sourcecode, but from its product and support services; and finally, intellectual property rights are not a part of its businessmodel. She further says that instead of centralizing ownership in the hands of a few wealthy individuals, open source
decentralizes ownership and in the process, builds upon the creative energy of thousands. Other authors suggest that
the main difference between open source and proprietary software is in open source ’s focus on sharing and
cooperation, instead of control and exclusivity. They argue that open source licenses are an alternative to the
governance mechanisms in the intellectual property model (Valimaki, 2005, p. 113). Lindberg (2008) notes that in the
intellectual property model the proprietary software companies own the copyright to all of the software thatthey distribute, while, alternatively, in the open source model projects are owned by their communities, or more
precisely by their contributors (p. 156). He explains that, just as depositors become part owners, when they put their
money into the credit union repository, coders become part owners when they put their code into the source coderepository (p. 156).
Another strong argument is that open source is an alternative to traditional economic intellectual property
incentives (Maher, 1999, p. 695). While contemporary intellectual property law is based on the assumption that onlyeconomic incentives can motivate creators, open source contrasts that sentiment in every sense. Proponents of opensource argue that the open source model exposes the problems of proprietary systems and that the model ’s
philosophy emphasizes the good of the community over the good of the individual (Halbert, 2003). The most
significant feature of open source is the voluntary contribution and sharing by creative people, just for the sake of theinformation being used and enjoyed by others. The ongoing trend in contemporary literature is to praise this value
system and to advocate for its expansion into other realms of technology (Halbert, 2003). Open Source Chemistry,
Open Source Biotechnology and Open Source Drug Discovery are a few examples of this trend. But as clarified earlier,open source relies on the principles of intellectual property rights to create binding legal relationships among creators.
Thus, the property relationship, social organization and business dimensions of open source need to be analyzed to
determine how close open source is to emerging as an alternative to the present model of intellectual property.

In copyright history, the earliest efforts to establish authorship and property rights were the result of lobbying by

2(Jaszi, 1991, p. 296). But even before romanticism, the term “authorship ”signified a

Thus, the law predominantly relies on the concepts of authorship and inventorship to ascertain ownership of
intellectual property. But this artificial construction of authorship creates a myth of genuine authorship consisting ofindividual acts and it fails to take into account the reality of writing practices (Woodmansee, 1984, p. 926). As
Woodmansee (1984) points out, most writings have been and are still carried out collaboratively (p. 926). That is why
Jaszi (1991) complains that the law often proves ungenerous to non-individualistic cultural productions like “folkloric
works ”, which cannot be re-imagined as products of solitary, original “authorship ”on the part of one or more discrete
and identifiable authors (p. 302). The only deviation we see from the idea of individual authorship is the category of
“joint authorship ”accommodated within the copyright law framework. But to constitute a work of joint authorship,
the law requires that several authors collaborate on a pre-concerted basis and that the contribution of one author is
not distinct from the contribution of the other authors. Whenever the contribution of one author is distinct from the
other authors, the tendency in the law is to disaggregate the collaborative creation and to recognize individualauthorship on separate copyrightable parts (p. 314).
In effect, intellectual property law completely disregards the cultural value of serial collaborations —that is, works
resulting from successive elaborations of an idea. The age of folklores being over, this issue becomes more acute in the
modern day intellectual networks, where people voluntarily collaborate over distance and time. In this novel creativeenvironment, the real identities of individual contributors often become irrelevant and they will be better known for
their inputs, which if useful will immediately be merged with equally useful inputs from others. Thus, the final creation
will be the result of a highly distributed production process, and, if we go by the principles of intellectual property law,it will be characterized by the presence of widely dispersed individual ownership. The individual ownership, even
though theoretically possible, will often become meaningless and impractical, as individual contributions will be
inseparable from the collective creation and thus are hard to assess. When addressing collective creations, the realfunction of property must be to serve as a mechanism for unified and collective ownership. However, the
contemporary practices in intellectual property law seek a coerced centralization, either in the form of an employee –
employer relationship or through some other artificially created license-based relationship, insisting convergence ofownership. Both methods of coerced centralization rest on fragmented pieces of individual property, which is nothingmore than a myth in this case. In effect, intellectual property facilitates private/individual ownership over collective

1.2 |The use of intellectual property to promote collaborative research in software
Software development always has been a collaborative work, where multiple programmers work on separate tasks
based on a clear division of labor. This has been the case since the early 1950s, when computing technology was at itsinfancy (Weber, 2004). In those days, programmers perceived themselves as craft people and completed their work
with extreme playfulness. Just like the traditional craft people, programmers wanted the freedom to manage their
projects from start to finish (Weber, 2004). But this went against management prerogatives and the then prominentFordist production theory, which emphasized the division of tasks to increase efficiency (Weber, 2004). The
implementation of Fordist thinking led to a clear-cut division of labor in software development, and programmers lost
their autonomy. In this new production model, managers talked to clients and took down their requirements.Architects worked on the algorithm and designed a work plan for coding, testing and installations. The managers then
organized the programmers into different teams and entrusted them to code for different parts of the requisite
program, adhering to the work plan. When computing technology began to advance, it became much more complexand almost impossible to program without adhering to organized development methods. Initially this began as aninformal organization, but, with the rise of the software industry, it soon evolved into systematic methodologies.
The “Waterfall development model ”was one of the earliest methodologies adopted for programming. In this
methodology, software development is a process that flows down steadily through different phases. This modelactually originated in manufacturing and construction industries, but as no methodology tailored for software
development was available at that time, the waterfall development model was adopted for software development. In
“incremental build model ”, another software development methodology, the target software is divided into several
components and each of them is developed separately. Once development of each component is finished, the
software is delivered to the client. The uniqueness of this model lies in its design, which makes it possible for many
people to work in teams on different functions of the same software. At present, “agile software development ”is the
most popular methodology in the industry. In this model, software is developed through collaboration between self-
organizing, cross-functional teams. This methodology employs certain interesting concepts like “co-location ”and “pair
programming ”.“Co-location ”refers to the practice of putting programmers together into groups and asking them to
work together in a single room. In “pair programming ”two programmers are asked to work together on the same
computer. The “Agile Methodology ”thus offers a closer collaboration between programmers and consequently is
preferable for complex programs. This exposition of software methodologies is done to show how capital adapts to
situations, where innovation is possible only through collaborative effort. These methodologies are tailored toorganize collective labor in such a fashion as to lead to the intended result. But it would be a challenge to manage
intellectual property that is developed through this collective labor. Here is the significance of coerced centralization
of property ownership that intellectual property law insists.
The employer –employee relationship is one such instance, where intellectual property law forgets about the real
author and instead vests ownership with the employer. A “work made for hire ”signifies a situation, where the work is
prepared by the employee within the scope of his or her employment (Garon & Ziff, 2011, p. 491). In situations, wherethe creation of a computer program qualifies as a “work made for hire ”, copyright law vests ownership of the program
with the employer.

EULAs impose prohibitions on reverse engineering, decompilation, modification, sharing and resale of software. Thus,
the business model of proprietary software is dependent on the revenue from software licenses.
1.3 |Private property elements in the open source model
Beyond theory and practice, the humanistic argument for intellectual property rights is that such rights enable thecreator to make a living by exploiting his creation. To participate in the modern-day market driven economy, thecreator has to rely on external entities that are capable of distributing, and there by exploiting his work. In the process,the creator in the majority of the circumstances will be required to surrender all, or substantially all of his rights to this
external entity. This is usually done through licensing. This explains the complex structure of property rights in
facilitating creations and the use of licenses in facilitating the exploitation of these creations. In this structure, thedifferent entities, such as the creator, the intermediaries and the user, are all interconnected through specific
economic linkages. Comparatively, open source uses the same licensing system to create an altogether different sort
of relationship between the creator, the intermediaries and the user, by enabling the user to have more freedom withregard to the creation and permitting better utilization of it. Thus, the open source licenses also rely on intellectual
property protection to create a relationship between the parties. The basic characteristic of the open source method
of software development is that the community of unpaid volunteers contributes creations to a community leader, theintermediary, which is often an incorporated company or organization. This community leader in turn facilitates the72|

making submissions. It is the intermediary ’s task to manage these submissions, identify the errors, select the improved

freedoms to the user depending on the business model followed by the intermediary. Quid pro quo to the freedoms
granted, the license may impose certain restrictions on the users ’future actions. Certain licenses require the user to
distribute a modified version of the software only along with the underlying source code.

Large scale cooperation in production may face several problems. Highly parallel and distributed contributions in
open source may pose challenges considering the business interests involved. Thus, it becomes necessary for companiesinvolved in open source to evolve a governance system that regulates community creation inside the community.
Governance in open source has to do with how decisions are made about the workings of the community (Goldman &
Gabriel, 2005, p. 30). It is the control over the property which enables the business organization to create such a system.As open source entails a large scale cooperative network, it may require governance at different levels. The open source
model is never chaotic, but rather, a well-organized modality of production. But while comparing it with the proprietary
model, Raymond (2001) says, Linux overturned his belief that softwares needed to be carefully built like cathedralsbecause of the critical complexity involved (p. 21). Speaking of “Linux Trovald ’ss t y l e ”, Raymond notes that the norm was
to delegate everything possible and Linux community seemed to resemble a bazaar of differing agendas and approaches
out of which a coherent and stable system could seemingly emerge only by a succession of miracles (p. 22). But, later on,
with the increased orientation of open source toward business, a formalized governance system slowly emerged.Nonetheless, voluntary participation and voluntary selection of tasks remained the key elements of the open source
process. This, unlike the corporate atmosphere, lets each individual choose what he wishes to work on or contribute to.
Even though there is no enforced division of labor, there is a conscious segregation of tasks that the volunteers canchoose to do. Thus open source projects are distributed production processes that work through on-line interactions
between members. There will be standard versioning tools, compilers, bug-tracking tools and customs for using them.
And then there will be a hierarchy of developers centered on the concept of meritocracy, whose rights are expanded onlyafter their abilities have been demonstrated (Goldman & Gabriel, 2005, p. 7). The biggest mistake a business organizationcan make in open source is active and visible control. This will demotivate and turn off volunteers and contributors. Thus,
companies have to develop a cover-up in the form of a formalized procedure by which community members are elevated
to different levels of the governance structure, based on their proven ability. Then, the company ’s control will be invisible
and unnoticed. At the same time, the company will retain its control over community creation through the licensing
system and it can exercise this control through this governance structure.
When a user-programmer makes a contribution to the project, the contribution will first undergo a peer review,
where other members in the same user group may approve, disapprove or even make improvements to it. Once
approved, these submissions will travel up through a hierarchy of gate keepers who are responsible for that particular
part of the code base (Weber, 2004). This may again travel up to higher levels of the hierarchy and may finally beincluded in the release version. In the Linux development community, the peer-review process is a structured
approach of generating criticism of existing versions, evaluating those criticisms and eliminating “errors ”(Lee & Cole,
2003, p. 639). Thus, even an ordinary user can propose changes to the code. This encourages a large number ofdevelopers to add extensions and improvements to the base creation. Individual contributors are closely monitored bythe community, and an individual ’s position in the governance structure is dependent on the quality of his
contributions: active and talented members may climb the ladder of hierarchy, while passive members will remain at
the same level. On the top of this structure is a leadership group over which the business organizations have control.The versioning and release of official versions of the community creation are done under the supervision of this
leadership group. Further, the rules of practice, customs, ethics, technology standards etc. of the community are
decided by this leadership group. These are all formulated in a way that is complementary to the company ’s business
model. It is the control over property in open source that creates the distinction between the contributing
programmer and the leadership group. The leadership group in reality represents the “ownership of property ”.
The collaborative nature of open source often poses challenges in directing creativity to the areas desired by
business organizations. If the individual contributions are not channeled into priority areas, there will be a cluttering of
the open source arena with unprofitable innovations. But at the same time, the business organization may have clarity
with regard to the priority areas that require immediate community attention. The role of the leadership group is toidentify priority areas and direct participants ’creativity toward those areas. The leadership group will set these
priority areas as tasks to which individual contributors or small user groups can collaborate and contribute.

It is a common practice in contemporary literature to refer to open source as “commons ”, and there is an ongoing debate
regarding the possibilities of a “privately created commons ”to mitigate the negative effects of intellectual property
expansion (Boyle, 2008, p.203). “Commons ”refers to a particular institutional form of structuring the right to access, use
and control resources. It signifies collective ownership of resources. But in reality, open source neither puts thecommunity creation into the public domain nor does it protect the creation as community property. It is notable thatopen source licenses always rely on private property rights, either of the individual contributor or of the consortium or
company that maintains the community. The fallacies of this model lie in our lack of understanding of the communities ’
role in open source creations. Open source works on community based peer-production. It is the result of manyindividuals contributing to a common project motivated by a variety of reasons. But then how would private property

assumption that community use will result in mismanagement. Interestingly, open source is a classic illustration of how a
community will nurture and nourish even private property, simply for being allowed to use it.
