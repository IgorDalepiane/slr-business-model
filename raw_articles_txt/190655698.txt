BSTRACT : This paper examines the effect of the freemium strategy on Google Play,
an online marketplace for Android mobile applications. By analyzing a large panel
data set consisting of 711 ranked mobile apps, we found that the freemium strategyis positively associated with increased sales of the paid mobile apps. Positive trial
experience as represented by high review rating of the free version of a mobile app

the sales of its paid version. This finding suggests that although offering a free trial
version is a viable way to improve the visibility of a mobile app, offering a qualityfree app is more important in boosting sales of the paid app. Moreover, we found

Since Apple made a key turning point in the business by launching its App Store

For example, there are contradictory viewpoints among mobile app vendors about
the effect of free apps on their paid version. Some vendors believe that providing
free apps decreases the total sales of the paid versions of the apps. Despite the
potential revenue streams from free apps ’in-app purchases, advertising, and sub-
scriptions, these mobile app vendors still see the traditional pay per download as the
most prolific revenue source to target. And to them, the effect of a free app on its
paid version is potentially negative, as the free version may cannibalize the sales of
the paid version, leading to lower overall revenue and profit.
Other mobile app vendors, however, believe that offering a free version of their
app is an effective way to attract consumers, as some consumers who purchased their
app would not have done so if they had not experienced the free version of the app.
Moreover, a free app that makes it to or near the top of the ranking list can
significantly increase the visibility of the app because it will be displayed on the
main page of the website. This creates a vicious loop and will lead to more trials of
the free app, which in turn can potentially boost sales of the paid version. Hence,
these vendors explicitly encourage consumers to try the free version first before
making a purchase.
These contradictory viewpoints leave us with several interesting questions: How
does the free version of a mobile app affect the sales of its paid version? Do the
results learned from use of the freemium strategy in other product markets still hold
in the mobile app market? And last but not least, what is the role of the ranking
systems for both free and paid apps in the Android mobile app market? To answer
these questions, we developed a set of hypotheses and empirically tested them using
a longitudinal data set collected from Google Play ’s website. From our findings, we
attempt to offer insights to app developers and marketplace designers into how
consumers make their purchase decisions in the presence of widely available free
apps.
Literature Review
The Freemium Strategy
The effects of the freemium strategy in the software market have been studied
extensively in the literature [ 10,22,28,41]. Bawa and Shoemaker [ 6] maintained
that free samples can create three potential effects on sales: (1) an acceleration effect,
whereby consumers decide to purchase a product earlier than they would have if the
free sample had not been given; (2) a cannibalization effect, whereby the free sample
reduces sales of the product; and (3) an expansion effect, which occurs when
consumers would not otherwise purchase a product without the free sample.
Because the outcome of the freemium strategy depends on the interplay of these
three effects, which are in turn determined by various factors, the impact of free
samples can differ significantly across different categories.
In the traditional software market, in addition to the widely established brand
building [ 37,41] and learning effects [ 14], software vendors often exercise theFREEMIUM STRATEGY FOR MOBILE APPS 329

freemium strategy to exploit economic rents ascribed to positive network effects
[23], product complementarity [ 47], or an accelerated diffusion process [ 34].
However, unlike traditional software applications, which often exhibit demand side
economy of scale, mobile apps are generally characterized by minimal learning and
switching costs and a low degree of interdependence among apps from the same
developer. Hence, the classic network effects or product complementarity theory
cannot be used to account for the dynamics in the mobile app market. Rather,
adoption of paid mobile apps depends critically on two important factors: product
visibility and product quality.
Determinants of Success in the App Market: Product Visibility and
Product Quality
The importance of product visibility and product quality in determining the success
of mobile apps in the market can be explained in the context of a new product
adoption process. In the process, consumers generally go through five steps: (1)
awareness, (2) interest, (3) evaluation, (4) trial, and (5) adoption [ 51]. The first step,
awareness, is when consumers are informed about the product attributes [ 36]. In a
competitive market environment, as the number of products in a category increases,
generating awareness of a new product becomes more difficult [ 35]. Android app
market presents such a challenge not only because there are now close to 1.3 million
apps [ 4] but also because every month some 15,000 new apps are introduced [ 50].
Product visibility, measured by the rank of the app, is therefore essential to generat-
ing a high level of awareness of an app. This is especially true if we consider that
most consumers find the apps they are to download through the ranking list
published by online marketplaces such as Google Play [ 1]. Highly ranked apps
also generate more interest among potential users because they reflect the interest of
other consumers, and such peer influence has been shown to be one of the primary
drivers of purchase decisions in online shopping [ 7].
During a new product ’s evaluation and trial steps, product quality is critical in
determining whether consumers will purchase it. In the software market, consumers
are likely to judge the quality of software on the basis of the quality of the free trial
version [ 10]. In the mobile app market, the quality of a free app plays a critical role
in the purchase decision of its paid version. This is because many app developers
provide free apps with similar quality to their paid version albeit containing ads or
with reduced functionality. A study conducted by AdMob [ 1] revealed that the
primary reason for purchasing paid apps was that consumers liked the free version
and decided to upgrade to the paid version. Furthermore, other studies have shown
that on Google Play and Apple ’s App Store, a large portion of paid app consumers
had tried the free version first before purchasing [ 49].
In the online environment, product quality can also be inferred from online
customer reviews, which are generally left by customers who have purchased and
experienced the product, and so the reviews are very relevant and informative. The330 LIU, AU, AND CHOI

impact of online reviews, along with product ratings and number of reviews, on the
purchase decision of customers has been studied extensively in a variety of markets,
such as movies [ 34], online book stores [ 35], online shopping stores [ 36], and video
games [ 37]. The results of these studies generally support the idea that online
reviews have become a primary reference for consumers, and the consumer-gener-
ated information plays a significant role in the purchase decisions of buyers [ 44].
However, the extent to which online reviews affect consumers ’purchase decisions
differ due to a variety of factors, such as product type, product visibility, quality and
quantity of the reviews, reputation of the reviewers, and freshness of the reviews.
Product characteristics are one of the most significant factors moderating the effect
of online reviews. The impact of online reviews on sales of utilitarian products,
compared with that on sales of hedonic products, is relatively high due to the focus
on product functionality rather than on individual preferences in product evaluations
[38]. Similarly, such an impact is more significant in the evaluation of search
products [ 31,39], whose quality can be evaluated before consumption (e.g., a
calculator), than in that of experience products (e.g., wine), whose quality can
only be determined after consumption [ 36,40].
The popularity of a product may also moderate the impact of online reviews. With
thousands of products competing for consumer attention and media coverage, less
attractive products receive little attention and product coverage, resulting in a higher
degree of product uncertainty. Online reviews thus become a primary source of
information for consumers to reduce such uncertainty. Hence, it is not surprising to
see that products less popular in the market are more significantly influenced by
online reviews [ 37].
In terms of the direction of feedback, negative online reviews are found to be more
critical in the decision-making process than positive online reviews due to the
overall risk aversion among consumers [ 35]. This is consistent with the results of
previous research demonstrating that in the traditional brick-and-mortar stores,
negative word of mouth more strongly decreases the willingness to purchase than
positive word of mouth increases the willingness to purchase [ 41].
The quality and quantity of reviews also play an important role in increasing sales
volume. Prospective consumers tend to rely more on online reviews that are more
thoroughly composed [ 36] and those with a higher number of reviews [ 34]. In
addition, the impact of reviews is more substantial if the reviews are written by
reputable users, although such an effect on sales performance decreases over time
[42].
Although the foregoing results have been consistently demonstrated in the litera-
ture, almost all of the aforementioned studies examined the impact of product
visibility and product quality on sales of the same product. Therefore, it will be
interesting to see how the free version of a product affects the market performance of
its paid version in a market where the freemium strategy is implemented. In
particular, it is compelling to ask whether the findings in the extant literature still
hold when both the free and the paid versions of the same product are ranked and

Dynamics in Android App Market of Google Play
Google Play is not the first website to publish product ranks and review ratings.
With the rapid advance in data analytics technology, more and more electronic
commerce sites (such as Amazon.com) have provided product rank and user reviews
of the products listed on their websites to assist consumers in making their purchase
decisions. In turn, researchers have keenly identified the rich research opportunity
associated with the vast amount of data available on these websites and proposed
innovative ways to take advantage of the data resources [ 24,26]. In the past decade,
researchers have studied the relationship between product rank and actual sales and
used the demand data recovered from product rank to estimate consumer surplus [ 7],
analyze price elasticity [ 11], and study product cannibalization [ 25]. These studies
offer a solid theoretical foundation for our research.
Although Google Play adopts a ranking system similar to the ones implemented by
many e-commerce sites, one important distinction lies in the simultaneous publica-
tion of the ranking lists for both the free and the paid apps on Google Play, which is
typically not present in other websites that sell regular commodities. This offers us a
unique opportunity to examine the impact of the adoption of the free version of a
mobile app on its paid version. On Google Play, the visibility and quality of a mobile
app are largely manifested through two important indicators: the product rank and
the review rating. In the next section, we develop several hypotheses to investigate
how these two factors contribute to the adoption of the paid version of a mobile app.
Research Model and Hypotheses
Impact of a Free App on Sales of Its Paid Version
In the traditional software market, due to the difficulty in obtaining sales data,
studies involving the freemium strategy have largely adopted analytic tools, and
the findings are highly contingent upon a variety of factors. For example, the
performance of sampled software has been found to differ by software categories
[22] and strategies for sampling (e.g., full functionality with limited time period or
limited functionality without time limitation) [ 42]. The profitability of the freemium
strategy often depends critically on the demand for product compatibility and the
strength of network effects [ 9,10]. However, even without strong network effects,
offering a fully functional software for free in the early stage may boost a software
vendor ’s profit in the long run [ 34].
In the mobile app market, we expect the net impact of the freemium strategy on
sales to be positive due to the unique characteristics of mobile apps. As a highly
centralized marketplace, Google Play is an official portal for app developers to
publish their apps and is therefore extremely competitive due to the large number
of competing apps in each category, the very low search cost, and the ease of
comparison across a variety of offerings.3This market structure leads to a fourth
“competitive ”effect beyond the acceleration, cannibalization, and expansion effects332 LIU, AU, AND CHOI

=== PÁGINA 9 ===
identified in Bawa and Shoemaker [ 6]. When a large number of substitutes in a
product category are available for free, there is probably a higher degree of canni-
balization of these free substitutes than of a paid app ’s own free version. Moreover,
not offering a free version may significantly reduce the awareness of and interest in
the product, which may hurt sales more than cannibalization does [ 3]. Given that
cannibalization is more likely to emerge in markets with a high degree of product
differentiation [ 25], and in the mobile app market there exists a relatively low degree
of product heterogeneity, one would expect the competitive effect to dominate the
cannibalization effect in this market.
In addition to economics reasons, the freemium strategy may also stimulate
demand due to consumer psychology. Kim and Morris [ 40] showed that consumers
experiencing a trial of low-involvement products tend to have better affective and
cognitive responses to the products than to products requiring high involvement.
Similarly, Kempf [ 39] argued that consumers with personal experience with a
product tend to demonstrate stronger belief, confidence, attitudes, and purchase
intentions than those with indirect experience with it, such as through advertising.
Given the similarity between mobile apps and the product types examined in the
foregoing studies, we expect that the freemium strategy would have a similar impact
in the mobile app market. Therefore, we predict that offering the free version of a
mobile app is more likely to improve the market performance of its paid version, as
represented by the number of downloads of the paid app.
Hypothesis 1: Offering the free version of a mobile app is positively associated
with a larger number of downloads of its paid version.
Impact of the Rating and the Rank of a Free App on the Rank and the
Revenue of Its Paid Version
The existing literature has suggested that product rank and online reviews are good
indicators of product visibility and product quality, and both have been shown to
have a significant impact on consumer ’s online purchase decision [ 12,19,45,53]. In
the case of mobile apps, an interesting difference arises from the fact that a lot of
mobile apps offer a free trial version for consumers. As mentioned earlier, there is
ample anecdotal evidence that shows that consumers interested in purchasing a
mobile app always prefer to try the free version when one is available [ 29,32].
Consequently, the experience from the free version trial will be highly influential in
the subsequent purchase intention. Because Google Play publishes information
about the ranks and ratings of both the free and the paid apps, an interesting question
arises: How do the visibility and user satisfaction of the free app, as represented by
the rank and the rating of its free version, influence sales of its paid version?
Drawing on the extant literature, among paid apps that offer a free version, we
expect that a higher rank of the free version would increase the visibility of the app,
which in turn would result in more trials of the free app. Because some consumersFREEMIUM STRATEGY FOR MOBILE APPS 333

who try out the free version of the app will decide to buy the paid version, all else
being equal (e.g., the quality of the app), the larger the user base of the free app, the
more users will buy the paid version (note that the ratio of conversion might
decrease, but the absolute number of users will always increase with more free

be ambiguous. On the one hand, a highly rated free app can effectively promote the
sales of its paid version. On the other hand, some users who are very satisfied with
the free version will end up not purchasing the paid version, leading to some
cannibalization of the paid app. However, if we compare two paid apps that both
offer a free version, given the highly competitive nature of the mobile app market
and the abundance of substitute products, the app with a lower quality free version is
even more likely to discourage potential buyers from purchasing its paid version. In
other words, both apps may suffer from the cannibalization effect, but net sales will
still favor the app with the better rating. Therefore, for paid apps that offer a higher
quality free version (as reflected in the review rating of the free version), we expect
the impact of the freemium strategy to be more significant than it is for apps that
offer a lower quality free version.

product to reduce uncertainty. This is especially true for experience goods such as
PC software. However, in the mobile app market, for apps offering a free version,
consumers can test the features and functionalities of the apps before purchase,
technically turning them into search goods. Consequently, we expect the impact of

Differential Impact of the Rank and the Rating of the Free Version of
Hedonic and Nonhedonic Apps
As with traditional software, mobile apps may be categorized into two subgroups:
hedonic and nonhedonic. Game and entertainment apps, which are mainly used for
fun, can be classified as hedonic apps, and such apps as utility and business
programs can be classified as nonhedonic apps. The evaluation of hedonic products
is usually subjective and tends to vary from person to person, as it is largely based
on individual preferences rather than commonly accepted standards, whereas the
evaluation of nonhedonic products is usually objective and consistent across users.
Researchers have found that the impact of online reviews on hedonic products is
relatively smaller than the impact on nonhedonic products [ 8]. Such a finding is
expected to be more apparent in the mobile app market because there is often a free
version of the app. In the mobile app market, because the majority of the free apps
offer features and functionalities similar to their paid versions, consumers have
plenty of opportunity to thoroughly evaluate the apps before they make a purchase.
When it comes to hedonic apps, due to their more engaging nature (e.g., games),
consumers tend to spend more time when they get to try the apps, relative to
nonhedonic apps. This in turn leads to more confidence in the consumers ’own
evaluations of the app and, therefore, greater likelihood of the consumers ’relying on
their personal experience rather than the review ratings of other people to determine
whether they will purchase the paid version. Hence, it can be expected that the
impact of the rating of the free version of a mobile app on its paid version will be
less significant for hedonic apps than for nonhedonic apps.
When consumers rely primarily on their own experience in determining whether to
purchase an app, their awareness of the app (and hence the chance the app will be
picked for free trial) becomes a more salient factor in ultimately driving the sales of
the app. Because there are more than a million apps in the Google Play store, one
that makes it to the top-ranked list achieves much greater visibility and hence an
increased chance of being purchased.Therefore, we argue that, relative to a non-
hedonic app, the chance that a hedonic app will be adopted by a consumer depends
more on its visibility to consumers (as represented by its rank4) than on the opinion
of other consumers (as represented by its rating). Therefore, we expect that theFREEMIUM STRATEGY FOR MOBILE APPS 335

Data and Measures
Sample
To test our hypotheses, we collected data from Google Play and focused on its Android
apps store, an online marketplace dedicated to promoting the adoption of mobile apps
compatible with the Android platform. In addition to providing links for downloading
apps, Google Play ’s Android apps store also publishes various ranking lists. Among
them, the Top Free, Top Paid, and Top Grossing lists are the most relevant for our
research questions. The Top Free and Top Paid ranking lists include the top 504
applications in the free and paid categories based on the number of downloads, whereas
the Top Grossing list includes the top 504 apps based on their revenue, which consists of
both upfront app purchase revenue and in-app purchases (i.e., fees to obtain an advanced
feature or to unlock a level in a game). Because these lists are updated daily, we created
three software agents to automatically parse on a daily basis the three ranking lists and
product web pages related to the ranked apps in each of the three lists.

Variables and Measurement
The data for the majority of the variables were readily available from the web pages we
parsed on Google Play. However, a major data challenge in our study was the lack of
actual sales data. Like many electronic commerce sites such as Amazon.com and other
mobile app providers such as Apple, Nokia, and RIM, due to competitive reasons,
Google does not reveal the actual number of downloads for the apps purchased through
Google Play. Without these data, we could not test hypotheses related to the impact of
the free version of an app on the sales of its paid version. This challenge is very similar to
the one confronting many IS researchers who seek to understand the relationship
between product rank and actual sales on various online marketplaces [ 7,11,25]. In
most of these studies, product rank is publicly available, whereas actual sales data are
not. However, it is generally assumed that product rank is largely determined by the
actual sales on the majority of these e-commerce sites. Given this assumption, by
analyzing the available historical sales and product rank data, researchers have found
that there exists an inverse relationship between product rank and actual sales [ 7,11,24,
25], which can often be represented by the power law, or Pareto Type I distribution
(Pareto 1896), as shown in the following equation:FREEMIUM STRATEGY FOR MOBILE APPS 337

Given this relationship, if the scale and shape parameters are known, researchers can

examined how the product rank changes in response to a change in demand by
purchasing a large quantity of several low-ranked books on Amazon.com. More
recently, Garg and Telang [ 24] developed a simple yet effective approach to estimate
the demand parameters by jointly solving the foregoing Pareto equation for both the Top
Paid and the Top Grossing ranking lists in Apple ’s App Store.
Although the foregoing methods have been widely adopted in studies that seek to
recover the actual sales from publicly available product rank data, these methods are less
applicable to our case. As revealed by several studies [ 46,48], unlike other e-retailers,
Google Play does not rank its Android apps based solely on the number of downloads.
Although the download number still plays an important role in the ranking algorithm
adopted by Google Play to unknown degrees, other factors such as rating, freshness of
the app, retention rate, and history of growth have all been found to influence the rank
[18]. Given the unknowns involved in the ranking algorithm, the inverse function used
by prior studies could no longer be applied to our data. In light of this new challenge, we
had to resort to another, nontraditional approach to recover the sales data.

Discussion
The main empirical question in this study is whether offering a free version of a
mobile app affects the sales of its paid version, and if so, how the existence of the
free version interacts with other product attributes such as review rating and applica-tion type (e.g., low-involvement vs. high-involvement product; hedonic vs. nonhe-
donic product) in shaping a consumer ’s adoption decision. Our findings provide
empirical evidence that helps to answer these questions.
First, we found that offering consumers a free trial version of a mobile app is
positively associated with increased sal es of its paid version (H1). This finding
provides strong support for the freemium strategy adopted by a number of
developers in the Android app market. As discussed earlier, the nature of the
Android ecosystem has led to a very competitive mobile app market. With
thousands of apps available for consumers to choose from, offering a free versionis an effective way to increase the awareness and interest of an unknown or
newly introduced app. However, some developers are skeptical about the con-
sequences of such a strategy, as they question whether offering a free version of
an app might cannibalize sales of the paid version. When a free version of an app
is available, consumers who would have paid for the app might choose to
continue to use the free version instead of purchasing the paid version. Our
empirical findings seem to contradict this outcome and suggest that the benefits
of the freemium strategy may outweigh any potential loss. This finding may be
attributed to a few possibilities. One, it i s likely that cannibalization will still
occur to some extent; however, there is strong competition among mobile appdevelopers due to the rapidly growing population of mobile device users and awell-designed centralized marketplace that facilitates ease of comparison.
Therefore, a mobile app developer who chooses not to offer a free version maylose more customers to other competitors than it would have lost to cannibaliza-
tion from its own free trial version. Two, it is also possible that the majority of
the consumers who adopt the free version of an app are those who would not
have adopted the app if it was offered at a cost, and the free version attracts a lot
of customers who are willing to pay for the paid version after they have
experienced the free version, leading to an expansion effect. Three, as free
apps prevail in the market, consumers will form an expectation that they shouldbe able to try a mobile app for free before purchase, leading to a self-reinforcing
feedback loop that further accelerates the widespread offering of free apps in the
mobile app market. Regardless of which outcome dominates, it is clear from our
empirical results that providing consumers an opportunity to try a mobile app for
free actually benefits the developer more than it hurts it.
Having found support for H1, we proceeded to analyze whether the freemium
strategy has different impact on different types of mobile apps. Given the
similarity in features and functionalities between the free version of most
Android apps and their paid versions, it is not surprising to see that rating of
the free version positively contributes to the sales of the paid version (H2b).FREEMIUM STRATEGY FOR MOBILE APPS 347

However, what is surprising is that the rank of the free version does not have a
significant positive impact on the sales of the paid version (H2a), and this
finding resonates in our sensitivity analysis, where testing the hypotheses on a
subset of the sample with a lower rank does not alter the results. The fact that
H2a is not supported can be attributed to several factors. First, as revealed by
Google and other market analytics firms,12Google ’s app rank is jointly deter-

herd behavior, which suggests that individuals tend to imitate one another [ 5],
does not seem to be as salient in the mobile app market as in other IT markets

of the ranking list, which implies that when consumers are attracted to an app by
the high ranking of its free version, they do not automatically take this high rank
as a sign of superior quality and automatically purchase the paid version. More
than likely, they will still trial the free version to determine if the quality of the
app is indeed satisfactory before they make the purchase.
This finding yields important implications for successful implementation of
the freemium strategy in the mobile app market. It implies that among apps that
offer a free version, those with a higher quality (as represented by the review
rating) are more likely to generate a higher level of sales than those with higher
visibility but without high product qualit y. Given that a lot of developers have
invested heavily in boosting the rank of t heir apps in a number of different ways
[43], while overlooking efforts to improve app quality, this finding reminds
them that the old golden rule of quality determining market performance still
holds well in this rapidly evolving market. With the opportunity to try out an
app for free, the mobile app market has tec hnically created a virtual fitting room
and provides consumers with a shoppin g experience highly similar to the one
found in a traditional department store. Ultimately, consumers will rely more on
their own trial experience rather than on product rank or peer review in making
their purchase decisions, f urther stressing the importance of delivering quality
products in this burgeoning market.

Conclusions and Directions for Future Research
As is generally true with any empirical research, our results are subject to
interpretation and are limited to the data available. The most obvious threat to
the validity of the conclusions reached her e is the longitudinal nature of the data.
It is not clear how long it takes for owners of the free apps to upgrade to a paid
version. Although we did perform an analysis with a time-lagged model, it may
not represent the true pattern of app purchases by free app owners. However, if it
takes longer for a free app user to make a purchase decision, then our results are
underestimating the positive impact of the freemium strategy. Hence, our results
can be interpreted as a conservative, rather than inflated, estimate of this effect.
Future studies could complement our research through use of additional research
methods such as surveys to obtain a more accurate understanding of the upgrade
time frame.
Moreover, although we managed to verify whether an app offers a free version
or not by manually checking all the apps offered by the same developer, we were
not able to gather data on the number of similar apps in the same product
category and thus control for within- category competition. We hope such data
may become more easily attainable for fut ure researchers to further understand
other competitive factors that may also influence a developer ’s decision to adopt
the freemium strategy.
Last but not least, due to the fact that Google publishes only the top-ranked apps,
our sample only includes the top performer in the Android market. Therefore,
splitting the sample based on product rank does not allow us to truly differentiate
popular apps from less popular apps. Our sample would have been more balanced
had we had access to the ranking of the remaining mobile apps.
Despite these limitations, our study is one of the first attempts to empirically
assess the impact of the freemium strategy on both sales and revenue of mobile
apps. Our findings thus both contribute to the academic literature and provide
guidance to practitioners in the mobile app market in several important ways. For
researchers, our study builds on the gr owing body of research that utilizes
publicly available e-commerce data to empirically validate research questions.
Our research has extended Bawa and Shoemaker ’s study [ 5]o nt h et h r e e
sampling effects (acceleration, cannibalization, and expansion) by adding a fourth
“competitive ”effect, accounting for the belief that offering a sample is another
crucial element in market competition when there are many companies vying
with similar products. In such markets, be yond traditional factors such as price,
quality, and marketing efforts, competitive advantage may arise from the adop-
tion of the freemium strategy, as suggested by the higher number of downloads
for paid apps that offer a free version. Our study also contributes to the freemium
literature by showing that, although impr oving product visibility through offering
a freemium product is helpful, it is more important to offer a quality freemium
product, especially for nonhedonic goods. Finally, we have added to the literature
on online word of mouth [ 12,20,45] by testing the impact in the mobile app350 LIU, AU, AND CHOI

Our research also extends existing knowledge of the freemium business model and
