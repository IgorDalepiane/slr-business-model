ABSTRACT
Prior scholars have deeply de ﬁned the sources of value, typologies, and
value capture mechanisms of website businesses. The advent of new e-
businesses, i.e. mobile applications, requires investigation into which are
the successful variations of e-businesses that have been selected andretained for surviving the new digital era, and how they have been
formed. A mixed qualitative-quantitative business model analysis of
2250 mobile applications of the Google Play Store is proposed. Results
are interpreted according to evolutionary lenses. Content apps, whose
value is driven by their e ﬃciency, lock-in, design, and ability to add
complementary monetisation mechanisms (i.e. In-App purchases) beside
the ads value capture schema (or ‘mixed ’for ‘pay per download’ apps),
are the ones that can survive digital Darwinism . E-business models and
their innovations are the products of evolutionary processes; from that,
e-business models should be evaluated and re-evaluated over timeaccording to the modi ﬁcations of the multi-level environment.ARTICLE HISTORY

1. Introduction
Within the last 10 years, the business environment has drastically changed due to the advent of a
series of technological innovations, among which the introduction of mobile data technology is con-
sidered as the key turning point from where thousands of new disruptive e-businesses have been set.In practice, a second wave of e-businesses (after the dot-com one) has arrived.
New and established entrepreneurs and strategists feel under pressure in adapting their ﬁrms to
this change, among which, also ﬁnding the right business model, thus how to create and capture
value (Amit and Zott 2001 ; Zott, Amit, and Massa 2011 ), seems another important challenge in
order to survive the digital transformation and to crack open markets. In practice, today ’sﬁrms are
pushed, within the competitive phase of their business life cycle, to keep pace with the digital revo-lution driven by mobile data technologies –the so-called digital Darwinism by which ﬁrms who
survive and continue competing are the ones that have been able to best adapt themselves to
digital changes –and, relatedly, to adapt their business model according to an evolutionary
dynamic (Chesbrough and Rosenbloom 2002 ; Kask and Prenkert 2018 ).
The main mobile data technology that is driven this revolution is mobile applications, i.e. softwares
accessible by mobile devices that allow access to a series of products/services from anywhere and by
anyone, cutting the spatial and timely barriers to the delivery of value for customers. Apps have
changed the rules of several industries and markets, apart from the intrinsic technological innovation,mainly thanks to their free-based business models that do not always ask customers to pay for

2. Theoretical background
2.1. E-business model
According to a shared deﬁ nition among strategy scholars, the business model of a company mainly
depicts how it creates and captures value (Demil et al. 2015 ; Osterwalder and Pigneur 2010 ; Zott, Amit,
and Massa 2011 ). The business model concept was originally conceived in order to investigate how
new technologies have changed the business environment, mainly with reference to the occurring
dot-com era. The escalation of interest in the business model concept continued with the advent,
at the end of the ‘00s, of disruptive e-businesses mainly based on mobile apps activated by mobile
devices. Since then, the business model literature has been concerned with the study of e-business
models (Zott, Amit, and Massa 2011 ), which were originally interpreted as the means to obtain
value from the technological advantage that is owned (Chesbrough and Rosenbloom 2002 ).
In this vein, the study of Amit and Zott ( 2001 ) can be considered as valuable because providing
insights on which are the sources of value of e-companies. Indeed, these scholars stated that the cre-ation of value in virtual markets is based on: eﬃciency –meaning the fast transmission of data on the
Internet in order to reduce interconnection and negotiation; complementarity –meaning the capacity
to propose products/services that are vertically or horizontally connected; lock-in –meaning the wish
of customers or business partners to repeat transactions with the same ﬁrm because of the irreplace-
able overall value; novelty –meaning the creation of new value by connecting previously unrelated
parties, eliminating ine ﬃciencies in transaction processes or capturing the latent needs of consumers.
Yet, Wirtz, Schilke, and Ullrich ( 2010 ), in recognising the new challenges and opportunities of the
new digital era seminally de ﬁned four general typologies of business model in terms of value creation:
(i) content-oriented , focused on the production and provision of online content through user-friendly
platforms (e.g. The Economist online) almost relying on advertising or premium fees as revenue
streams; ( ii) commerce-oriented , focused on the trading aspects of online transactions using online
media (e.g. eBay), so they rely on commissions as revenue streams; ( iii) context-oriented , focused
on structuring already existent information in order to help Internet users ﬁnd the service that
satisﬁes their needs (e.g. Yahoo), those almost rely on advertising as revenue streams; ( iv) connec-
tion-oriented , focused on o ﬀering virtual spaces that enable participants to join online networks
(e.g. Facebook) that usually pay a subscription for those.
Beside this general classi ﬁcation, McGrath ( 2010 ) and Osterwalder and Pigneur ( 2010 ) have
studied the recurring mechanisms by which ﬁrms catch pro ﬁts. In particular, the following have
been identi ﬁed: ( i)
advertising model ,(ii) freemium model , some parts of the product or service areTECHNOLOGY ANALYSIS & STRATEGIC MANAGEMENT 89

2.2. Evolutionary approaches: theoretical and operational premises
Organisational evolution studies the competitive relationship between organisations and theirenvironment. Within this area there are di ﬀerent approaches, among which are the generalized Dar-
winism and co-evolution (Abatecola, 2012 ;2014 ).
On the one hand, generalised Darwinists try to fully transfer biological assumptions to the social
domain, i.e. the VSR principles and the replicators-interactors mechanisms. The ﬁrst are the well-
known principles for whom species evolve and adapt to their environment by an iterative process.In the social domain it has been transferred in the following way: ( i)current routines, competencies
and/or business practices (i.e. genotypes) undergo intentional or blind variation (Campbell 1965 ), (ii)
certain types of the expressed variations (i.e. phenotypes) are selected by an external or internal selec-
tion, and ( iii)selected variations (i.e. of the underlying genotypes) are retained , preserved, duplicated,
or otherwise reproduced (Breslin and Jones 2012 ).
However, within the generalised Darwinism approach, scholars have not always agreed on the
outlined de ﬁnition of the VSR principles. According to a largely accepted standpoint, the selection
activity is the output of the interplay of the units of analysis of evolution (Hull 1988). Another view,
the so-called ‘continuity hypothesis ’(e.g. Cordes 2007 ;W i t t 2004), declares that evolution is
domain-speci ﬁc: diﬀerent forms of evolution exist apart from the natural one due to the
diﬀerent domains in which evolution is investigated. Yet, it interprets selection and variation as
closely connected by systematic feedbacks. From that, variation is operationalised as the result
of conscious design (Cordes 2007 ;W i t t 2004), leaving space for the intentional variation in evol-
ution. This intentionality is then adapted to the environment –composed of consumers, entrepre-
neurs, or political institutions –who select technologies, products, and routines of behaviour
(Cordes 2007).
The second mechanism proposed by generalised Darwinists is the replicators-interactors one. In
particular, replicators are elements that pass, like genes, to others (e.g. individuals, groups, ﬁrms)
through successive replications, while interactors are entities that interact with their environment
causing di ﬀerential replications –from biological to socio-economic studies (Breslin 2016 ; Cristofaro
2019 ). From this interpretation it seems to be that replicators are the objects of selection; however,
also in this case, there are di ﬀerent
views. For some scholars, replicators are the knowledge com-
ponents within the evolving unit, e.g. routines, that are subjects to be transferred or copied, while
interactors are the vehicles for this transfer or copying activity (Aldrich et al. 2008 ). Another main
standpoint recognised di ﬃculties in separating the knowledge component of the evolutionary
element and the speci ﬁc activity by which it is played out (Breslin and Jones 2012 ): considering
the socially constructed concept of the organisation, the transfer of the knowledge component
occurs by involving others in the activity/task, and the transfer itself is based on communicationand the use of a shared language.90 M. CRISTOFARO

Contrasts do not exist only in the theoretical de ﬁnitions of these mechanisms but also in their
operationalisation. Indeed, Breslin ( 2016) highlighted that there are two main approaches: the
entity and the practice view. According to the enti ty view, replicators are investigated in terms
of ideas, while interactors are considered in ter ms of technological artefacts. According to the
practice view, replicators are investigated in terms of chunks of discrete information and
abstracted cognitive structures, while interactors are considered in terms of behaviour, language
and socially situated practices. The practice view i s similar to that of the continuity hypothesis (e.g.
Cordes 2007;W i t t2004), whose scholars have de ﬁned the ‘business conception ’as the ﬁrm repli-
cator. Indeed, the business conception is considered to mainly lie in the human social-psychologi-cal dispositions.
This last operationalisation, therefore, is closer (in respect to the others) to the co-evolutionary
approach. Indeed, according to co-evolutionary scholars, organisational outcomes derive from thespeciﬁc power conﬁ gurations between organisations and their environment (Ca ﬀerata 2016 ). In con-
trast to some generalised Darwinists (e.g. Aldrich et al. 2008 ), co-evolutionary scholars (Abatecola
2014 ; Cristofaro, Leoni, and Baiocco 2019 ) take into account that the reality faced by ﬁrms is not
objectively existent but is enacted by organisations ’members. Co-evolutionary scholars, in other
words, conceive the ﬁrm-environment relationship as less deterministic and more dialectical; this
highlights the ﬁrst property of co-evolution: thinking in circles (Caﬀerata 2016 ). A second property
of co-evolution is the multi-level logic (Abatecola 2014 ), meant as the in ﬂuences occurring at
diﬀerent analysis levels (macro, meso, and micro). A third property of co-evolution is the interdepen-
dence and reciprocal feedbacks between the di ﬀerent units of analysis (at whatever level) (Lewin and
Volberda 1999 ).
Due to the strong support for the outlined evolutionary assumptions and mechanisms in social
studies, business model researchers have also started applying them to explain how businessmodels evolve and innovate. Chesbrough and Rosenbloom ( 2002 ), for example, highlighted that
business model innovation occurs through increasingly adapting to external environment modiﬁ -
cations. However, as well explained in Kask and Prenkert ( 2018 ), business model innovations that
are selected and retained over time depend on both internal and external factors. The internal modiﬁ -
cation of value creation sources and value capture schemes are, therefore, levers to use for allowing
the
active adaptation of ﬁrms to the change of the environment.
3. Empirical setting and methodology
From the technological point of view, mobile apps are mobile software speci ﬁcally designed by
developers according to the operating system of the device they would like to be run. However,for diﬀerent operating systems there exist di ﬀerent virtual marketplaces from which users can down-
load apps. According to Statista ( 2018b ), Android is the main operating system with 85% of the
market share; moreover, within the Google Play Store, the Android marketplace, are currently avail-able 2.8 million apps.
For this work data were collected from 25 pay per download apps (i.e. a payment is needed to
download the app) and 25 freeapps (i.e. payment is not needed) for each category of the Google
Play Store (i.e. 50). The data collection was done between November 2017 and January 2018 and
took into account the ﬁrst 25 pay per download and 25 free apps ranked in the top 50 over the
data collection period; this 3-months ranking was provided by Similarweb.com. However, for some
categories there were fewer than 25 apps, so because of this 2250 mobile apps instead of 2500
have been collected.
Each app has been downloaded and categorised by the principal investigator and two colleagues
expert in business modelling in terms of value creation scheme according to the four typologies ofWirtz, Schilke, and Ullrich ( 2010 ), and in terms of value capture mechanism according to the business
model patterns of McGrath ( 2010 ) and Osterwalder and Pigneur ( 2010 )–although the possibility to
discover the presence of new value creation schemes and capture mechanisms has been left open.TECHNOLOGY ANALYSIS & STRATEGIC MANAGEMENT 91

4. Measurements of constructs

anisms, the raters studied the apps ’description in order to identify the presence of: optional ser-
vices requesting payments, promotion of other apps from the same software house, other forms
of advertising, subscription and; where none of those elements was present, the app was initially
considered as free. The value capture variable was treated as categorical, but the presence of a
value capture mechanism was not seen as exclusive because others may be contemporarilypresent in the same app. In these cases, the value capture mechanism was considered as
mixed . In sum, the categorisation has been done acco rding to the discrete direct observation
initially following the theoretical de ﬁnitions of the previously cited scholars (Taylor-Powell and

5. Results
Looking at the description of the value capture mechanisms that emerged from the direct obser-vation of the 2250 mobile apps, three new mechanisms emerged: ( i) in-app purchase , the application
is free but it contains extensions, or additional content, which can be purchased directly within theapplication itself. This di ﬀers from the freemium model because the latter o ﬀers two versions of the
same service; ( ii) free as a service , these are free apps that constitute an additional access for users of a
service (e.g. mobile apps of telco services); and ( iii) agreements with third parties, these are apps that
require the contributions of other business players to exist. The application acts as a ‘catalog ’fed by
third parties; companies recognise the bene ﬁt of the greater visibility paying fees to the developer.
In order to investigate the distribution of the 2250 mobile apps according to business model ’s
typologies, two contingency tables have been created –one for ‘free ’mobile apps and one for
‘pay per download ’apps. The contingency tables proposed help in providing a picture of the inter-
relation between typologies of e-businesses and their value capture schemes ( Tables 1 and 2).
Some relevant insights emerge from a ﬁrst look at the tables: ( i)the content category accounts for
80% (901 out of 1125) and 91.6% (1031 out of 1125), respectively, for the ‘free ’and ‘pay per down-
load ’mobile apps ’categories; and ( ii)about 69% of pay per download apps (777 out of 1125) are
based on mixed value capture mechanisms. There was a statistically signi ﬁcant di ﬀerence between
the four typologies of business value creation as determined by the Chi square test and related
post hoc test.
In particular, the post hoc test on free mobile apps revealed that: ( i)within the content typology
there is a signiﬁ cantly higher presence of the capture value scheme called ads and in-app-purchases

value scheme called free as a service ( p= 0.00), ( iii)within the context typology there is a signiﬁ cantly
higher presence of the capture value scheme based on ads ( p= 0.00), and ( iv)within the commerce
typology there is a signi ﬁcantly higher presence of the capture value scheme based on agreements

However, scholars of the continuity hypothesis do not include technological artefacts –which are

From what has been said, replicators may not be entirely transmitted; some of them are selected

The varied replicators of new e-business that are maintained and transmitted to survive digital

Replicators are the subject of di ﬀerent groups, and positioned at di ﬀerent levels, of exerted forces:
organisational (i.e. entrepreneur or strategist, app developers and designers, active investors) andexternal interactors (i.e. app users, app platforms).
In order to explain the co-evolutionary dynamic, the e-business model generation is considered
from its very beginning. The e-business model elements are ﬁrstly generated in the mind of the entre-
preneur/strategist and privately stored until shared with others. In order to realise the e-business and
ﬁnd the necessary funds for it, he/she has to share the e-business model conception (through com-
munication and involvement in the e-business model practice) with other organisational interactors.By reﬂecting on them, organisational interactors act as modi ﬁers of the replicators and vary their
characteristics (Witt 2004 ). In other words, the evolutionary units of analysis are enacted by individ-
uals, groups and organisations and are continually modi ﬁed through actions (Breslin 2016 ; Breslin
and Jones 2012 ; Hodgson and Knudsen 2010 ). However, the di ﬀerent points of view of the interac-
tors, despite in ﬂuencing each other ’s reasoning in a co-evolutionary manner (Cristofaro 2019 ) exert
forces that can be divergent.
The depicted dynamics also apply to the external interactors-e-business model relationships.
Indeed, e-business model elements are di ﬀerently in ﬂuenced by platforms ’technical requirements,
opportunities and threats, as well as users ’responses. For example, app platforms may ask for
diﬀerent fees to be paid, which inﬂ uence the value source ‘design of added monetization ’and (as
a consequence) value capture schemes (Whallalabs.com 2016 ). App users, as emerged from the the-
matic analysis, have, instead, di ﬀerent concerns about the value sources of the e-business model,
inﬂuencing
the allocation of resources from the e-business. Apart from these dynamics, app users

Furthermore, organisational and external interactors are concerned with the reciprocal actions

Stemming from what has been ﬁgured out, variation and selection mechanisms occur in a co-evol-
utionary way. Variation and selection are not only caused by the intentionality of the entrepreneur/
strategist and close collaborators, but also by external forces with whom they have a dialogue (Aba-
tecola 2014 ; Aldrich et al. 2008 ;C aﬀerata 2016 ). How organisational interactors conceive apps ’
business models depends on the output of the circular relationship in which they are involved
with their external interactors (i.e. thinking in circles; Ca ﬀerata 2016 ; Cristofaro 2019 ). From that,
the selection of variations depends on the interdependence and reciprocal feedbacks that occur
at diﬀerent, also in hierarchical terms, interconnected levels (Abatecola 2014 ; Lewin and Volberda
1999 ); this explains why some business models ’features survive the evolutionary process and why
others are selected out. According to the forces exerted, e-businesses are both object and subject
of their evolution, depending on whether or not e-business models ’features are mainly determined
by the external or internal agents (Ca ﬀerata, 2016 ).
Through the consideration of the evolutionary perspective it is possible to recognise that past fra-
meworks (Amit and Zott 2001 ; McGrath 2010 ; Osterwalder and Pigneur 2010 ; Wirtz, Schilke, and
Ullrich 2010 ) do not depict the current reality of e-businesses; indeed, by de ﬁnition, replications
are not static (Aldrich et al. 2008 ), but they have evolved over time: the successful features that e-
businesses should incorporate in order to survive the pressures of the environment are di ﬀerent
from those of the past. Business models should therefore be investigated considering their evolution,especially looking at the replicators-interactors ’dynamics (Abatecola 2014 ; Kask and Prenkert 2018 ),
which 
has a co-evolutionary e ﬀect on the composition of replicators that are passed to the ‘oﬀspring ’.
Conclusions
Through the mixed qualitative-qu antitative e-business model ana lysis and the adoption of evol-
utionary lenses, this work sheds light on which e-business models ’features are needed in order
to survive the new digital era and how they have been formed. Analyses of the varied, selected
and retained e-business models ’features have shown that: ( i)among the four Wirtz, Schilke, and
Ullrich (2010 ) e-business categories, the majority of apps are content-based; ( ii)pay per download
apps are based, in terms of value capture mechanisms, on mixed schemes, usually including thefreemium one; ( iii)free mobile apps are based upon: in-app-purchases (for content apps), free as
a service (for connection apps), ads (for context apps), and agreements with third parties (for com-
merce apps); ( iv)besides Amit and Zott ’s(2001) traditional sources of value, other pivotal features
of apps ’success are present in the new wave of e-busi ness, namely: app design, developer behav-
iour and design of app monetisation; ( v)among the old and new sources of value, only e ﬃciency,
lock-in, design, and the design of added monetisation were found to be statistically signi ﬁcant for
app success.
In other words, evidence has shown that value creation sources (Amit and Zott 2001 ), typologies
(Wirtz, Schilke, and Ullrich 2010 ), and value capture mechanisms (McGrath 2010 ; Osterwalder and
Pigneur 2010 ) have evolved from website e-businesses to the new ones. All these features, indeed,
varied from the ﬁrst to the second wave; however, only some of these variations have been selected.

whose value is driven by their e ﬃciency, lock-in, design, and ability in adding complementary mon-

Theﬁrst and main implication for the business model literature derives from the application of
evolutionary lenses. Indeed, thanks to this, business models and their innovations result as a product
of evolutionary processes. Despite this having been initially discussed by other business model ’s
and evolutionary scholars (e.g. Kask and Prenkert 2018 ), they neither speci ﬁcally delineated the
VSR process nor de ﬁned replicators and interactors of e-business models and their dynamics –
which is the most di ﬃcult thing in the adoption of the evolutionary lens as underlined by Breslin
(2016 ). What evolved from the ﬁrst to second wave of e-businesses are the sources of value, typolo-
gies, and value capture schemes (i.e. replicators) of their business models, whose variation and selec-
tion occurred by the co-evolutionary interactions among internal and external agents. In particular,
interactors are the entrepreneur/strategist, app designers and developers, active investors, app users,and app platforms, which are positioned at di ﬀerent and interconnected hierarchical levels that con-
tinuously exchange feedbacks (Abatecola 2014 ; Lewin and Volberda 1999 ). In this vein, entrepreneurs
or e-business strategists are recommended to accurately understand who are the shapers of the e-business model at that point in time so as to precisely intervene if internal forces are not adequately
responding to the competitive environment. In fact, in accordance with the co-evolutionary literature
(Caﬀerata 2016 ), entrepreneurs or e-business strategists must allow app designers and developers to
modify e-business models when in the presence of a muni ﬁcent competitive environment. Vice versa,
when in the presence of a greedy competitive environment, they must limit app designers ’and
developers ’modiﬁcation of e-business models.
The deﬁ nition of what evolves and which are the interactors of the evolutionary process is the ﬁrst
step in transferring the outlined reasoning to the study of business models of other industries and/ortechnologies. To do that, scholars should be concerned with identifying: ( i)what are the business
model ’s elements that disappeared/appeared over time, ( ii)who are the major shapers of the inter-
dependent relationships among business models and their environment, and ( iii)how does their
power relationship change over time (because they produce di ﬀerent outputs, i.e. retained business
models ’features).
From that, entrepreneurs or e-business strategists in charge of de ﬁning the e-business model,
should consider that the successful features in the current digital era cannot be the same for thenext digital revolution; and from that, the analysis of the value sources, typologies, and value
capture schemes should continue over time and according to the type of e-business (website- or
app-based) they would like to conduct. Indeed, the results of this work show that running awebsite- or a app-based e-business requires considering di ﬀerent value sources, typologies, and
value capture schemes in order to survive the selection. Whoever is interested in proposing a newe-business, or in adapting it to not being a victim of digital Darwinism ,
 should take into high consider-
ation that content apps are the most di ﬀused ones (that also means being in heavy competition) and
that the design of the contents themselves, being eﬃ cient, creating a lock-in relationship with users,
and being able to add complementary monetisation mechanisms, are the sources of value to careabout. Yet, if the mobile app is o ﬀered for ‘free ’, value should be captured by In-App purchase or
ads, while if it is o ﬀered by paying a certain amount, then mixing other value capture mechanism
can help in increasing revenues. In sum, entrepreneurs or e-business strategists are recommended,for increasing their chances to survive and subsequently succeed, to design e-businesses oriented
to content-based apps and recur to di ﬀerent value capture schemes without relying on only one
of them for monetising the e-business.
