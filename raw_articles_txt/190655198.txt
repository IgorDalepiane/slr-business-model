Cross -organizational platforms with a purpose to enable the 
exchange of data about their patients face not only tech nical 
challenges but also challenges for the viability of the business 
model of their ecosystem. Regional healthcare information systems 
(RHIS) have been evaluated from many aspects, like utility, pro cess 
improvement, benefits to users and patients and cost savings. 
However these  evaluations  do not explain the problems in the 
viability of their ecosystems. This paper develops an ecosystem 
evaluation framework (EEF) based on platform theories and theory  
of constraints  (TOC) . It  demonstrates how EEF is used in 
evaluation of a RHIS to explain its problems in viability. EEF is 
composed of a model and a method. The model defines the 
ecosystem: The platform, the producers and consumers, other 
stakeholders, t he friction between the producers and consumers, 
the transactions reducing that friction, and the goals of the 
ecosystem and the goals of its stakeholders. The method applies 
following criteria: Does the platform serve its purpose in reducing 
the friction between producers and consumers, does the platform 
benefit every stakeholder in the ecosystem enabling them achieve 
more of their own goals, does the platform solve the chicken -and-
egg problem , reach the critical mass , create network effects  and 
does the improvement of the platform benefit every stakeholder. 
Using EEF in evaluation of RHIS reveals the inherent difficulties 
for digital business platforms, specifically when the ecosystem 
consists of both public and private organizations with mi smatching 
goals. In the future EEF helps to address these problems early on.  
I. INTRODUCTION  
The purpose  of c ross-organizational platforms for 
collecting, storing, sharing and managing patients´ health data 
is to enable  collaboration between service provider s. Regional 
healthcare information systems (RHIS) are platforms 
connecting electronic health  record  (EHR)  systems within 
primary and secondary care, both public and private  
organizations   [1] . Portals , like Active Life Home,  are 
platforms connecting personal device vendors and car e 
providers   [2] . These platforms have been evaluated   form 
various viewpoints like user satisfaction   [3] ,  usability   [4] , 
asses sment of impacts   [5] , cost savings   [6] and incentives  for 
collective action   [7]  . However, t he ado ption of the RHIS is 
still low  [4]  or the business model alignment is complicated   
[8] .  
Recent upsurge of digital platforms are promise d to change 
the ways how health care is provided and delivered to patients . 
For e xample , Philips, Apple  and Google are providing digital platforms  [9]  in healthcare.  However, as  [10]  say, digital 
platforms are not just technology, and their viability should not 
be assessed only from technological aspects but rather they 
represent a multisided platform as a business model  and their 
viability should be assessed from that perspect ive.    
The purpose of this paper is to develop an Ecosys tem 
Evaluation Framework (EEF) for understanding the chances of 
survival  of a digital business platform (RQ1).  We demonstrate 
the use of EEF in explaining the  problems in the business 
model  of the RH IS (RQ2). EEF should be able to explain the 
problems in the business model when doing a post -mortem 
analysis of an implemented platform. EEF is also useful if it 
can predict possible problems in a propose d platform . A lot of 
time and effort  can be saved  instead of building and 
implementing the platform just to see that its business model is 
broken.  
The knowledge contribution of this paper is an 
improvement on how to assess the viability of an digital 
business platform and its ecosystem.  
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 
explains the research method and the  main concepts of our  
justifi catory theories of Digital Business Platforms  and the 
Theory of Constraints  (TOC ). In section 3 we  design EEF. In 
section 4 we demonstrate how to use EEF in evaluation of 
RHIS. In section 5 we discuss the overall validity of EEF,  
limitations and suggestions for further research.   
II. RESEARCH METHOD  AND JUSTIFICA TORY THEORIES  
A. Design Science Research (DSR)  
This paper follows Design Science Research (DSR), as  
suggested by   [11] ,  [12] . DSR process includes the six steps   
[13] : problem identification and motivation; definition of the 
objectives for the solution; design and development  of the 
artifact as a proposed solution ; demonstration; evaluation; and 
communication.   
The introductory section already identified the problem, the 
need for the solution  and the objectives for the solution . The 
artifact , the solution is EEF , which is the outcome of the design 
and development step . We evaluate  the artifact  EEF by 
demonstrating how to use it in a case study .

B. Business Models, Digital Business Platforms and t heir 
Ecosystems  
According to Osterwalder  [14]  a business model describes 
the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers and 
captures value. Busine ss models often focus on a single 
company   [15] ,  [16]   and viability of  their revenue 
mechanisms  [17] , but our focus is in platforms.  
Platforms have many different definitions. Gawer  [18]  
presents a typology of platforms and identifies internal 
platforms, supply -chain platforms, industry platforms and 
multi -sided markets. Ecosystems rel ated to platforms and 
business  models were pioneered by Moore  [19] where he 
argues that a Business Ecosystem is a concept and unit of 
analysis helpful in business strategy and practice.  Tiwana  [20]  
defines the ecosystem from technology perspective as "the 
collection of the platform and the apps specific to it".  
For evaluation of the  viability of the  cross -organizational  
integration  platform we  look at the platform as a  business 
model pattern:  multi -sided business platform.  Multi -sided 
platforms bring together two or more distinct but 
interdepende nt groups of customers and create value by 
facilitating interactions between the different groups   [14] .The 
platform  and ecosystem architectures and economic s are  a 
topic of contemporary research   [21] ,  [22] ,  [23] . 
Our evaluation is focused  on certain type of multi -sided 
platfo rms: Digital Business Platforms, as shown in Fig. 1.   
The purpose of Digital Business Platforms is to  create value 
by enabling  value  exchange between two groups, t he producing 
peers and the consuming peers , resulting in a business 
transaction   [24]  , called the core transaction.  The transaction 
involves the transfer o f information, goods and/or services and 
money. The platform always transfers information whereas 
goods and/or services and money may or may not flow through 
it. The platform  has to capture some of the value in order to 
cover its costs and make some return .  
One category of platforms is an exchange platform  [10], 
where the transaction happens directly between the producer and the consumer, like in ordering a taxi or making a 
reservation. The platform uses its data to match the right 
consumer with the right producer. Another platform category is 
a content platform like Youtube, where the producer creates a 
piece of content which then can be consumed by several 
consumers. The platform collects the data and offers various 
search functions for the consumer s to find the right content.  
According to   [10] , multisided platforms create value by 
reducing frictions between producers and consumers. The 
platforms are more valuable  to all parties the more important 
are the frictions they address.  
According to  [10]  the core transaction is the set of actions 
producers and consumers must comp lete in order to exchange 
value. The four steps of the core transaction are:  
• Create: a producer creates value or makes it available 
to be consumed through the platform.  
• Connect: in every transaction, one user takes an action 
that sparks the exchange by con necting with another 
party  
• Consume: once consumers find the right match, they 
can consume the value created by the producer  
• Compensate: consumers create value for the producer 
in exchange what they consumed.  
According  to Choudary  [25]  the successful digital 
business platform has to  
• Solve the initial chicken -and-egg problem: to reach the 
critical mass of producers and consumers  
• Create network effects: to scale the platform value 
along with increasing user population   
•  Ensure a balanced surplus for all groups: to attract the 
platform stakeholders, including the platform provider.  
Examples of well known and often referenced successful 
platforms are  
• Alibaba, which connects the manufacturers and 
resellers of  goods  
• NASDAQ and other electronic exchanges, which 
connect sellers and buyers of  financial instruments  
• OpenTable, one of the first digital business platforms, 
which connects restaurants and those who need to 
make a reservation  
• Uber, which connects the drivers and those in need of a 
ride.  
• YouTube, which connects video clip producers, 
advertisers and those who watch video clips and 
advertisements  
• Facebook , which  started with people who want to 
interact with each other and extended to include the 
advertisers.  
These examples  represent both business to business (B2B) 
and business to consumers (B2C) models. In a context of a

C. Theory of Constraints  
TOC is a management practice and philosophy originally 
developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt  [26] . It helps us to focus 
our improvement efforts  into the constraints of the system and 
also helps us  to assess the impact of proposed technologi cal 
solutions  on the performance of the system   [27].  
TOC starts with the premise th at every system is built for a 
purpose, its global goal. A system is any collection of 
interconnected parts sharing a common goal. Every action to 
improve the system should be judged by its impact on the 
overall purpose. A constraint is anything that preve nts the 
system from achieving higher performance relative to its goal. 
When we want to improve the performance of a system, we 
need to focus in the constraints that se t the limits to the 
performance of that system.  
For the assessment of the impact on perfo rmance TOC 
proposes measurements  of throughput, inventory and operating 
expenses. Often throughput is more meaningful than cost 
savings.  Cost savings have a limit under which the necess ary 
conditions of providing quality products and services to 
customers  and satisfactory working environment for employees 
cannot be met. However, throughput has no upper limit. When 
assessing a proposed technology we need  look into its potential 
to improve the throughput  as the units of goal of the system . 
Improving throughput  implicit ly exploit s the constraints  [26] . 
III. ECOSYSTEM EVALUATION FRAMEWORK  (EEF)  
When planning or building digital business platforms the 
initial focus is often in technical questions like software 
architectures and interfaces. Securing the viability of the 
platform should also be addressed early on. For this purpose 
we design EEF.  It  has two components: a model of the 
ecosystem and the criteria for the evaluation  of its viability . 
A. The  model  
First we model the platform based in section II  B and fig. 1:  
1) Define the platform  
2) Define the problem that the platform is trying to reduce  
3) Defin e the purpose of the platform  
4) Define the ecosystem of the platform  
5) Define the transactions enabled by the platform  
6) Define the revenue model of the platform  
 
When defining the platform we describe both the technical 
aspects like information systems, but the focus is on business 
perspective lik e organizations, users and business processes.  B. The method  
We define the method as criteria  which need to assessed  in 
the evaluation process.  First we discuss the rationale for the 
criteria and then we list the criteria together.  
Based on TOC, the ecosystem  is built for a purpose, a 
common goal. The first criteria in our EEF is the ability of the 
ecosystem to achieve  its common goal . The platform  should  
reduce th e friction it is supposed to do.  
Every stakeholder has to benefit of  participating in the 
ecosystem . Without experiencing benefits, the stakeholder 
would rather drop off, and the viability of the ecosystem would 
be in danger . Building on TOC, a benefit for an organizational 
stakeholder is an improvement in its own performa nce. As 
second criteria  the transactions enabled by the platform should 
help each stakeholder t o achieve more of its own goal.  
Third criteria  involves  the critical success factors 
previously defined for the platforms.  Initially the platform has 
to solve t he chicken -and-egg problem: to get enough  producers 
and consumers on board so that the transaction s start to take 
place   [25] . Then successful ignition requires that  the platform 
attract s both sides  to reach the critical mass  [10] . By reaching 
the critical mass the ecosystem starts to create network effects, 
which can be cross -side or same -side  [20] .  
If the ecosystem is succes sful, it ignites and grows until it 
starts to reach th e full potential of the ecosystem, covering all 
potential users or facilitating all potential transactions.  
However, there is always need for improvements. The platform 
provider and the parties  should have an incentive to improve 
and further develop the platform.  The first version of the digital 
platform probably needs to be improved based on the feedback 
and the learning that happens in the ecosystem, as well as new 
technologies emerge.  Fourth  criteria for the viability is that 
each stakeholder benefits of improving the platform. It means 
that parties are prepared to invest in the improvements.   
After having evaluated the ecosystem using these criteria a 
summary of the viability of the ecosystem  is made .  
The list of the criteria:  
1) Does the platform serve its purpose   
2) Does the platform benefit every stakeholder in its 
 ecosystem?  
3)  Does the platform solve the chicken -and-egg problem, 
 reach the critical mass and create network effects?  
4) Does the continuous improvement of the platform 
 benefit every stakeholde r in its ecosystem?  
5) Is the the ecosystem viable?  
 
In building a digital business platform there are other 
critical issues to be solved. For example, from users´ 
perspective usability, privacy, security, from developers 
perspective the APIs and master data management and from 
operational perspective performance and reliability of 
computing and networking resources. Although  important, they 
are outside the scope of this paper.

IV.  DEMONSTRATION OF EEF  IN USE  
 We evaluate the utility of EEF by demonst rating its usage 
for assessing RHIS ecosystem (Fig. 2) . The first author of this 
paper was in charge of the design of the  technical  architecture 
of the RHIS used in the case study. Research data has been 
collected by interviews with various representatives of the 
participating organizations in Satakunta Macropilot project and 
later from various research reports and papers analysing th e 
experiences of using RHIS. Also usage data and reasons for 
discontinuing the use of RHIS has been received by interviews 
with representatives of Satakunta Hospital District.  
A. Define the platform and  the ecosystem.  
1) What is the platform?  
In this demonstra tion we look into a specific RHIS product. 
Its prototype was built in Satakunta Macro Pilot Project 2000 -
2002 and then commercialized and  put into production and 
used in Pirkanmaa, Satakunta and Varsinais -Suomi healthcare 
regions . The platform and the con nected systems are shown in 
fig. 2.  
2) The problem the platform is trying to reduce  
RHIS was introduced in Finland in the beginning of 2000 
as integration platforms to support seamless service chains. 
The problem the RHIS is trying to solve is that in healthcare 
organizational boundaries and electronic healthcare systems in 
silos create b arriers for exchange of patient data between 
service providers.  Certain medical conditions need the patient 
to be referred from primary care to secondary care and back 
again . Those patients  perhaps need also services from social 
sector. Not sharing patient  data at all or manually exchanging 
data may end up in duplication of activities like laborat ory and 
radiology examinations and diagnosis, unnecessary exchange 
of paper based health records, delays in flow of information, 
risks due to lack of information c ausing medication errors and 
faulty diagnosis, unnecessary costs and dissatisfaction of 
patients and emp loyees.  It is known that about 20% of the patients cause about 80% 
of the costs of public healthcare   [28] . But we do not know in 
advance who these ex pensive patients are or will be, so we 
need to collect the data for everyone. Also we know that about 
10% of the patients visiting primary care need services from 
secondary care. Again, we do not know in advance who they 
are, so we collect data for everyon e visiting primary care.  
3) The purpose of the platform  
The platforms  like RHIS are used to collect, store, share 
and manage patient´s health data, based on the conse nt of the 
patient.  Their purpose is to enable exchange of patient data 
across organizational  boundaries in order to support seamless 
flow of care services needed by the patient and to improve 
continuity of care. RHIS provides APIs to organizational 
systems like electronic health records, laboratories, imaging 
and others which are the producers of  patient data to the RHIS. 
RHIS offers a user interface which is used to consume the 
patient data  using standard web browser.  The category of RHIS 
is a content platform where the producer creates a piece of 
content which then can be consumed by several consumers.   
4) The ecosystem of the platform  
The producers and consumers of patient data are 
organizational actors and their employees.  Each organization 
can play either role  because in a healthcare setting available 
data about the patient is consumed and new  data is produced.   
Other stakeholders in the ecosystem are the patients and the 
home municipalities of the patients. The patients are the 
potential beneficiaries of the exchange of their medical data, 
although they are not directly viewing that data. Howe ver, they 
need to give their consent to use the data to the producer and 
consumer of the data. The home municipalities are those who 
pay for the care of the patient in Finnish public healthcare.  
5) The transactions enabled by the platform  
The purpose of RHIS  is to enable  the exchange of the data 
about the patient  immediately when the data is needed . The 
four steps of its core transaction are, based on the generic use 
case in interorgani zational infor mation process in fig.3.

The four steps of the core transaction   are: 
• Create: A producer, who is a healthcare professional in 
a participating healthcare organization, records the 
results of a hea lthcare activity in his sy stem, like 
electronic health record, for the organizations own use. 
The system sends the results to  RHIS  where they are 
available to be consumed by other organizations . 
• Connect: When a patient visit s a doctor in an 
organization which is connected to the RHIS, the 
doctor will ask if the patient has been treated in another 
connected organi zation, and then asks for the consent 
to access  the data.  There are also situations, where the 
patient is not prese nt, for example when the doctor in 
specialized care is looking through referrals from 
primary care and has to take a decision whether to 
schedule an appointment or just give a reply as an 
consultation. In this case the consent has been given in 
advance.  
• Consume: The doctor retrieves the patient data and 
uses it in the care service of the patient.  
• Compensate: Usually c onsumers create value for the 
producer in exchange what they consumed.  However, 
in  the case of the RHIS the producer i s not 
compensated direc tly by the consumer. Viewing the 
data is free for the consumer.  Indirectly the 
compensation happens when the consumers create new 
data and make it available to be consumed in new 
transactions.  The value to the platform comes from 
accumulating more data av ailable to be consumed.  
6) The revenue model of the platform  
The provider of the RHIS is a private company who se goal 
is to make money , now and in future . Several options were 
discussed  as the revenue model for RHIS provider . First option 
was to charge  the co nsumer of data  per usage of data . 
However, this option wa s rejected, because it was  considered 
to be discouraging  for the consumers to consume the data. An 
unintended cons equence would be that, t he most costly  patients 
with lots of data  made  available in RHIS would become even 
more costly  if each access to their data would be charged.    
Another option was to charge the producer of data per each 
unit of patient data they would create. However, also this 
option was  rejected. Only about 10% of patients visiti ng 
primary care would ever have their data accessed using RHIS 
so why would primary care have to pay for creating data for all 
their patients?  
The chosen option was a subscription model based on a 
fixed monthly fee. Each participating organization would p ay 
their share of the total cost . It was considered to encourage the 
use of the platform: the more transactions, the smaller would 
be the cost per transaction.  For practical arrangement  it was 
agreed that the healthcare district would pay the monthly cost 
to the platform provider and reimburse the cost from the 
municipalities in the service fees.  
B. Evaluation of the platform and its ecosystem  
1) Does the platform  serve its purpose?  The RHIS has been implemented in three healthcare 
districts. Pirkanmaa used  it from February 2004 until February 
2007. Satakunta used it from 2004 until 2014 and Varsinais -
Suomi  began in February 2006 and is still using it. The 
organizations have been from public sector only.   
The RHIS has been evaluated from several aspects.   
Mäenpää  [29]  evaluated five aspects: flow of information, 
collaboration, process redesign, system usability and 
organizational culture.  Nykänen   [5]  applied  following  
aspects: access to the system, how to search and retrieve the 
data, the speed of the system and the benefits for patient care.   
The results of the evaluations show that the RHIS works, it 
does what it is meant to do: enable the exchange of the patient 
data across organizational boundaries. Most important 
improvement compared to prev ious manual exchange of 
patient data is the immediate availability of the data  when  
needed. However, according to  [29]  continuity of care,  based 
on collaboration of organizations had some problems.  
Previously the flow of papers also coordinated the actions in 
the patients service chain. Without papers the patient 
himself/herself was responsible to coordinate the flow of 
services.  
Using EEF exposes the first critical factor in the ecosystem: 
there is no clear measure for the goal of the ecosystem. 
Improvement impacts achieved by use of RHIS, especially 
impact for system throughput, are difficult to assess without 
clearly defined mea sures . 
2) Does the platform benefit every stakeholder in its 
ecosystem?  
Based on TOC , a generic goal of any for -profit business 
organization is “to make money now well as in the future” .  
[30]  added two prerequisites, “without violating the necessary 
conditions of providing satisfying work environment for 
employees and ensuring customer satisfaction”.  
For the platform provider, which is a private company, we 
can use this goal for the evaluation. The more money the 
platform provider can charge, the more it achieves its goal.  
In public healthcare defining the goal is not so clear. We 
follow the approach by Ronen et al.   [31]   who propose that 
the goal of a publicly financed health organization is to 
“maximize quality healthcare services provided to its 
customers, subject to budgetary constraints”.   We use this 
definition in assessing how the platform improves the public 
healthcare organizations to achieve more of th eir goal.  
Based on the evaluation studies of RHIS  [29]  using the 
platform improves the quality of care through better clinical 
decisions, timely availab le data and better continuity of care 
through electronic discharge summaries   [32]  and medication 
documentation   [33]  .  Using the RHIS does not increase the 
unit costs of care. The number of users can be estimated and 
budget allocations made so the necessary condition of the goal 
is met.  
Also the pati ent as a beneficiary benefits of the use of the 
RHIS, although there are no clear measurement s in place for 
following the improvements from the customer perspective.

3)  Does the platform solve the chicken -and-egg problem, 
reach the critical mass and create n etwork effects?  
To get started RHIS needed enough users and data . The 
users should be consumers of data and producers of data from 
organizations who have the need to exchange data with each 
other. The typical service chain of a patient is from primary 
care to secondary care and back again. This is also a many -to-
one model within the region where many primary care health 
centers are served by one secondary care organization.  
Satakunta region started with connecting their secondary 
care EHR system to the RHI S so that all primary care doctors 
would have the opportunity to consume the data of their 
patients when the patients are discharged from secondary care. 
Also historical EHR records from secondary care were batch 
loaded to the RHIS to increase the value of  accessing RHIS. 
Gradually also primary care organizations started to produce 
the data to the RHIS to be consumed by the secondary care 
doctors.  
The number of users in Satakunta region  (population 
around 275 000 residents)  grew from about 300 up to 3700 
when almost all potential users had become registered users. In 
the end of 2011 there were 15 EHR and other patient data 
providing systems providing patient data into the RHIS. The 
number of data items in the RHIS was about 18 000 000 out of 
which about 16 000 items per month were accessed.  
Varsinais -Suomi  region (population around 470 000 
residents)  followed the Satakunta example  when later  starting 
the use of  the RHIS.  In the end of 2011 there were more than 
6000 registered users and 12 systems providing patient data. 
The number of data items in the RHIS was about 10 000 000 
out of which about 16 000 items per month were accessed.  
Pirkanmaa region (population around 500 000 residents) 
had a different start. They used RHIS from 2004 until February 
2006 when  it was discontinued   [5] . There were problems in 
connecting the patien t data providing systems into the RHIS  so 
paper based data exchange remained in use and the acceptance 
of RHIS did not reach the critical mass.   
Within Satakunta and Varsinais -Suomi regions practically 
all potential users had become registered users.  Network effects 
did not influence this to happen, because the organizations in 
the region had already made an administrative decision to use 
RHIS.  
From the RHIS provider point of view, only 2 regions out 
of total 20 regions in Finland became customers. On e reason 
might be that the need to exchange data between regions is 
very small and the regions can choose from several competing 
solutions to exchange patient data. Also the implementation of 
the national EHR archive (KanTa) started around 2005 so the 
mark et for RHIS did not see any  further  network effects  based 
on the number of regions as customers of RHIS . 
4) Does the improvement of the platform benefit every 
stakeholder in its ecosystem?  
Satakunta Region took the decision to discontinue using the 
RHIS on 20 14. The reasons behind the decision were that the 
RHIS was too difficult to use and it was too expensive. When 
analysing more closely the business model of the ecosystem, the deadlock becomes clear. To improve the usability of the 
platform, the RHIS provid er shou ld be able to increase its  
money flow to cover the invest ment. However, because the 
usage fee was a monthly fee per user and the potential to 
expand the user base was already limited the investment would 
not have resulted into increased revenues. Al so the option of 
increasing the mon thly fee per user was not possible, because it 
was already considered to be too much from the customers 
point of view, who were budget based public organizations. 
There was no simple solution to overcome this deadlock, 
ultimately caused by misalignment of goals of private and 
public organizations.  
Varsinais -Suomi region is still using the RHIS but also 
having discussions whether to continue or not.  
5) Summary of the evaluation  
In summary the evaluation of the RHIS we can conc lude:  
The RHIS as a platform serves its purpose partly: it enables 
the electronic exchange of patient data making it readily 
available when needed, thus reducing the friction of paper 
based patient data exchange. However it does not improve the 
continuity of care in improving the flow of services to the 
patient.  
The RHIS  improves the stakeholders to achieve more of 
their own goals. The primary care and secondary care service 
providers can make better clinical decisions and can save time 
when not having to  wait for the papers to carry the patient data. 
Also the RHIS provider makes money now, but its ability to 
make money in future is on risk.  
The RHIS failed to reach critical mass in Pirkanmaa region 
and its use was discontinued after three years. Satakunta  and 
Varsinais -Suomi region made a different start and the critical 
mass was reached.  
The network effects were seen: the platform became more 
valuable to the consumers of data, the more providers were 
connected to the platform. In the end most of the publi c 
healthcare organizations in the regions were connected to the 
platform and the full potential was achieved.  
The incentives to the ecosystem to improve the RHIS  were 
missing, because of the conflicting goals of the public 
producers and consumers of data a nd the private provider of 
the RHIS . In the end the use of  RHIS was discontinued in 
Satakunta region and is at risk in Varsinais -Suomi region.  
We could  ask how the revenue models should be changed 
to better align the goals of the organizations to enable 
investments needed to improve the RHIS. However, the 
purpose of this paper was to demonstrate the evaluation of the 
viability of the ecosystem.  We have shown that the viability is 
at risk because of mismatch in the goals of the stakeholders.  
V. DISCUSSION  
Evaluation of digital business platforms use often  criteria 
like validity, usability, effectiveness and cost savings . 
However, they  do not reveal the problems inherent in the 
business models of the ecosystems  around the platforms.

In this paper we combine  the platform theories and theory 
of constrain ts to design EEF. It is a novel method which helps 
to understand the possible problems in the business models 
when designing and implementing digital platforms. EEF can 
be applied in post -mortem evaluation for existing digital 
business platforms, and in early evaluation for proposed 
platforms. Early evaluation tests the viability of business model 
and gives a chance to mitigate the viability risks or to avoid 
poor investments entirely . 
Our demonstration of EEF u sage in RHIS case exposed a 
viability problem: mismatch in the goals of the platform users 
and platform provider .  
This finding leads also to more serious question of how to 
align goals of public organizations and private companies in 
ecosystems involving both types. Clearly expressing the goals 
of each stakeholder and having an agreement of how they can 
be best achieved could improve the viability of these types of 
ecosystems.  
The study has several limitations , which also would give 
avenues for further res earch .  
We have demonstrated the use of EEF in one case only. It 
would be important to see, how EEF could be used in other 
cases, perhaps outside healthcare industry.  
We use the concept of a goal of a system as an evaluation 
criteria. However, it is not a lways easy to find a proper metrics 
to measure the units of goal so that the impact of the platform 
could be assessed. This problem is not actually related to EEF 
but is more serious managerial problem: how c ould an 
organization be managed without clear me trics of its 
performance.
