Partial least squares structural equation model ABSTRACT  
Leveraging the Behavioral Reasoning Theory, in this paper we study the consumer ’s perception of 
the new subscription-based business models designed to give users access to a catalogue of 
smartphone apps. Specifically, we employ a PLS-SEM approach to study the data gathered on a 
sample of 294 mobile apps users in Italy. In particular, we have found that Global Motives are the 
main predictor of the consumers ’ behavioral intention and that context specific reasons, albeit 
differently, provide the linkages between the consumer values and the global motives without a 
direct effect on behavioral intention, partially confirming Westaby ’s theory.   
1.Introduction 
Business Model Innovation (BMI) - i.e. “the discovery of a fundamentally different business model in an existing business ” (Mar-
kides, 2006 , 20) - has become a hot topic in the innovation field (Afuah, 2014; Mao et al., 2020; Pang et al., 2019; Saebi et al., 2017; 
Schmidt and Scaringella, 2020 ). Several studies have shown that BMI is one of the main drivers to improve company performance 
(Gerdoçi et al., 2018; Lambert and Davidson, 2013; Zott et al., 2011 ), and it is one of the roots of many disruptive innovations 
(Chesbrough, 2010 ). According to some authors, BMI is about creating more value for the customers, so their perception of the new 
business model is a relevant factor in predicting their success (Afuah, 2014; Baden-Fuller and Haefliger, 2013; Clauss, 2017; Comes and 
Berniker, 2008; Jain, 2014; Johnson et al., 2008; Sanchez and Ricart, 2010 ). 
Several companies have introduced a BMI based on subscription-plans to access their services (McCarthy et al. 2017; Wang et al. 
2005 ) in several industries: entertainment and media (Burez and Van den Poel, 2007; Chyi, 2005; Laakso et al., 2016 ), web services 
(Enders et al., 2008; Horng, 2012 ), e-books (Hsiao and Chen, 2017 ) and mobile telecommunications (Lim et al., 2012 ). These 
subscription-based plans ask users to pay a fee to get access to a catalogue of services (such as movies, songs, books), instead of asking 
them to pay for each of them one by one. 
Mobile apps – i.e. software for mobile devices – have had a disruptive impact on the world economy, with more than 2 million apps 
in the Google ’s Play store and 1.83 million apps in Apple ’s AppStore in 2019, an expected global market bigger than 935 billion U.S. 
dollars in 2023 (Statista, 2019 ), and more than 6.3 billion users in 2021 (EPRS, 2018 ). 
Several authors have studied their adoption by consumers (Hew et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2017 ; Min et al., 2019 ) and the related 
business models (Arora et al., 2017; Dinsmore et al., 2016; 2017 ). At the same time there is still a lack of studies focused on the new

We aim to fill this gap, focusing our research on how consumers do perceive these new BMI, and on the factors driving their 
subscription intention. Consumer perception is a relevant topic in choosing the right way to market a BMI (Laukkanen, 2016; Rob-

We study these drivers with the lens of the Behavioral Reasoning Theory (Westaby, 2005a). This theory expands the widely used 
Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen, 1985, 1991) to factor in both the values and the believes, and the context-specific motivation 
underlying the Global Motives (i.e. attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control). Context-specific motivations are 
considered critical factors when consumers have to change their behavior (Snyder, 1992; Peterson and Simkins, 2019) as when they 
have to take out a subscription to get access to a catalogue of mobile apps. Moreover, comparative studies found that this extension of 
the TPB may help to provide a better explanation of the consumer behavior than the original Ajzen’s model (1985, 1991), as 
considering the context-specific motivations may help in tailor suiting the framework to the specific context (Westaby, et al., 2010). 
Our research model has been tested on a sample of 294 Italian mobile app users, that have been surveyed to understand if values 
and beliefs, and context specific motivations, are good predictors of the global motivation, and of adoption intention as well. 
The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we review the literature on Business model innovation, on innovation adoption, and 
on Behavioral Reasoning Theory; Section 3 has been used to outline the research hypotheses, while Section 4 is used to present the 
research methodology from the definition of the measures used in the research and the following data gathering procedure. The data 
analysis and the related results are presented in Section 5, while the Section 6 is used to identify some of the theoretical and managerial 
implications of our study and propose some hints for further research. Overall, our results partially confirm the BRT finding a positive 
effect of Values and Beliefs and Reasons, both for and against subscribing a plan to get access to a catalogue of apps, on the related 
global motives but only a mediated effect of these factors on the adoption intention. 
2.Literature review 
2.1. Business model innovation and the new subscription plans 
Business models are frameworks describing how businesses can deliver or capture value (Amit and Zott, 2001; Johnson et al., 2008; 
Teece, 2010; Zott and Amit, 2010). Some players may find relevant to explore new business models since they signal novelty and 
innovativeness to the other market players (Creusen and Schoormans, 2005; Kim et al., 2009). The new way of proposing and offering 
certain services – differently from the usual ones – is a significant element of differentiation from competitors (Negassi and Hung, 
2014) useful to increase consumer purchase intentions and, therefore, revenues (Chandran and Morwitz, 2005; Chernev, 2003; Kim 
et al., 2009). 
Business Model Innovation (BMI) is topical in the innovation research stream (Aspara et al., 2010; Lambert and Davidson, 2013; 
Markides, 2006). Afuah (2014) suggested that BMI is about doing things differently and changing the rules of the game (slightly or 
radically) to make money. On the same page, several previous studies have shown that business model innovation is a meaningful 
driver to improve company performance (Gerdoçi et al., 2018; Lambert and Davidson, 2013; Zott et al., 2011). 
According to Gambardella and McGahan (2010), a business model innovation occurs when a firm adopts a novel approach to 
commercializing its underlying assets. The innovation of business models is at the heart of disruptive innovation in organizations 
(Markides, 2006). According to several authors (Afuah, 2014; Voss and Zomerdijk, 2007; Wang et al., 2015) there is a business model 
innovation when there is a substantial change in the way in which revenues and profits are earned (for one or more businesses). 
Casadesus-Masanell and Zhu (2013) hold that BMI is mostly about finding new ways to create and capture value for the company’s 
various stakeholders. 
According to Cavalcante et al. (2011) there is a BMI when the management changes the business model the company operates in at 
least one of the following four directions: creating a new business model, extending an existing one, revising it, or terminating it. Amit 
and Zott (2012) focused on the company activities to identify three general models of BMI: adding new activities to an existing business 
model, organizing activities in novel ways, and changing some parties that perform the activities. 
BMI is often related to other forms of innovation (Spender et al.,2017). Several authors (Chesbrough, 2010; Richter, 2013) showed 
that a BMI is often needed to profit from disruptive technological innovations. According to Baden-Fuller and Haefliger (2013), a 
typical typology for BMI covers four fundamental dimensions: customer identification, customer engagement, value delivery, and 
monetization. 
In defining the BMI, knowing the customers, their habits, and how they behave, is a cornerstone (Wu et al., 2013). BMI is 
particularly useful in firms focused on the Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) to correctly define their value creation 
processes. 
In the last years, a subscription-based business model (Wang et al., 2005) where consumers periodically pay a fee to access a 
product/service, has quickly become wide-spread (McCarthy et al., 2017; Pauwels and Weiss, 2008). 
Subscription-based business models have been adopted by players in several web-based industries, such as Web 2.0 services 
(Horng, 2012), pay-TV (Burez and Van den Poel, 2007), journals (Laakso et al., 2016), social networking sites (Enders et al., 2008), 
online news (Chyi, 2005), e-book services (Hsiao and Chen, 2017) and mobile telecommunications (Lim et al., 2012). 
These subscription based business models have only been recently introduced in the mobile applications market where the main 
business models are premium - i.e., when the app is paid in order to install it on the mobile device – and freemium – i.e., when users can 
get the app for free, but they have to pay to get new features or to access new tiers of service - (Arora et al., 2017; Dinsmore et al., 
2017). Mobile Apps developers, also, may monetize their services (Scholz, 2017) displaying advertising within the app, or generating M. 2.2. Innovation adoption 
Over the years, many scholars have focused on studying the driver behind consumers’ innovation adoptions. They have been 
studied with several theories built on the Diffusion of Innovation Theory (DIT) (Al-Jabri and Sohail, 2012; Rogers, 1983), such as the 
Theory of Reasoned Action (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980; Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975, 2011; Liker and Sindi, 1997; Hale et al., 2002), the 
Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989; Kim, 2016; Venkatesh and Davis, 2000), and the Theory of Planned 
Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1985, 1991). 
According to the TPB (Ajzen, 1991) (see Fig. 1a), the behavioral intention – i.e., how much people are willing to perform the 
behavior - depends mostly on three main general factors. 
In this theory the main driver of the behavioral intention is the user’s attitude (Kotchen and Reiling, 2000) - i.e. “the degree to 
which a person has a favourable or unfavourable evaluation or appraisal of the behaviour in question” (Ajzen, 1991, 188), then the 
behaviour will be affected by the “perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform the behaviour” (Ajzen, 1991, 188), namely 
the user’s Subjective Norms (SN) – i.e. “a person’s perception that most people who are important to him think he should or shouldn’t 
perform the behavior in question” (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975, 302). Finally, there are the effects of the Perceived Behavioral Control 
(PBC) - i.e. the “subjective degree of control over performance of the behaviour itself” (Ajzen, 2002, 668). TPB has been used in various 
studies on innovation adoption (Hameed et al., 2012; Marcati et al., 2008; Perri and Corvello, 2015; Taylor and Todd, 1995; Weigel 
et al., 2014) even if it has been criticized as there’s a difference between behavioral intentions and the actual behavior (Foxall, 1997; 
2007; Morwitz et al., 2007; Sun and Morwitz, 2010), and not taking into account the personal norms and the affective evaluation of the 
behavior (Davies et al., 2002). 
In 2005(a), Westaby developed an extension of the TPB, i.e., the Behavioral Reasoning Theory (BRT) – see Fig. 1b – to account for 
the relevant role of reasons and beliefs in decision making processes already found in the Reasons Theory (Westaby and Fishbein, 
1996), and in the functional approach to motivation (Clary et al., 1998). 
BRT tries to have a more in-depth understanding of consumer decision-making by including context-specific reasons, which serve 
as important linkages between consumers’ values, global motives (namely the attitude, the subjective norms, and the perceived 
control), and behavioral intentions (Claudy et al., 2013; Peterson and Simkins, 2019). 
In the BRT framework, Westaby looks into the specific factors people leverage in explaining their own behavior as a source of 
insights on the attitude, the intentions, and the behavior as well (Westaby 2005a, 2016). In BRT, behavioral intentions can still be 
predicted by looking at Attitude, Subjective Norms, and Perceived Control as in the TPB, but these are seen at a higher level in 
accordance with Ajzen (2002) that may be influenced, or strengthened by the consumer reasons and his/her values and beliefs. Ac-
cording to Westaby, there are two main motivations to take into consideration the reasons behind a given behavior. 
First of all, Reasons are used to evaluate and compare the various behavioral alternatives (Bagozzi et al., 2003; Pennington and 
Hastie, 1993; Westaby and Fishbein, 1996) and they support decision making (Westaby, 2005a) so they are able to increase their 
commitment in the selected course of action; furthermore they are used by people to justify, to create a rationale, behind their actions 
(Westaby, 2005b; Westaby and Fishbein, 1996). 
In the BRT, Reasons are defined as “the specific subjective factors people use to explain their anticipated behavior” (Westaby 
2005a, 100). Reasons are not limited to the factors helping or hindering the behavior, or to a benefit/cost logic, but they include also a 
more general facilitator/constraint perspective to capture a wide array of specific factors in the full explanation set. 
Furthermore, the BRT see the reasons as dichotomous as the two classes are not seen as mere opposites (Westaby et al., 2010) but as 
two different dimensions that may be evaluated looking at different factors (Claudy et al., 2013; Gupta and Arora, 2017; Venkatesh 
et al., 2003) with different influences on the consumer/user behavior (Claudy et al., 2015; Ryan and Casidy, 2018). 
Both types of motivations, general, and context-specific ones, are influenced by the person’s beliefs – i.e “beliefs refer to a person’s 
subjective probability judgments concerning some discriminable aspect of his [or her] world” (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975, 131). Beliefs 
and values are a person’s subjective evaluation that the behavior could result in a wide array of outcomes in the future (Westaby, M. 3.Conceptual framework and hypotheses 
According to the BRT Framework (Westaby, 2005a) it is fundamental to consider the effects of both global motives and specific 
motivations (Reasons For/Against) to correctly predict the Adoption Intentions. 
Moreover, Reasons are used to predict the Global motives too, and Values may influence both Global Motives and the Reasons. 
In this paper we consider the division of Global Motives in each of their components – namely Attitude, Subjective Norms and 
Perceived Control – as each of them drive the adoption intention in a different way that may benefit, or hinder, the other two (Westaby 
et al., 2010). 
3.1. Global motives → Adoption intentions 
Our literature review has highlighted how Global Motives may be strong predictors of behavioral intentions (Westaby, 2005a). This 
link was already established in the DIT (Al-Jabri and Sohail, 2012; Rogers, 1983) and it was present even in other innovation adoption 
theories such as TRA (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975), and TPB (Ajzen, 1985). BRT classifies three main factors as global motives, i.e., 
Attitude, Subjective Norms, and Perceived Control, as they are “relatively broad substantive factors that consistently influence in-
tentions across diverse behavioral domains” (Westaby, 2005a, 99). 
Attitude represents the person’s global positive or negative evaluations toward doing the behavior and is often considered as a 
specific psychological tendency (Eagly and Chaiken, 1993) that are key determinants of consumers’ purchase decisions, thus people 
who hold more positive attitudes toward an innovation are more likely to adopt it (Bagozzi, 1992). 
Subjective Norms are the person’s perception of the influence other relevant parties have on driving him toward a specific behavior 
(Ajzen, 1991), and it has been often seen as a limit to the personal behavior (Boudon, 2003) coming out of the person’s perception of 
the others’ expectations (Ajzen, 1991; Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980). 
The last dimension is the Perceived Control that is focused on how the person perceives its control on the execution of the behavior 
and the relative ease or difficulty (Ajzen, 1991; Westaby, 2005a). 
According to Westaby et al. (2010), it is useful to test how each global motives’ subdimension – namely Attitude, Subjective Norms, 
and Perceived Control, may determine the behavioral intention, so we hypothesize that: 
Hypothesis 1.Consumers’ global motives toward subscription model - i.e., Attitude toward the behavior (H1a), Subjective Norms 
(H1b), and Perceived Control (H1c) - will positively influence their Adoption Intentions. 
3.2. Reasons→ Global motives, intentions 
According to the BRT, in order to predict the intention, global motives must be coupled with the context specific ones, i.e., the 
reasons for and against a given behavior. 
Westaby (2005a, 98) suggested that reasons “help individuals justify and defend their actions, which promotes and protects their 
self-worth”. Reasons are cognitions that could specifically influence a person’s behavioral explanation set (Westaby, 2005b). Several 
studies based on BRT show that context-specific reasons have impact on global motives, i.e., the attitude toward the behavior, sub-
jective norms, and perceived control (Claudy et al., 2015; Gupta and Arora, 2017; Westaby, 2005b). According to Claudy et al. (2015), 
people perceive in a different way the context specific motivations driving them to perform a given behavior, and those driving them to 
not perform it. Moreover, according to several authors (Claudy et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Westaby et al., 2010) the two kinds 
of Reasons may be evaluated looking at different factors. 
Hance, and considering the division of global motives in three separate sources of motivations (Westaby et al., 2010), we define the 
following hypotheses: 
Hypothesis 2.Consumers’ reason for subscription model adoption will positively influence their global motives - i.e., Attitude to-
ward the behavior (H2a), Subjective Norms (H2b), and Perceived Control (H2c) - toward subscription model adoption. 
Hypothesis 3.Consumers’ reason against subscription model adoption will negatively influence their global motives - i.e., Attitude 
toward the behavior (H3a), Subjective Norms (H3b), and Perceived Control (H3c) - toward subscription model adoption. 
At the same time according to the Westaby (2005a, 101), both the Reasons For, and the Reasons Against, will have a direct impact 
on the Adoption Intentions as “reasons can be powerful drivers of intention because people feel more comfortable with themselves 
when they have reasons to justify, and defend, their anticipated actions, even if their global motives are not perfectly aligned with their 
intentions”. It follows that Reasons may have a direct, not-mediated effect intentions. Accordingly, we have defined the following 
further sub hypotheses: 
Hypothesis 4a. Consumers’ reason for subscription model adoption will positively influence their Adoption intention of a sub-
scription plan.

3.3. Values → Reasons, global motives 
Schwartz (2006) suggests that values are broad motivational constructs as they play a key role in selecting and evaluating 
behavioral alternatives. In particular, according to Fishbein and Ajzen (1975) , values about the expected outcomes may have a strong 
influence on people ’s motivational process. BRT highlights that people ’s processing of their value information has a direct impact on 
the reasons people use to explain their anticipated behavior (Westaby, 2005a ). Moreover, according to Claudy et al. (2015) , people will 
be more prone to adopt an innovation when they see that their values are compatible with it. Accordingly, we have: 
Hypothesis 5.Consumers ’ values will positively influence their Reasons For subscription model adoption (H5a) and will negatively 
influence their Reasons Against subscription model adoption (H5b). 
In line with BRT, Values are expected to have direct and unmediated impact on Global Motives as they are used to orient the 
people ’s decision-making processes (Westaby, 2005a ). As Claudy et al. (2015, 533) suggested “consumers might choose a more 
heuristic or simplified decision-making process and decide to adopt an innovation without fully evaluating its benefits and costs. In 
other words, in some instances individuals form attitudes without rationally justifying their global motives. ” 
On these bases, we propose the following hypothesis: 
Hypothesis 6.Consumers ’ Values will positively influence their global motives - i.e. Attitude toward the behavior (H6a), Subjective 
Norms (H6b), and Perceived Control (H6c) - toward subscription model adoption.

4.Measures 
The present research adopts scales developed in extant research to measure most of the constructs included in the study. All the 
constructs were measured on a 7-points Likert Scale (ranging from 1strongly disagree, to 7strongly agree). 
We have measured the Adoption Intentions adapting a three-items scale of Behavioral Intention (Claudy et al., 2013; Fishbein and 
Ajzen, 1975; Gupta and Arora, 2017; Westaby et al., 2010 ). 
The three Global Motives (Attitude, Subjective Norms, and Perceived Control) were each assessed with three different indicators to 
take into account their specificity (Westaby, 2005a ) and we measured each of them adapting to subscription plans some measures 
regularly used in behavioral intention frameworks (Ajzen, 1991; Fishbein and Ajzen, 2011; Westaby, 2005a, Westaby et al., 2010 ). 
In line with previous studies (Claudy et al., 2013, 2015; Gupta and Arora, 2017; Westaby, 2005a; Westaby et al., 2010 ), we fol-
lowed an elicitation procedure with a focus group of mobile users to define a small set of context-specific reasons, both helping 
(Reasons For) and hindering (Reasons Against) the subscription to a catalogue to get access to mobile apps. 
For the focus group, we engaged 18 people (nine males and nine females) of different age groups selected to have user in three 
categories non-experts (those with fewer than 40 apps installed on their phones), users (with 40 to 80 apps on their phones), and 
enthusiast users (more than 80 apps installed on their phones). The meeting was started presenting them the main characteristics of the 
existing subscription plans (Google Play Pass, and Apple Arcade); then the participants were asked to list first, and discuss later 
(Fishbein and Ajzen, 2011 ), the main factors for and against adopting a general subscription plan to get access to smartphone apps. 
In the end, the focus groups identified as “reasons for” subscribing a plan to get access to mobile apps: to have access to a broader 
selection of apps (“I am free to try the apps I don’t know ”), to have the opportunity to share the same subscription among various

4.1. Data collection 
We have tested our research model on a sample of Italian mobile users. 
Italy is a vibrant country in the field of mobile applications, in fact, in 2018 over 32 million people use mobile devices (e.g. 
smartphones and tablets) every month to connect to the Internet and access to mobile applications - a growing number of users 
(“mobile surfer”) has installed many mobile apps (about 22) - and each incumbent company offers users about 4 Apps (on average) on 
the Play Store (Osservatorio Mobile, 2019). 
We collected the data with an anonymous online survey that we shared using both email and social networking sites (such as 
Facebook and LinkedIn). In order to avoid the social desirability bias the survey was introduced by a message explaining that it had 
been created for academic research, with no right or wrong answers, the message was repeated on the survey frontpage. The survey 
was shared with 637 smartphone users, 357 of them answered the survey (about 57% answer rate). 
After deleting the incomplete responses and the outliers, identified using a variant of the Mahalanobis distance (Leys et al., 2018), 
we studied the remaining 294 complete surveys. This model was tested for Common Method Bias using the full collinearity test and the 
higher VIF was 3.655 (RF on ATT) lower than the suggested value of 5 (Kock, 2015). 
The characteristics of the sample are reported in the following Table 1. Most participants are male (67%), on average respondents 
are between 30 and 39 years old (about 40%), and they have responded from all the Italian regions. 
5.Data analysis and results 
We have studied our framework with the Partial Least Squares approach to Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) (Hair et al., 
2011, 2014) using the packages plspm and SEMInR of R-CRAN (v.3.6.2). This approach has been successfully adopted in various 
business-related fields, such as: consumer behavior (Schubring et al., 2016); e-business (Chang et al., 2016), marketing (Reinartz et al., 
2004) and strategic management (Hair et al., 2012; Hulland, 1999; Sarkar et al., 2001). Moreover, this approach has been already used 
to test the BRT approach (Gupta and Arora, 2017; Nicholls and Schimmel, 2016; Sivathanu, 2018). 
This choice is also supported by several theoretical motivations. As our study is the first to date to study the subscription plan to get 
access to a catalogue of mobile app market, it should be considered an exploratory one; furthermore, the focus in our study has been 
put more on understanding how the various constructs are related more than on finding the best model to represent a given reality 
(Hair et al., 2011). The PLS approach is the preferred ones in both situations (Hair et al., 2014). 
Moreover, in our case, the PLS-SEM approach is the suggested approach as it does not rely on any distributional assumption on the 
measured variables (Henseler et al., 2009; Reinartz et al., 2009). 
Furthermore, the PLS-SEM approach has been found to be able to get at least as good results as the covariance-based one when the 
sample size is small, and when there are only few indicators for each latent (Hair et al., 2019; Reinartz et al., 2009). This is useful for 
our study as, as highlighted in the previous sections, some of the constructs are based on only three measured variables.

5.1. Step 1 – quality of the measurement model 
As our model is composed only by reflective indicators, the test of the measurement model looks at both model reliability and its 
validity. At the same time as the model is a fully reflective one, loadings are equally scaled covariances (Rigdon, 2012 ) so we do not 
have to check for multicollinearity. 
For the indicator reliability, we have checked that all the outer loadings are higher than 0.6 (Chin, 1998; Henseler et al., 2009 ), 
while for the internal consistence reliability we adopted three criteria:  
≡Each construct ’s Cronbach ’s Alpha is higher than 0.7 (Hair et al., 2011 ),  
≡Each construct ’s Dillon-Goldstein ’s Rho is higher than 0.7 (Chin, 1998 ), and  
≡Each construct ’s 1st eigenvector is higher than 1, while the 2nd one is lower than 1 (Sanchez, 2013 ). 
After testing the measurement model reliability, we tested the constructs validity looking at both the convergent validity – the 
extent to which a measure relates to other measures of the same phenomenon (Hair et al., 2017 ) – is assessed looking that the average 
variance extracted (AVE) of each block is higher than 0.50 (Hair et al., 2016). Then we looked at the discriminant validity of the 
constructs – that the various constructs does effectively represent different theoretical concepts – is verified using the cross-loading 
approach (Ravand and Baghaei, 2016 ) – an indicator ’s loading with its associated latent construct should be higher than its load-
ings with all the remaining constructs (i.e., the cross loadings). 
The results of the first three assessments are reported in the following Table 2, while the results for the test of the discriminant 
validity are reported in the following Table 3. 
In our first test, we found that PC4 (“There are subscription plan available for my smartphone ”) outer loading on Perceived Control 
was slightly lower than 0.58 so, following the approach defined by several authors (Hair et al., 2016; Sanchez, 2013 ) we decided to 
leave it out of the model as prescribed by several authors and to repeat the tests. The new model passed the four tests as shown in both 
tables. 
As the new model was able to pass the four tests, we have a valid measurement model, and we could assess the structural model to 
test the research framework hypotheses.

To evaluate the hypotheses of this study, we adopted a bootstrap procedure with 5.000 resamples (Hair et al., 2016, 2019; 
Streukens and Leroi-Werelds, 2016 ) to estimate the path-coefficients and their confidence interval. The results of the bootstrapping are 
reported in Table 4 and in Fig. 3. 
As Table 4 points out, most of the hypotheses in our model are verified, with the only exception of the effect of the context specific 
motivations (both Reasons For, and Reasons Against) on the Adoption Intention, namely Hypotheses 4a and 4b. These results imply 
that the total effects of the context-specific motivation on the Adoption Intention (0.263 for the Reasons For, and 0.216 for the Reasons 
Against – both significant with a p-value D.1) are fully mediated through the three subdimensions of the Global Motives. 
Looking at the influences of Values, we have a stronger effect on the context-specific motivations (respectively the path is 0.718 for 
the Reasons For, and  0662 for the Reasons Against) and a lower one for the three dimensions of the Global Motives. 
Looking at the Reasons For, the differences on the three Global Motives are not statistically significant considering that the path 
coefficients ’ confidence intervals do overlap. The same consideration holds for the Reasons Against as well. 
Finally, our results for the General Motives, show that the strongest effect on the adoption intention is related to the Perceived 
Control. Furthermore, the effect of Perceived Control on Adoption intention is significantly higher than the one from the Subjective 
Norms, while Attitude is not statistically different from both of them as shown confronting the path coefficients ’ confidence intervals as 
done before. 
6.Discussion and conclusion 
The app landscape has been evolving rapidly in the last few years (Feng and Yu, 2020; Scholz, 2017 ). Several studies in this field 
have shed some light on different business models and on the main consumers ’ behaviors (Arora et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2017; 
Dinsmore et al., 2016, 2017; Jang and Chung, 2015 ). However, the recent introduction of the subscription-based business model could 
have disruptive impacts as it has already done in other industries (such as music and movies streaming services, or the videogames 
subscriptions plans), so there is an ongoing call for research contributing to understanding the developments of the apps and the 
related innovations. 
In this paper, we study for the first time – to the best of our knowledge – the main reasons affecting the consumer ’s interest in the 
new subscription-plans for mobile applications with an approach based on the BRT. 
This research contributes to extend literature on BMI by providing some insights on the new subscription-based business models 
when applied to the apps. The study focuses on the consumer perspective using the lens of the BRT to improve the understanding of this 
phenomenon thus offering future research opportunities. 
The study advances the extant theoretical knowledge in several respects. 
First, it contributes to the nascent mobile applications ’ literature, and specifically we focus on the consumer ’s adoption of a new 
business model, taking out a subscription to a catalogue of mobile apps, that even if it is still unavailable in many countries, it has 
already successfully disrupted other industries (e.g. Music, Videogames, and Books). 
Second, this research shows the importance to consider the consumer ’s values and his/hers context-specific motivations (Reasons 
For, and Reasons Against) influence on Global Motives confirming previous behavioral intention studies (Claudy et al., 2013, 2015; 
Westaby et al., 2010 ). 
According to our findings, in the context of subscription plans to get access to a catalogue of mobile applications the “reasons ” are 
mostly used to justify the user’s Global Motives (attitude, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control), as the relationship 
between “reasons ” and “intention ” are both not significant highlighting only a partial support of original BRT model (Westaby, 2005a ) 
as, we have found no evidence for a direct effect of the context-specific motivation on adoption intention (Westaby, 2005a ). Our results 
seem to provide further support to some studies (Norman et al., 2012; Claudy et al., 2015 ; Tani et al., 2021 ) that have only partially 
supported the idea that both Reasons For and Reasons Against have a direct link on the behavioral intention, often finding that only 
one of the constructs had a direct effect. 
One possible explanation for this result may be related to the exploratory nature of our study. As the subscription plans to get access 
to apps are still not available in most countries and in particular in the specific context of our research, Italy, they were still fairly new, 
most people may have not really already considered to take out such a subscription. This explanation would be coherent with 
Westaby ’s original model (2005a) as context-specific motivations are seen to justify an already chosen behavior, and these plans may 
be too new for that. Therefore, we have found only their effect on global motives; at the same time, we hold that these speculations 
should be tested again in the future after these plans have become more widespread. 
Furthermore, our study confirms the idea that Reasons For and Reasons Against are not mere opposites (Claudy et al., 2015 ; Tani 
et al., 2021 ) as confirmed by the results of the test for the measurement model validity (both convergent and divergent ones). This 
finding support Westaby (2005a) model supporting a perspective where Reasons For and Reasons Against are seen as two broad 
sub-dimensions with different effects and different relationships with Global Motives. 
Our results highlight several, interesting, managerial implications. 
All the elicited Reasons For and Reasons Against have confirmed as relevant predictors of the Global Motives. 
Accordingly, managers should try to leverage these new subscription-based plans in their strategies engaging a broad set of app- 
developers in order to offer a broad catalogue to help users in choosing freely which ones they want to try without having to pay for 
each of them and without having their experience interrupted by ads. 
The results hint that managers should implement policies to ease the users fear to be unable to access the apps they normally use as 
some of them may not be included in the catalogue or, they may be excluded at some later moment in time. Implementing policies to M. These results will help mangers to design their strategies and increase the use of subscription-based plans more customized and

view and, therefore, play a relevant role in the growth and development of the mobile app market. Given its importance in the current 
economies, it will be fundamental for companies and the main players of the ecosystem to support the rapid spread of these new plans 
and support their development. 
At the same time, as this study is the first to target the perception consumers have of the subscription plans for smartphone apps, it 
suffers from some limitations. Above all this limits our opportunity to compare our results with other similar studies in the same field. 
For the same reasons we have chosen to provide the Gaston-Sanches Global Fit Index in order to help further studies to compare with 
this one. 
Our model gives strong evidence on the advantages of adopting the BRT framework to understand the consumers’ intention to 
subscribe a fixed-price plan to get access to a smoother user experience in their mobile apps consumption; but we have got only a 
limited number of context-specific motivations for, or against, this behavior, probably limiting the potential predicting power of these 
two factors in our study. On the same page we have decided not to use some “values” measure often used in literature, such as Openness 
to Change (Schwartz, 2006), as they were never used in this specific field before. 
Another limitation is linked to the specific moment in time; as in Italy these plans were new at the moment of the data gathering 
process, we have to acknowledge the respondent did not have a direct experience with them so their perspective could be somewhat 
biased driving us to suggest repeating this whole research in the future in order to investigate the actual behavior as well. 
Finally, the final sample is made up of about 300 people in a single country. Both these aspects represent a strong opportunity for 
future research. In fact, we can replicate the questionnaire to a larger number of consumers through several other channels, e.g. 
additional social networks (such as Instagram, Twitter or even tech-oriented websites) or administer it to specific classes of subjects 
such as students, and we can conduct the survey also through other countries in order to compare differences as well as obtain a 
confirmation of our results.
