We interpret mobile operating systems such as iOS and Android as multi-sided platforms
that serve a variety of “customers, ”including consumers (users), handset makers, network
operators, app developers, advertisers, and chip manufacturers. After reconstructing the
history of smartphones, we explore the variety of business models that operating systemowners or sponsors have implemented, and highlight their implications for the relation-
ships between platform owners/sponsors and customers. Next we focus on those
dimensions of the smartphone experience that consumers declare they value the most,namely network quality, the features of the operating system itself, and the selection of
apps available for each platform. We study how different mobile operating-system owners
or sponsors have managed to innovate in these various dimensions, often by engagingother customer types. In the concluding section we rely on the insights derived frommulti-sided platform theory to analyze the reasons why two mobile platforms —Apple's
and Google's —have been able to displace various incumbents.

1. Introduction
In recent years a rich academic literature has developed on markets with multi-sided platforms. These are platforms that
connect two or more distinct types of customers and that are characterized by the existence of indirect externalities —an
extra customer on one side generates benefits for customers on the other side. Multi-sided platform models have been
applied to a wide variety of markets, including real estate brokerage and matchmaking. Among the empirical studies of
multi-sided platforms are Rysman (2004) on the Yellow Pages directories, Kaiser and Wright (2006) on German magazines,
Landsman and Stremersch (2011) on game consoles, and Seamans and Zhu (2012) on the impact of Craigslist on newspapers.
In this study we apply the multi-sided platform model to understand the evolution of the smartphone industry. We focus
on smartphone operating systems, such as Apple's iOS and Google ’s Android, as multi-sided platforms that connect different
types of customers that demand the services of the platform, including users, application developers, handset manufac-
turers, network operators, advertisers, and even chip makers. (We refer to the operating systems that are at the core of our
study as “mobile operating systems, ”“smartphone operating systems, ”or simply “operating systems. ”)
Even though by their very nature mobile operating systems connect a variety of customers, their ultimate goal is to
attract consumers (users). Thus, we focus on the strategies that mobile platforms have resorted to in order to achieve thisContents lists available at ScienceDirect

We attempt to provide insights not only on how various mobile platforms addressed the take-off problem but also on the
strategies they have adopted to sustain momentum with users —strategies that usually involve interactions with other
customer types.

2. Historical background
The mobile phone emerged as a consumer item in the early 1990s, in effect riding on the technology and infrastructure
that had been developed earlier to satisfy the corporate communications market and elite customers. The second half of the
1990s saw a wave of new single-function consumer devices, such as media players, digital cameras, and GPS systems, and
infrastructure to support them. For a small increment to manufacturing cost, it became feasible to incorporate audio players
and digital cameras into the mobile phone, which thus became a “feature phone ”—an integrated bundle of special-purpose

2.1. The smartphone pioneers
The term “smartphone ”came into use in 1997 and represented a new way of thinking about mobile phones.
Smartphones were no longer single-purpose or multi-purpose devices, but rather universal handheld computers that
incorporated a telephone. A key feature of smartphones was the ability to run software programs, later called “apps, ”that
enabled them to perform tasks that had not been envisaged when the phone was manufactured. Smartphones were, at their
core, classical universal computers.
The smartphones that became popular in the 21st century were built on the technology of handheld computers, often
called Personal Digital Assistants (PDAs), developed within the computer industry. All of the early smartphones had at their
core software operating systems devised for PDAs. The main ones in the pre-2007 period were Palm OS, Windows CE (later
called Windows Mobile), Symbian, and BlackBerry OS.
Palm Computing was founded in 1992 by Jeff Hawkins, who in the early 1990s had created one of the first handheld
computers, the GRiDPad ( Jackson, 2001 ). After being acquired by U.S. Robotics, in 1996 Palm introduced the PalmPilot 1000
PDA, which had 128k of memory and a monochrome touchscreen display. Designed to replace paper rather than full-fledged
computers, the PalmPilot 1000 had four basic functions —calendar, addresses, to-do lists, and memos —and sold more than a
million units in the first 18 months after introduction. The operating system that controlled the PalmPilot PDA, the Palm OS,
was first used in a smartphone in 2001.
The success of the PalmPilot 1000 generated a flurry of entry into the PDA market. Microsoft had started working on an
operating system for mobile computing devices, codename Pegasus, in late 1994 ( Tilley, 2001 ). By September 1996 it had
entered into agreements with six hardware partners —Casio, Compaq, HP, LG Electronics (for Hitachi), NEC, and Philips —to
bring to market PDA-like devices running on the operating system (renamed Windows CE 1.0). These early Windows-based
handheld computers were not particularly successful. By late 1997 Palm had about two-thirds of the market for handheld
computing devices, and the Windows mobile OS trailed considerably behind. Smartphones based on Windows CE were not
introduced until 2002.
Symbian has its roots in an operating system named Epoc developed by Psion, a British computer company. Psion had
been founded in 1980 by David Potter, an academic physicist, and in its early years had become a leader in software for
microcomputers. In 1994 Psion introduced a small handheld computer, the Series 3, which was a hit in the British market
(Orlowski, 2010 ). After the Series 3 took off, Psion engineers started designing an operating system, called Epoc, which
would serve as the foundation for the long-term successor to the Series 3. While exploring the possibility of licensing its yet
unreleased operating system, Psion established a relationship with Nokia, the Finnish conglomerate, which was about to
introduce the Communicator 9000, a mobile-phone and PDA combo. Negotiations between the companies led to an
agreement according to which Nokia would use the Epoc operating system on its future line of Communicator devices.
In the summer of 1997 Psion released the first devices based on the Epoc operating system. Although the Epoc handheld
computers were widely held to be superior to those running Windows CE, Psion ’s top management was inclined to license
Windows CE for the Psion computers in order to avoid a David-versus-Goliath confrontation with Microsoft. In this context,
one of Psion ’s managers suggested the idea of creating a spin-off company that would own the Epoc operating system and
would be owned by some of the key mobile-phone industry players. The company, named Symbian Ltd., was founded in
June 1998, and was jointly owned by Nokia, Ericsson, and Motorola. The Symbian operating system was used on a mobile
phone for the first time in 2001.
In 1984 two engineering students, Mike Lazaridis and Douglas Fregin, founded Research in Motion (RIM) as an
electronics and computer-science consulting business in Waterloo, Ontario. Early on the firm specialized in wireless data
technology, and by 1988 had become the first company outside Scandinavia to develop connectivity products for Mobitex
data communication networks ( McQueen, 2010 , pp. 19 –77;Sweeney, 2009 , pp. 1 –78).
In 1996 RIM introduced the first two-way messaging pager, the so-called Inter@ctive Pager, and in 1999 the first product
in a line that would bring the company international recognition —the BlackBerry, a device that allowed business executives
to access their corporate email wirelessly from anywhere at any time. RIM ’s first smartphone, the BlackBerry 5810, reached
the market in 2002. A mobile phone that included enterprise e-mail support, text messaging, and a browser, the 5810 has
been dubbed “the first truly successful smartphone ”(Ahmad, 2011 , p. 16).
2.2. Apple ’s iPhone
The smartphone market changed radically with the arrival of Apple ’s iPhone in June 2007 ( Vogelstein, 2008 ). Apple ’s
management had started exploring the development of an Apple phone in 2002, soon after the introduction of the iPod MP3
player. One of the key problems consumers were facing in the early 2000s, and especially after the introduction of the iPod,
was mobile device proliferation —they were carrying simply too many of them, including a PDA, a phone, an MP3 player, and
sometimes a digital camera.
By 2004 the iPod business, which already accounted for 16 percent of Apple ’s revenues, looked promising but also
vulnerable —mobile phones were becoming increasingly sophisticated and the price of storage was plunging. Further, music
stores rivaling Apple ’s iTunes, which had opened for business in April 2003, were proliferating. Apple responded with a first
“music phone, ”the ROKR, jointly developed with Motorola and introduced in 2005. The ROKR —which had an unfriendlyM. Campbell-Kelly et al. / Telecommunications Policy 39 (2015) 717 –734 719

As the ROKR episode was unfolding, Apple established a business relationship with Cingular, the wireless carrier. Apple
and Cingular —which was acquired by AT&T in December 2006 —held protracted negotiations that led to a business deal
centered on the introduction of an Apple phone, internally known as Purple 2. Apple ’s iPhone, developed in an atmosphere
of complete secrecy during 2005 –2006, was announced by Steve Jobs in January 2007. It became commercially available a
few months later.
Jobs presented the iPhone as a “revolutionary mobile phone ”that combined the capabilities of a phone, an iPod, and an
“Internet communicator ”(Thomas, 2007 ). The entire front surface of the iPhone was a touchscreen and all of its functions
were activated by touch. It ran the Mac OS X operating system (later rebranded iOS when used on the iPhone), offered a
radically new and improved Internet access capability, and was able to wirelessly download music and movies from the
iTunes store.
In the second quarter of 2007, when the iPhone became commercially available, Symbian was the undisputed leader in
the world smartphone market with a share of almost 66 percent of all units sold ( Gartner Research, 2008 ;Markoff, 2007 ).
The iPhone share grew quickly, from less than one percent when it was introduced to 16.13 percent in Q4-2009. The rise of
the iPhone was accompanied by a steep decline in the shares of both Symbian and Windows CE. The share of the Blackberry
OS kept on growing through late 2009, mainly because the Blackberry was less of a consumer item and was embedded in
many organizations.
2.3. Google ’s Android
By late 2008 the iPhone was well on its way to becoming an important player in the world smartphone market. Its
ascent, however, did not remain uncontested for long. In September 2008T-Mobile released the first smartphone running on
Android, the operating system developed by Google ( Lendino, 2012 ).
The history of Google ’s Android goes back to Andy Rubin, a computer and electronics hobbyist who had worked for Apple
and WebTV ( Markoff, 2007 ). In the late 1990s Rubin and a group of engineer friends had founded a start-up in Palo Alto,
California, named Danger Inc, which in the early 2000s developed the Sidekick, one of the first smartphones to effectively
combine Web access, email, instant messaging and other applications.
The Sidekick developed a cult following among hip teenagers but never became a contender in the broader smartphone
market. Rubin left Danger Inc in 2004 and launched a new startup, Android, whose goal was to design “a free, open source
mobile platform that any coder could write for and any handset maker could install ”(Roth, 2008 ). The company would
generate revenues by selling support services for the system. In 2005 Rubin approached Page to obtain Google ’s
endorsement for Android. Rather than just endorsing the start-up Google acquired it in July 2005 for an estimated $50
million. This unleashed a flurry of speculation in the technology world about the potential implications of Google ’s turning
into a smartphone vendor.
At the time when it acquired Android, Google was reportedly concerned about two facts: first, web surfing had started to
migrate from personal computers to mobile phones, and second, the Internet capabilities of existing smartphones were
limited ( Roth, 2008 ). Consumers ’migration toward the mobile web could potentially undermine the foundations of Google ’s
success precisely because mobile phones were not particularly effective platforms for searching the Internet (and viewing
Google ’s ads).
Android ’s acquisition by Google did not turn Google into a smartphone vendor, at least not immediately. Contrary to
Apple, which sold the iPhone as an integrated and proprietary bundle of hardware and software, Google chose to follow a
strategy not unlike the one that Microsoft had employed in the 1980s in its relationships with PC makers —Google developed
an operating system and licensed it to any and all handset makers. The key difference between Google ’s Android and
Microsoft ’s DOS and Windows was that Android was open-source and cost the handset manufacturers nothing.
In November 2007, only a few months after the iPhone had reached the market, a group of technology companies that
included Google, T-Mobile, HTC, Qualcomm, and Motorola announced the formation of the Open Handset Alliance for thedevelopment of the Android operating system ( Schonfeld, 2007 ). The first Android smartphone, introduced in September
2008 by T-Mobile, was reviewed poorly and did not sell well ( Vogelstein, 2011 ). The first successful Android smartphone
was Motorola ’s DROID, which reached the market in October 2009 ( McCracken, 2009 ).
The share of Android in the world smartphone market grew quickly after Q4-2009. By Q1-2011 Android had become the
leading operating system in the world market with a 35.99 percent share of all units sold, followed by Symbian (27.39
percent), Apple ’s iOS (16.75 percent), the BlackBerry OS (12.90 percent), and Windows Mobile (3.63 percent).

2.5. Changes in consumer behavior
The introduction of Apple ’s iPhone and Google ’s Android led to dramatic changes in behavior among consumers. When
Apple ’s iPhone was introduced in mid-2007 about 78 percent of individuals who had a landline in the U.S. also had a mobile
phone ( PewResearchCenter, 2011 ). We do not have information on the proportion of mobile-phone owners who had a
smartphone at the time but it is usually assumed that it was extremely limited. By May 2011, 33 percent of mobile phone
owners had a smartphone and by September 2012 the share had risen to 53 percent —that is, by mid to late 2012 more than
half of all mobile phone owners in the U.S. owned a smartphone (as opposed to a traditional feature phone)
(PewResearchCenter, 2012 ,2013 ). Other sources provide further details on mobile phone usage. By mid-2012 about 94
percent of U.S. consumers had a mobile phone; among them, 53 percent used a smartphone, 38 percent a feature phone,
and 9 percent a multimedia phone (that is, one that had a touch-screen and a qwerty keypad but not an advanced operating
system) ( Nielsen, 2013 , p. 9).

3. Mobile operating systems as multi-sided platforms
We view mobile operating systems such as iOS and Android as multi-sided platforms. The multi-sided model has been
applied to a variety of industries, including real estate brokerage, newspapers and magazines, network television, computer
operating systems, and electronic payment instruments.
3.1. Features of multi-sided platforms
Multi-sided platforms have three fundamental features ( Evans, 2003 , pp. 327 –334).1First, for multi-sided platforms to
emerge at least two distinct types of customers have to exist. Second, in multi-sided platforms there are indirect
externalities arising from the interconnection among different customers types. Third, an intermediary is required to
internalize the externalities flowing from one side of the platform to the other.
Mobile operating systems are multi-sided platforms that clearly meet these three conditions. First, there are a variety of
customers, including users, handset makers (such as Samsung and Nokia), network operators (such as AT&T, T-Mobile, and
Verizon), advertisers, chip makers, and application developers (see Fig. 1 ). Second, there are externalities arising from the
interconnection among different customer types. The most obvious of such (indirect) externalities are those flowing from
users to app developers, and reciprocally —a developer that starts creating apps for a mobile operating system makes users
of the operating system better off, and an additional user of a mobile operating system makes developers engaged in
creating apps for it better off. Third, the mobile operating system is the platform that mediates the relationships among
these various customer types.
Multi-sided platforms have a number of features that make them different from more traditional businesses and
markets. A key characteristic of every multi-sided platform is that at the starting point it faces a chicken-and-egg problem:
in a platform with two customer types (say, Type-I and Type-II), Type-I customers do not want to commit to participating in
the platform until they see enough commitment on the part of Type-II customers, who in turn refuse to join until they see a
sufficiently large number of Type-I customers on board. Multi-sided platforms have to be creative enough to solve this
conundrum; if not, they never achieve the kind of scale that allows them to take off.
The evolution of markets with multi-sided platforms tends to be volatile and unstable. This is so because of the
interactions between the demands on the various sides of the platform —since demands are interconnected, small changes

3.2. Business models
Even though all mobile operating systems are multi-sided platforms, not all of them have structured their business
model in the same way. One of the key distinctions among them is that, in some cases, the creators of the operating system
have chosen to be the handset makers as well, whereas in others those functions have remained distinct. We identify five
different business models that have developed historically in the mobile-operating-system market.
The first model is one in which the mobile operating system is closed-source, and its owner is a corporation that
functions also as the handset maker. Put differently, the corporation that owns the proprietary operating system sells an
integrated bundle of software and hardware. This is the approach adopted by RIM for BlackBerry OS, Palm, and Apple
for iOS.
The second model —adopted by Microsoft for Windows Mobile —is one in which the mobile operating system is closed-
source, and its owner is a corporation that licenses it to handset makers for a price. The key distinction here is that the
operating system ’s owner does not supply the hardware and is thus forced to interact with another set of partners (or
customers), the handset makers.
The third model —implemented by Symbian Ltd. —is one in which the mobile operating system is closed-source, and its
owner is a consortium or joint venture of handset makers that licenses it for a price. This model is somewhere in between
the first and the second in that some of the owners of the proprietary operating system do supply the hardware as well, but
at the same time the system is licensed to handset makers other than its co-owners.
The fourth model —adopted by Google for Android —is one in which the mobile operating system is open-source and is
licensed to handset makers for free. In addition, its evolution is dictated by an alliance of companies that includes its main
developer —Google —and a variety of partners (or platform customers) from different areas of the multi-sided platform
(handset makers, application developers, network operators, and even chip makers).
The fifth, and final, model is the one adopted by the Symbian Foundation. In this case the operating system is open-

handset makers, chip makers, and at least one network operator.
3.3. Addressing the chicken-and-egg problem
As pointed out earlier, one of the key problems multi-sided platforms face is how to attract enough customers of
different types at the starting point ( Caillaud & Jullien, 2003 ). Historical examples of markets with multi-sided platforms
show that platforms frequently focus on attracting a sufficiently large number of a certain kind of customer in the hope that
customers on the other side will follow.
When Bank of America launched its credit card program in California in 1958, for example, every Bank of America
customer in Fresno, CA, received a card, regardless of creditworthiness. This experiment —a mass-mailing of 60,000 cards in
a specific location —was the bank ’s approach to solving the chicken-and-egg problem, since merchants in the area were
reluctant to join the program before seeing enough customers carrying the card ( Evans & Schmalensee, 2005 , p. 57).Mobile

As multi-sided platforms, mobile operating systems have faced the chicken-and-egg problem. Apple and BlackBerry, we
argue, focused on first attracting enough users with features, whereas Google, which entered an already crowded market,
chose to attract consumers indirectly by first attracting handset manufacturers and network operators. Google also attracted
app developers by creating an open-source operating system for which it was relatively easy to develop software.
Both RIM and Apple implemented a business model in which the owner of the operating system is also the handset
maker. Thus, for them attracting handset makers to the platform at the starting point was never an issue. RIM and Apple
achieved success by marketing an integrated bundle of hardware and software that, at the starting point, was particularly
effective at attracting consumers. The key to BlackBerry ’s success was supplying one feature —seamless access to corporate
email —that drew large numbers of users, especially business users ( Ahmad, 2011 , pp. 13 –18). Similarly, what propelled the
iPhone ’s lift off was a unique combination of highly innovative features: a touch-screen interface, a mature Internet
experience through a “real, computer-grade Web browser, ”a full-blown iPod music player, and impressive handset
hardware that included the largest and highest-resolution screen of any smartphone in the marketplace ( Mossberg &
Boehret, 2007a ,2007b ;West & Mace, 2010 ). Both RIM and Apple launched their multi-sided platforms by focusing initially
on getting consumers on board.
In fact, when Apple introduced the original iPhone in mid-2007 it did so exclusively through one network carrier —
Cingular, wholly owned by AT&T at the time ( Honan, 2007 ). The fact that Apple did not make agreements to have the iPhone
available in as many networks as possible reveals the company ’s conviction that it could draw consumers to the iPhone (and
to the Cingular network) on the basis of the iPhone features only. Further, no third-party applications were present on the
iPhone until the introduction of the iPhone 3G in mid-2008, which means that no attempt was made to attract consumers
on the basis of the quantity and quality of applications created by outside developers ( Krazit, 2008a ).
Apple shipped almost 6 million devices worldwide even before opening the App Store in mid-2008. Shipments declined
in the second quarter of 2008, right before the introduction of the second-generation iPhone, the iPhone 3G, but
skyrocketed in the third quarter of 2008, when Apple shipped almost 5 million units worldwide.
Google, on the other hand, introduced Android in a market already populated by powerful contenders, including
Symbian, RIM, and Apple. Rather than attracting consumers directly, Google ’s strategy focused on attracting handset makers
with an operating system licensed to them for free, and attracting third-party developers with an open-source operating
system for which it was relatively easy to develop applications. The first Android smartphone —the G1, designed mostly by
Google, manufactured by HTC, and introduced in October 2008 —did not have radically new features vis-à-vis the iPhone and
could not match the iPhone in terms of design ( Mossberg, 2008b ). As regards features and design, it was only in November
2009 —when Motorola introduced its Android-based Droid smartphone —that Google ’s Android truly started competing with
Apple ’s iPhone ( Mossberg, 2009b ).
In a sense, Google ’s strategy with Android was precisely the opposite of that implemented by Apple with the iPhone. In
Apple ’s approach there was no role for handset makers, whereas Google attempted to get as many of them as possible on
board for its multi-sided platform. In Apple ’s approach there was no role for multiple network carriers at the starting point,
whereas Google again made the operating system available to as many of them as possible.
3.4. Sustaining momentum: continuing to attract consumers to the platform
Attracting consumers at the starting point is a necessary condition for take-off. But historical evidence on multi-sided
platforms reveals that, in high-technology industries, platforms do lose momentum and shrink, sometimes to the point of
non-existence. The same factors that facilitate take-off once critical mass has been achieved also accelerate the decline when
platforms fail to continue to attract users.
What factors have attracted users to specific smartphone platforms? Recent survey evidence from the U.S. and Europe
reveals that the top dimensions that consumers take into account in the process of choosing a smartphone platform are the
quality of the network of the mobile service provider, the mobile operating system itself, the selection of apps that is
available for a specific operating system, and the overall cost of the monthly service ( Comscore, 2012 , pp. 12 –13).2The next
few sections of this study are organized around these topics: we investigate the way in which smartphone platforms have
continued to attract users via innovation in hardware and operating-system software, in network technology, and in the
provision of third-party applications.
Depending on the way the smartphone platform is organized, innovation in different portions of the platform has been
carried out by different stakeholders. None of the operating-system owners or sponsors has been directly responsible for
network-technology innovation —in this area they have relied on the initiatives and cost –benefit considerations of network
carriers or operators. Similarly, they have relied mostly on hundreds of third-party developers for the supply of applications.
Apple and RIM have been responsible for innovation in both hardware and mobile operating-system software, since they
supply an integrated bundle. Microsoft and Google have been essentially responsible for driving innovation in the operating

4. Sustaining momentum: attracting consumers with hardware and operating-system software innovation
In late 2011 consumers purchasing a smartphone in the U.S. ranked the mobile operating system itself as the second
most important reason for acquiring a specific smartphone, after the quality of the network. In Europe, they ranked the

features of the mobile operating system were considerably more important for smartphone owners than for other mobile
phone owners.
Mobile operating-system owners or sponsors have continued to attract consumers to the platform through hardware and
operating-system software innovation. For space reasons we focus only on innovation on the iPhone platform, but it is
worth noting that at least a portion of Apple ’s iPhone innovation has been a response to innovative features that Google and
its handset partners implemented on various Android-based handsets. Tables 3A and 3Bsummarize the evolution of

features it embodied, successive generations of the device have included catch-up features —that is, in later generations of

balance of costs and benefits is less clear, particularly when compared with the Google strategy of facilitating adoption by
engaging multiple network partners. In any case, the facts highlight once again that owners or sponsors of mobile operating
systems have had to rely on innovation by their partners to sustain momentum with consumers.

7. Attracting developers
Although Apple ’s lift-off was based on attracting consumers with features, attracting third-party application developers
soon became important for the company to maintain its momentum. Economic models of app developer behavior focus on
the cost and benefits of developing apps on specific operating systems.4Costs are determined fundamentally by how
difficult it is to create software applications for a given mobile operating system, and benefits are determined by how many
units of the application developers expect to be able to sell on the platform and at what price. An additional factor of
significance is the proportion of the price of the app the owner of the app store will retain for itself.
The predictions derived from such models appear, for the most part, to be borne out by survey data on mobile app
developers. A developer survey conducted in 2010 found that most developers worked on multiple platforms, on average 2.8
platforms per developer ( VisionMobile, 2010 , p. 13). In the multi-sided lingo, mobile app developers practice multi-homing.
This enhances competition among multi-sided platforms, since developers that are already working on multiple platforms
have already sunk the costs involved in learning to program on multiple operating systems and can switch relatively quickly
to the one that is attracting more users at a given point in time (or is expected to do so in the near future).
A similar survey conducted in 2012 asked developers about the main factors driving platform choice. The top five criteria
identified by developers for adoption of a platform were that the platform have a large installed base of devices (chosen by
54 percent of respondents), that it facilitate app development at low cost (43 percent), that it be a familiar development
environment (31 percent), that it offer good revenue potential (30 percent), and that it provide good documentation and
tech support (30 percent) ( VisionMobile, 2012 , p. 27). Two of the five top adoption criteria were clearly on the revenue side
(installed base and revenue potential), and the other three on the cost side (low cost development, familiar environment,
and tech support). Reach —that is, the size of the installed base —was selected consistently as the top adoption criterion for
developers in surveys conducted between 2010 and 2013 ( VisionMobile, 2010 ,2011 ,2012 ,2013 ).
The 2010 survey identified a number of channels through which developers took their mobile apps to market —apps were
sold via app stores, distributed via web portals (developer, third-party, or network operator portals), or preloaded on mobile
devices by network operators or handset makers. The survey also identified various monetization models, including pay-
per-download, ad-funded, one-off development fees, support and customization fees, and even per-unit device royalties

8. Attracting advertisers
Attracting advertisers has been important for mobile platform owners or sponsors for two reasons. First, ads placed on
the platform have been a significant source of revenues and profits, considerably more so for Google than for Apple. Second,
ads have been used by platform owners or sponsors to attract developers. And since developers and apps attract consumers,
in mobile platforms attracting advertisers is an indirect approach for attracting consumers.
As a matter of fact, advertisers seek eyeballs. In recent years advertisers have tended to migrate following the eyeballs ’
migration paths —first from traditional print media to the desktop- and laptop-accessible Internet and then from there to the
mobile Internet. By 2009 mobile advertising spending in the U.S., estimated at $416 million according to some sources, was
tiny compared with the corresponding figures for online advertising ($24 billion), newspaper advertising ($38 billion), and
TV advertising ($51 billion), but the trend was clearly in favor of mobile advertising, which had grown at annual compound
rates of 28 percent between 2005 and 2009 ( Varaiya, 2010 , p. 22). Mobile advertising revenues reached $3.18 billion in the
U.S. in 2012 ($9.76 billion worldwide) and were forecast to reach $3.8 billion in the U.S. ($11.4 billion worldwide) in 2013
(Gartner Research, 2013 ).
As ads started migrating to mobile devices, a number of multi-sided intermediaries —ad networks —arose to connect
operators and publishers that own mobile websites, on the one hand, and advertisers, on the other. (Publishers own “space, ”
such as websites, which they supply and demand ads to be hosted on that space, whereas advertisers supply ads and
demand space for those ads to be placed in.) This was another interesting example of auxiliary multi-sided platforms (ad
networks) arising independently from “core ”multi-sided platforms (mobile operating systems), but with the goal of
exploiting monetization opportunities available on the core platforms.
According to a categorization, three types of ad networks existed as of 2009. “Blind networks, ”such as AdMob, were the
largest ones, served ads mainly through independent mobile publishers, provided limited targeting options, and did not
allow advertisers to choose the website (or application) where their ads would be placed. Their pricing was mostly based on
cost-per-click (CPC) —advertisers paid publishers (typically a mobile website owner) when their ads were clicked. “Premium
blind networks, ”such as Quattro Wireless, were medium-sized networks that served a higher proportion of their ads
through premium publishers, offered more targeting options, and allowed advertisers to select the websites where their ads
would be shown but at a premium price. They used CPC pricing but also offered some brand advertising based on cost per
mille (CPM) pricing —the cost of an advertising placement divided by the number of ad impressions, in thousands, it
generates. “Premium networks, ”finally, such as Microsoft Mobile Advertising, were the smallest ones, served their ads
through prestige publishers, offered many targeting options, allowed advertisers to choose websites, and rarely relied on
CPC ( Varaiya, 2010 , p. 8).
As the mobile advertising market started growing, owners/sponsors of mobile operating systems took steps to improve
their competitive position in this market. Google had developed the Android mobile operating system precisely because it
was concerned that eyeballs (and advertisers) were starting to migrate to the mobile web. Because of its background Googledeveloped, from the beginning, a strong presence in mobile search advertising with the approach it had successfully applied
earlier, that is, by placing text ads linked to searches performed by users on Google ’s (now mobile) website.
In late 2009 Google took an important step to expand its presence in other segments of the mobile advertising market —it
acquired AdMob, an ad network that the entrepreneur Omar Hamoui had founded in 2006. AdMob had become particularly
effective at placing mobile display and text ads in mobile websites and apps. Google viewed the AdMob acquisition as the
most expeditious way of covering almost the whole spectrum of mobile advertising (search ads, web display ads, and app
display ads) ( Google, 2010 ;Kincaid, 2009 ).
The Federal Trade Commission launched an investigation of the acquisition because at the time “the parties were the two
leading mobile advertising networks. ”The investigation revealed that Google and AdMob viewed “the other as its primary
competitor, and that each firm made business decisions in direct response to this perceived competitive threat ”(FTC, 2010 ).
One of the key reasons why the FTC allowed Google ’s Ad Mob acquisition to take place was that, in December 2009,
Apple responded in kind by acquiring the third largest mobile ad network, Quattro Wireless, and then launching its own
mobile advertising network under the iAd name. The FTC believed that Apple would quickly become a strong player in the
mobile advertising market, not only because of its strong relationships with app developers and users, but also because it
would be able to leverage proprietary data on users of Apple mobile devices to offer highly effective targeted ads. Putdifferently, Apple had all the tools required to confront Google/AdMob in what was their core market —the so-called
performance mobile advertising segment in which advertisers buy space through auction and pay for ads on a CPC basis
(FTC, 2010 ).
Both Google and Apple sought to attract advertisers not only because of the advertising revenue itself but also because
increasing advertising revenue would provide each one of them with better opportunities to attract developers, which in
turn would further draw mobile operating system users. When Apple introduced the iPhone 4 in mid-2010, Steve Jobs
emphasized the role that iAd would play in helping developers monetize their apps. Even developers of free apps would be
able to earn revenues from their apps on the iOS platform: the iAd network would reach advertisers and would develop an
inventory of ads, and if app developers agreed to have the ads placed on their apps, they would collect 60 percent of the
revenues that Apple received for the ads. In fact, according to Jobs the iAd network already had $60 million worth of ad
inventory sold for 2010, a good portion of that coming from premium brands such as Nissan, GE, Best Buy, and Geico. Jobs
explicitly emphasized the multi-sided externalities: a large installed base of users attracted advertisers, which in turn gaveM. Campbell-Kelly et al. / Telecommunications Policy 39 (2015) 717 –734 729

The competition for advertisers between iOS and Android intensified as time went by, and it led to lower prices (and/or
better terms) not only for advertisers but for developers as well. In 2010, for example, brands were required to spend $1
million just to initiate a campaign on the iOS platform. That amount declined to $400,000 in December 2011 and further to
$100,000 in February 2012.
In addition, Apple announced that starting in February 2012 developers on the iOS platform would receive not 60 but 70
percent of the revenues generated from ads placed within their applications. This was a reflection of the fact that the
Google/AdMob share of the U.S. mobile advertising market had grown from 19 to 24 percent and the iOS/iAd share had
declined from 19 percent to 15 percent between 2010 and 2011 ( Reisinger, 2012 ).
9. Conclusions
Multi-sided platform theory provides useful insights to understand the economic and business history of smartphones.
We have interpreted mobile operating systems as forming the core of mobile platforms that serve the needs of a variety of
customers. The ultimate goal of mobile platforms is, of course, to attract users, but because of their multi-sided nature, they
need to attract other customer types —handset makers, network carriers, third-party app developers, and even advertisers —
in order to attract users.
Multi-sided platform theory also helps us understand why two mobile operating systems —Apple ’s and Google ’s—have
managed to capture substantial shares of the market, and why Google ’s operating system has caught up with, and
surpassed, Apple ’s. The iPhone ’s initial success was not due to the variety of third-party apps available for it —there were
none. Neither was it due to the variety of handset models —there was just one —nor to the variety of networks on which the
phone operated —it worked only on AT&T. It was due fundamentally to the superior features it embedded in terms of
handset design and operating-system software, including a mature mobile Internet experience. When it was introduced,
trusted analysts emphasized the extent to which the iPhone was considerably better than other smartphones in the market
(Mossberg & Boehret, 2007a ,2007b ).
Apple did not sustain momentum with users by attracting handset makers or network carriers —in fact, the iPhone
worked on only one network, AT&T, until 2011, when Sprint and Verizon joined the platform ( Mossberg, 2011 ). It sustained
momentum with users by, first, innovating steadily in terms of operating-system software and handset design, and, second,
by attracting third-party developers via the App Store. Although it opened the platform to third-party developers, it keptTable 7

Google responded with a multi-sided model characterized by considerably more openness. It created an open-source
operating system and gave its partners —handset makers and even network operators —the ability to modify it to achieve
product differentiation. It engaged as many handset makers and network carriers as it could from the beginning, and it
made it extremely easy for third-party developers to create apps for the platform. Table 8 shows the speed with which

The extent to which the (more open) Google model succeeded can be seen from Figs. 2 and 3 .
Worldwide Android shipments surpassed iPhone shipments in late 2010 and grew much faster thereafter (Fig. 2). The
Android platform grew not only faster but also more steadily. The iPhone platform grew in spurts, with shipments declining
before the introduction of a new model, and exploding immediately thereafter. Android shipments showed less volatility,
since new versions of the operating system were introduced frequently and new handset models embedding the Android
operating system were introduced almost every month from 2010 on ( Fig. 3 ).
Two additional features of the (more open) Android multi-sided model facilitated its faster growth. First, Android
engaged a variety of network operators before Apple did, and innovation in network technology by the various operators
facilitated adoption. In fact, one of the key problems of the iPhone in the early years was that it only worked on the AT&T
network, and the quality of the AT&T network appears to have varied considerably by location ( Mossberg, 2008a ,2009a ,
2010 ,2011 ). Second, since it engaged a variety of handset makers as well, Android phones quickly became available in an
extremely wide price range, much more so than the iPhone —Android ’s“budget ”handsets clearly facilitated adoption
(German, 2011 ).
The decline of the incumbents —BlackBerry and Symbian —was the mirror image of the rise of the newcomers and can
also be understood in the context of multi-sided platform theory. The BlackBerry thrived with a focus on one feature, mobile
access to corporate email, but could not match the bundle of capabilities that the original iPhone offered. Symbian phones
were no match for the original iPhone either, and by the time the Symbian stakeholders decided to follow an open-source
strategy it was likely too late —network effects were already propelling the rise of the iPhone. It is clear that early on there
was some hesitation among network carriers and handset makers regarding whether to support Symbian or Android, but
once Android started taking off, network effects worked their magic —Android skyrocketed and Symbian collapsed.
Competition between Apple and Google in the smartphone market brings to the forefront once again the tension that West
(2003) carefully analyzed in earlier stages of the computer industry: that between “appropriability ”and “adoption. ”To recoup
the costs of developing the platform, the sponsor must be able to capture at least some portion of the benefits derived from
the platform. At the same time, in order to obtain any returns at all, the sponsor must foster adoption, which requires sharing a
portion of the platform ’s returns with various customers (since often customer innovation leads to adoption by users, as we
have seen in the case of smartphones). Differential emphases on appropriability and adoption have led to different business
models in the smartphone industry, and we have relied on multi-sided platform theory to shed light on those models.
