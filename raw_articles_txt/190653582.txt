Abstract
The digital media software (DMS) industry is an important yet relatively underexplored industry in terms of dynamics,evolution, market potential and growth opportunities. Using Adobe as an exemplar, this case highlights the evolution of theDMS industry with a focus on Adobe ’s key business segments, business model and business strategy –product diver-
siﬁcation and inorganic expansion –speci ﬁcally capturing Adobe ’s response to changing market needs. Why Adobe made
an important yet risky strategic decision to transition from the time-tested perpetual licence price model to a cloud andsubscription-based pricing model is the primary focus of this case. We cover the contextual imperatives that caused this
strategic shift and the repercussions of the strategic decision on Adobe ’s overall performance and future growth trajectory.
This case attempts to familiarize readers with the dynamics of the DMS industry and allow them to assess Adobe ’s customer
value proposition and business models by utilizing concepts and frameworks from the strategy, technological innovationand platform business domains.

Introduction
The digital media software industry (DMS) consists ofproducts, solutions, and services, enabling enterprises,teams, and individuals to design, develop graphics and
videos and improve their productivity through collabora-
tion. The main software segments of DMS include desktoppublishing, graphic and digital imaging, document readersand viewers, digital video editing and DVD authoring.During the last decade, the DMS market has shown con-sistent double-digit growth and reached 12.4 billion in2020. With a CAGR of 12%, the DMS market is expected toreach US$ 38.2 billion by 2030 ( Absolute Reports Pvt Ltd,
2022 ;Digital content creation market size, 2022 ;
FinancialNewsMedia.com, 2021 ). Adobe, Avid Technol-
ogy, Autodesk, Af ﬁnity (Serif) and Canva are among the
leading players in this market (refer to Exhibit 1 ).
Adobe faced two challenges to accommodate changing
market dynamics while transitioning from the time-testedperpetual licence price model to a cloud and subscription-based pricing model. First, customers perceived the new
business model as costlier than the previous one. Second,
the competition was heating up to seize market share andattract new customers by exploiting the ﬁrst-mover ad-
vantage. Recently, several of Adobe ’s competitors have
integrated video editing features into their photo editing
programs in response to shifting technological advances andconsumer demands. In contrast, Adobe ’s photo editing
products have primarily focussed on photo editing features.Will Adobe be able to develop more affordable and user-friendly (browser-based) versions of its creative software tocapture this new user category and compete with emergingcompetitors such as Canva in the consumer business seg-
ment? These are important questions whose answers lie in
the future.

Evolution of the digital media software
industry
The widespread use of social media platforms such as
LinkedIn, Twitter, Facebook and Instagram has increasedthe demand for digital content, boosting DMS softwaremarket growth. While increasing digitalization, the avail-ability of high-speed internet connectivity, the expandinguser base of smartphones and tablets, and the growing use of
social media marketing are expected to fuel the growth of
the DMS market, the availability of open-source softwareand piracy are key impediments to the market ’s growth. In
the following paragraph, we brie ﬂy discuss the factors that
have fuelled the industry ’s growth.
Technology
During the past decade, the expansion of the DMS busi-
nesses has been impacted by the rapid evolution of thetechnology landscape. From 2010 to 2015, worldwide in-
ternet bandwidth increased from 0.4 to 1.2 million Mbits/
second, as did the percentage of internet users and internetspeed. R&D spending in the semiconductor industry hasincreased, leading to higher performance of computers. Thecustomers ’preference for hosting shifted from their servers
to the cloud. By the end of 2013, approximately 80% ofFortune 1000 companies were using some form of cloudservices. Cloud spending increased from $14.1 B in 2010 to
$43.3 B in 2015. In 2014, 3D printing picked up speed at an
astounding rate, while the prices of 3D home printers werereduced by 90% in just 4 years. Mobile phones have becomepowerful enough to run image editing software such asPhotoshop. The trade of digitizable items (such as CDs,books and newspapers) decreased from 2.7% of overallgoods trade in 2000 to 0.8% in 2016. The explosion in tabletmarkets and the evolution of AR/VR led to a burst in content
creation. The IDC forecasted that AR/VR spending will
grow from $7B in 2018 to $128B in 2023. According to theIDC, the global public cloud services market size reached anastonishing USD 233.4 billion in 2019, up by 26%from 2018.Economic
The great recession of 2008 –2009 forced many businesses
to reconsider their ongoing revenue streams. However,following the end of the recession, economic growth andincreased IT spending created a favourable environment forthe industry. Global employment in the Information,
Communication, and Technology (ICT) sector increased by
16% between 2010 and 2015, rising to 39.3 million from34 million ( Digital Economy Report, 2019 ). Digitally
skilled workers started getting higher average pay. From2010 to 2015, digital advertising spending continued toincrease signi ﬁcantly.
COVID-19 was another recent factor that fuelled the
growth of the DMS industry. People laid off during a re-
cession, COVID or an economic shock began creating
digital material and sharing it on social media after gainingaccess to content-creation software such as Adobe Photo-shop and content publication platforms such as Instagram,YouTube, TikTok and Snapchat. These events set a virtuouscycle for content creators and publishers, propelling theDMS industry forward.

Legal, trade wars and politics
During the last decade, software piracy has become a
signiﬁcant concern in the IT industry. The Business Soft-
ware Alliance ’s (BSA) 2010 BSA Global Software Piracy
Study found that worldwide software piracy increased by
14% in 2010, reaching a new high of $59 billion in com-
mercial value. According to the National Bureau of AsianResearch, intellectual property theft costs the US economy$320 billion in 2014, roughly the same amount as USexports to Asia. Trade wars and ﬁrewall issues have neg-
atively impacted industry growth over the last decade,imposing stringent restrictions on data storage, sovereigntyand data management. In 2017 –2018, Facebook ’s Cam-
bridge Analytica scandal also raised serious concerns about
data usage policies. The General Data Protection Re-quirements (GDPR, 2018) regulations required online sitesto provide consumers with ‘notice and consent ’rights
before utilizing their personal information. Digitization andenvironmental awareness have caused reduced usage ofpaper and printers. Printer supplies have declined at a 3%annual rate as the digitization of businesses has reduced the
need for large-scale of ﬁce printing ﬂeets.
Competitive landscape
Adobe continued to dominate the market for a long time,
with 53% share in 2009, while Microsoft was at a distantsecond, with 17% share, followed by Avid Technology,
Apple and others. The industry witnessed sustained double-
digit growth during the last decade, leading to an explosionin the content-creation market. Adobe is ﬁrmly positioned
within the digital media market. Almost all its products inthis segment are market leaders. In addition, Adobe isuniquely positioned to expand the value offered to itscurrent consumers. However, it may face intense compe-tition as it tries to scale down its premium products for
entry-level customers at low prices. Many smaller mobile
developers and operating system software developers havedeveloped competing products that appeal to these markets.
Moreover, Microsoft has been touting their ‘Creators
Update ’, which seeks to enable creative professionals with
the included software. Af ﬁnity software, which started in
1987 by developing creative software for Windows, re-launched its completely designed Af ﬁnity app in October
2014. New, easy-to-use, cloud-based digital content-
creation software providers such as Canva have enteredthe market and threatened the existing marketing leaders.With the shift from desktop to mobile devices, companiesneed to become mobile-friendly by creating mobile versionsof their apps.Adobe ’s growth story
Founding years
In 1982, John Warnock and Charles Geschke founded
Adobe Inc. (previously Adobe Systems Inc.) in MountainView, California, to launch the PostScript programminglanguage. The company was listed on the NASDAQ in1986, and it introduced the PDF document format in 1993.Many software applications, such as Creative Cloud, Ac-robat Reader and Photoshop, are household names in the
digital media industry. When an image needs to be edited,
the phrase ‘Photoshop it ’is used.
Adobe ’s earliest products were the PostScript lan-
guage and the PDF format based on it. PostScript waslicenced to other printing companies, and it quicklybecame one of the most used printing languages in the1990s. Apple Computer licenced PostScript in 1985 foruse in its LaserWriter printers, which helped spark the
desktop publishing revolution. Apple became the ﬁrst
licencee of the software. PostScript managed to obtainsales worth $1.9 million in 1985. Next on Adobe ’s agenda
was international expansion. The company signed anagreement with Canon Inc. of Japan, under which Canonhad full licensing rights to Adobe PostScript. The PDFﬁle format was intended to store all the information re-
quired to view any document on any computer. With its
ﬂagship creative product, Photoshop, Adobe began re-
leasing creative software in the late 1980s, leading to therelease of Adobe Premiere in 1991 and Adobe AcrobatReader in 1993.
Modes of expansion –selected M&A and
diversi ﬁcation
Adobe has a long history of using acquisitions to drive
growth. In 1994, it acquired Aldus, which provided it
with the PageMaker design software and video editing
software After Effects, stil l a popular application. In
2005, Adobe acquired Macromedia, which gave it webdesign software Flash and Dreamweaver. In 2014, Adobeacquired Fotolia, a popular marketplace for stock pho-tography and video , for approximately $800 million. The
acquisition provided the capa bility to integrate the ser-
vice into Adobe ’s Creative Cloud and made it easier for
Creative Cloud members to use (and purchase) over
34 million images and videos in the company ’s portfolio.

Customers
The DMS industry consists of two primary user groups:
Amateur and Professionals. Amateurs can be furthersubdivided into two categories: home users and hobbyists.
Home users often use entry-level software solutions for
entertainment, personal, family and household purposes. In
contrast, hobbyists typically use professional software ap-plications for their projects, such as graphic design, editingand publishing. Similarly, the professional group consists oftwo categories: professionals and semiprofessionals. Theprofessional category includes animators, editors and videorecording/mixing professionals who use software applica-tions to derive their primary income directly. The semipro-
fessional category consists of individuals or organizations
that derive some income from these creative software ap-plications and are engaged in business support functions orpart-time ventures, such as graphic artistry, home video ar-chiving or wedding videography. Historically, Adobe ’se n -
terprise customers and individual users have paid a perpetuallicence price to use Adobe software for decades. Adobenoticed that the number of creative users did not increase as
quickly as earlier. Because the older versions of their software
worked well enough, buyers of their software were likely toavoid future releases. Under the desktop-based perpetuallicensing model, Adobe shipped approximately three millionunits per year, which stayed ﬂat for a long time.

needed to attract the next generation of customers to itsplatform and expand its target customer base. Switching to a
cloud-based subscription model allowed Adobe to expand

Business model changes
In 2012, Adobe went from a perpetual licence-based business
strategy to a monthly subscription business model ( Osterwalder
and Pigneur, 2010 ) when it launched the Adobe Creative Cloud.
Because subscription-based models were relatively unfamiliarto the market at the time, the paradigm change was sudden. In
May 2013, Adobe stated that the Creative Cloud would be the
primary access platform for its Cloud model. It accounted for75% of the company ’s revenue, valued at almost $3 billion,
three years after its inception. The following paragraphs brie ﬂy
highlight the changes between the two models.
Associates
In the traditional desktop-based perpetual licence model,Adobe was accustomed to selling its creative productsthrough resellers and retailers such as staples. These retailershelped Adobe sell its products in the box using CDs or ﬂoppy
disks. Adobe also collaborated with complementary softwaremakers such as Aldus and Netscape. The collaboration withthese partners helped Adobe realize a larger, more importantmarket for professional desktop applications. These part-
nerships helped Adobe ’sﬁle formats become the go-to
format for displaying graphical design on the web. Thisturned Acrobat into a web application, allowing it to be usedin areas other than Adobe ’sc o r ed e s i g n e rm a r k e t .W i t ht h e

any performance issues. Adobe entered into long-term
agreements and partnerships with Amazon for AWS and
Microsoft for Azure and other cloud-based services.
Key activities
Adobe has competed as a licence-based corporation formore than two decades. They have built a strong reputation
as providers of high-quality professional and consumer
design tools for which customers are willing to pay apremium upfront. However, the software business waschanging. Many companies began selling software as asubscription service in the cloud rather than selling licencesand CDs. To stay a ﬂoat, Adobe had to forsake the perpetual

The economics: Cost, revenue and results
Cost structure. The new provisioning model reduced reve-
nue losses associated with piracy, and it also eliminated
many of the vestiges of packaged software development,
such as packaging and shipping. Adobe was able to cut thecosts associated with maintaining, upgrading and sup-porting multiple versions of the same products. The sub-scription model also allowed Adobe to cut the cost ofcustomer acquisition, as it was much cheaper to renew anexisting customer than to acquire a new customer. It reducedthe cost of sales and lowered the marketing budget (nor-
malized to sales), as the company did not have to spend itsmarketing budget on every new product release. Productupdates can be easily regulated and executed by thecompany and for all customers at the same time using acloud suite. These resulted in cost-cutting advantages, suchas not dealing with customer service queries about a soft-
ware version from 5 years ago.
Revenue stream. Adobe used a perpetual licence model for
its digital media content-creation software business, whichallowed customers to purchase and use Adobe ’s products
for a lifetime. Adobe ’s revenues were cyclically driven by
major product or suite launches or upgrades, typically every18–24 months. There were spikes in revenue every
18 months when updates were released but were less pre-

Like all transitions, the transition from licenced software to
