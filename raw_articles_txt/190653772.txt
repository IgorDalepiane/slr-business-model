In the past decades, smartphone transit apps have emerged as a cost-effective strategy to enhance the transit user 
experience. This study provides an overview of transit apps used in 295 US transit agencies to shed light on the 
current practices and future trends of transit apps as a comprehensive information technology that serves public 
transportation. By conducting interviews with 21 transit industry leaders, we synthesized the perceived benefits 
and costs of transit apps and identified challenges and the corresponding strategies. Transit apps enhance user 
experiences by enabling account-based mobile fare payment and efficient two-way communications. To provide 
high-quality transit apps to passengers, transit agencies need to improve the reliability of underlying data and 
vendor management. In addition, five existing business models and emerging trends of transit apps were iden-
tified and discussed. The findings of this study advance knowledge about transit apps and provide practical 
recommendations for future improvements and implementations.   
1.Introduction 
Public transportation plays a critical role in enhancing mobility, 
promoting economic development, and improving environmental sus-
tainability (Brown and Werner, 2008; Hess, 2005; Baum-Snow and 
Kahn, 2000; Anderson, 2014 ). However, public transportation agencies 
have been struggling to maintain ridership, although many transit 
capital improvement projects have been implemented (American Public 
Transportation Association, 2019 ). Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic 
has brought additional nationwide substantial decreases in transit 
ridership since 2020 (Hu and Chen, 2021 ). 
In the past decades, smartphone transit apps have emerged as a cost- 
effective strategy that could enhance the transit user experience and 
have the potential to encourage transit use. Numerous transit apps have 
been published by transit agencies and private sector developers 
providing access to fare payment, trip planning, and real-time vehicle 
information on smartphones. As smartphone usage has become 
increasingly ubiquitous, transit apps emerged as an essential channel to 
disseminate relevant and timely transit information. 
A growing number of empirical studies have shown that for end 
users, different functions in transit apps could increase the perceived 
value of transit, save time, and increase transit use. Several studies have revealed that real-time information could enhance perceived safety, 
reduce waiting anxiety at stops, increase the perceived reliability of 
transit services, and build positive attitudes toward transit (Brakewood 
et al., 2014; Kaplan et al., 2017; Gooze et al., 2013; McCord et al., 2015 ). 
Watkins, Ferris (Watkins et al., 2011 ) and Brakewood, Barbeau 
(Brakewood et al., 2014 ) demonstrated that providing the real-time 
vehicle location to transit app users could shorten both their perceived 
and actual wait time at transit stops. During the COVID-19 pandemic, 
real-time crowding information alerted passengers about seat avail-
ability and overcrowding via transit apps (Bian et al., 2022 ). Moreover, 
real-time vehicle information made it easy to take transit and encour -
aged users to make more transit trips (Brakewood et al., 2020; Ferris 
et al., 2011 ). Previous studies also showed that mobile ticketing could 
eliminate the hassle of carrying cash and tickets, make it easier and 
faster to purchase transit passes, shorten the speed of boarding, make 
transit use more flexible, and provide contactless ticketing methods 
during the COVID-19 pandemic (Apanasevic and Markendahl, 2018; 
Brakewood, 2020; Mesoraca and Brakewood, 2018 ). Although a few 
existing studies discussed the benefits of other features in transit apps, 
scholars argued that trip planning, contact and feedback, notice and 
alerts, and favorite route/stop marking functions have the potential to 
improve the transit use experience and encourage transit use

Some researchers examined transit information provision and fare 
payment via mobile devices from transit operators ’ perspectives. Tang 
and Thakuriah (Tang and Thakuriah, 2012 ) and Brakewood, Macfarlane 
(Brakewood et al., 2015 ) showed that real-time vehicle information 
boosted bus ridership by about 2% in Chicago and New York, respec -
tively. Real-time information, trip planning, and service alerts on transit 
apps would help passengers and thus relieve bus drivers from the burden 
of constantly responding to inquiries. Brakewood (Brakewood, 2020 ) 
documented mobile fare payment practices via apps in North America 
and identified five business models. The authors reported “improving 
the customer experience ”, ” reducing cash handling, ” and “reducing the 
overall cost of fare collection ” were primary reasons for deploying mo-
bile fare payment apps. Apanasevic and Markendahl (Apanasevic and 
Markendahl, 2018 ) stated that for public transport companies, mobile 
ticketing could enhance their technical functionalities, create financial 
values, improve working environments, increase work efficiency, and 
build reputations. 
However, there is a lack of studies investigating smartphone apps as 
a unified information technology platform that integrates various 
functionalities to serve public transportation and its users. The transit 
apps related decision-making and operational process in different transit 
agencies are largely unknown, and so are the benefits and impacts of 
transit apps from transit agencies ’ perspectives. Many transit operators 
suffer from insufficient experience and knowledge about the latest 
technological innovations and encounter challenges during operations 
(Brakewood, 2020 ). 
2.Research aims 
This study has two main aims. First, we aim to assess the current state 
of transit apps offered by major US transit agencies for fixed-route bus 
services, examining their characteristics, functions, and app ratings. 
Previous studies demonstrated the potential benefits of transit apps for 
fixed route bus services, which are the primary mode of transit offered 
by most transit operators in the US (Bian et al., 2021; Federal Transit 
Administration, 2020 ). Other transit modes such as on-demand transit, 
commuter rail, heavy rail, light rail, streetcars, and ferries have unique 
operational and reliability characteristics, which often necessitate 
different needs and requirements for transit apps. To ensure the 
comparability of results, we decided to limit the study scope by specif -
ically targeting transit apps that serve fixed-route buses. 
Second, we aim to investigate the practices of transit agencies that 
provide and manage official branded apps. Compared to those agencies 
using shared apps available from private sector developers, agencies 
that developed their own apps have more extensive experience and up- 
to-date knowledge about the apps. By conducting interviews with each 
agency ’s transit app project leadership teams, we intend to (1) under -
stand the development and deployment process of officially branded 
transit apps in different transit agencies; (2) summarize the perceived 
benefits of transit apps from transit agencies ’ perspectives; (3) discuss 
the challenges and lessons learned from the app development and 
deployment processes; and (4) synthesize plans of public agencies for 
their transit apps. With the increasing prevalence of transit apps, these 
efforts will promote discussions, foster information sharing, and 
advance the general knowledge of this domain. 
3.Methods 
American Public Transportation Association (American Public 
Transportation Association, 2020 ) listed 295 transit agencies as regular 
US transit systems. In October 2021, we reviewed transit apps provided 
by these 295 transit agencies by investigating their official websites, 
news, and app stores. Functionalities and app store ratings of these 
transit apps were examined and summarized. 
Then we targeted transit agencies that have developed and operated an official transit app that serves their bus riders. Official transit apps in 
this study are defined as smartphone apps developed or endorsed by 
transit agencies. These transit apps are exclusively branded with the 
logos of the transit agencies, and managed by the transit agencies. A pool 
of transit agencies was selected based on the following criteria: (1) the 
agency provides fixed-route bus services to the public; (2) the agency 
publishes its officially branded transit apps that allow passengers to plan 
trips, purchase tickets, or access real-time information; (3) the official 
transit app is available for both iOS and Android systems; and (4) the 
app received more ratings than the median level on Apple Store or 
Google Play. The number of ratings – a reflection of user engagement 
level with the app – is a useful metric to identify transit apps that have 
been widely used by passengers. Transit agencies that operate apps with 
more ratings are likely to have more experience in operating transit apps 
and serving a larger user base. There are other meaningful criteria, such 
as the number of downloads, that can reflect the number of users. This 
study used the number of ratings due to data availability. 
We sent an interview invitation email to the leadership team of each 
qualified transit agency, recruiting team members who have been 
directly involved in the transit app development and/or deployment 
work. Professionals with technical or operational experience were 
included. The person of contact was selected based on their position 
ranking, following this order: General Manager, Chief Innovation Offi-
cer, President/Vice President of Technology & Innovation, Director, 
Project Manager, and Technician. 
The invitation email informed the potential participants that the 
interview would take place online via ZOOM and take around 60 min. 
Once the participant(s) agreed to join the study, the study information 
and interview questions were shared with them by email in advance. Up 
to three interviewees from the same transit agencies were invited. No 
financial compensations were offered to the participants. Interview re-
cordings were transcribed verbatim. Nvivo12 was used for transcription 
coding and analysis. 
4.Findings 
4.1. State of transit apps 
Among 295 transit agencies, 48 agencies specialized in ferries, rail, 
paratransit, long-distance shuttles, or university/resort/airport bus 
services. Most transit agencies (N 247) did provide fixed-route bus 
services to the public. The vast majority (N 240) provided transit in-
formation via smartphone apps, except for seven transit agencies. Some 
(N 125) were affiliated with off-the-shelf private-sector apps, such as 
the Transit App1, myStop, TransLoc, and Token Transit. These apps were 
developed and operated by private developers and support multiple 
agencies. About half of the transit agencies (N 115) developed or 
endorsed branded transit apps (Fig. 1). This proportion is much higher 
among large transit operators. American Public Transportation Associ -
ation (American Public Transportation Association, 2019 ) listed the 50 
largest transit agencies by ranking their ridership2. Among the 46 transit 
agencies that provided fixed-route bus services, 41 of them operated 
officially branded transit apps.

agencies. The app store ratings and functionalities of these apps are 
summarized in Table 1. For apps officially branded by transit agencies, 
most of them (N114 out of 119) were available for both iOS and 
Android systems. More than half of these apps received a rating score of 
3 and above in both Google Play and Apple Store. Contact information

4.2. Interviewed transit agencies and their transit apps 
Based on the criteria set in Section 3, 45 transit agencies were 
selected for interviews. According to Federal Transit Administration 
(Federal Transit Administration, 2021), the annual unlinked passenger 
trips in these 45 selected agencies ranged from 1.1 million (M) to 
1727.4 M. We sent invitation emails to these transit agencies to request 
interviews on their transit app projects. Representatives from 17 transit 
agencies accepted the invitation and participated in the interview 
(Fig. 2). Transit agencies with unlinked passenger trips below 10 M were 
slightly less inclined to join interviews (Table 2). 
In the fall of 2021 and spring of 2022, we interviewed 21 pro-
fessionals from 17 transit agencies who had been leading their transit app projects. The interviewees were from the department of information 
and technology (13 or 61.9%), the department of finance (5 or 23.8%), 
and the department of marketing and communications (3 or 14.3%). In 
terms of their transit apps, contact information, fare payment, favorite 
route/stop marking, real-time vehicle information, riders alerts, and trip 
planning are common functionalities available in most transit apps

4.3. Motivations 
In our study, all interview participants realized that information 
provision and communications via smartphone apps were crucial for 
transit riders. They agreed that fare payment, trip planning, and real- 
time vehicle information should be the essential features of transit 
apps. There were many reasons that motivated transit agencies to 
develop and operate their own officially branded transit apps. 
First, while many transit agencies are exploring the next-generation 
fare collection solutions (e.g., automated fare collection system, 
account-based fare system, open payment system), smartphone transit 
apps are often viewed as a cost-effective medium for account manage -
ment (on the users’ side), transactions, and fare validations. As different 
transit agencies offer different fare products and continue changing their 
fare structures, developing a transit app that could be easily and cost- 
effectively customized would be a feasible solution. For example, one 
interviewee mentioned: 
“We knew we needed a new [fare collection] system. But it takes a long 
time to get funding, the requirements, hiring a vendor, and then finally 
launching. One thing that we saw with mobile apps was that transit 
agencies were able to do it very quickly.” 
Second, transit agencies often face pressure from customers to pro-
vide one transit app that integrates all functions and can easily integrate 
new features over time. Having control over the functions and user in-
terfaces is critical for transit agencies. One participant noted: 
“One of the things that we were looking for with this is scalability and 
modularity. So that we could start introducing core features, and then add 
things on in the development timeline, while the customers are still able to 
benefit from the functionality. And that as technologies and preferences 
change, we can swap pieces in and out, while reducing the cost and time 
required to do so.” 
Third, the currently off-the-shelf private-sector apps could not meet 
all the needs of transit agencies and riders. Some fare payment apps and 
trip planning apps serve hundreds or thousands of regions globally, 
making it hard to do customizations for specific transit agencies. Some 
private-sector apps consume free-available GTFS/GTFS-RT data for their 
trip planning functions and end up providing inaccurate information

Based on interviewees ’ descriptions of how their transit apps were 
developed and maintained, five business models were identified (Fig. 3): 
(1) private sector shared app (e.g., Transit App2, Token Transit); (2) 
white-label app with minor customization (e.g., MTA eTix, CATS Pass); 
(3) white-label app with major customization (e.g., CharmPass, Muni -
Mobile); (4) in-house design vendor development (e.g., GoPass, SEPTA); 
and (5) in-house development (e.g., TAP LA). 
Private sector shared apps were developed by a single vendor but 
serve multiple transit agencies in different regions/countries. Users need 
to zoom into the targeted area or select the agency to find the transit 
information or purchase the ticket they want. The upfront and opera -
tional costs would be minimal as these apps involve limited features and 
little to no branding or tailoring for each transit agency. 
The white-label app is also known as SaaS (Software-as-a-service) 
product, which is developed by third-party vendors (e.g., Bytemark, 
Masabi, Moovel, Unwire, INIT) and subsequently rebranded by transit 
agencies. The contract amounts varied according to the scope of work 
and the level of customization. The interviewees reported that for their 
transit apps, the upfront costs of app development ranged from $0 to 
$1000,000. The ongoing costs could be an annual lump sum (ranging 
from $100,000 to $500,000) or a charge per transaction (if it had fare 
payment functions). According to the interview results, after contract 
execution, time spent on the initial front-end app development varied 
from a few weeks to a few years. This model allows agencies to 
customize and configure the interface and functions of the app without 
hiring employees with app development skills. Interviewees explained: 
“We are not app developers. We don’t hire those with that skill set. So we 
knew from the beginning that we wanted a SaaS [product], the off-the- 
shelf app, where the app developers were the experts. They have de-
signers; they have engineers; they’re product managers. ” 
“One of the biggest benefits [of choosing white-label apps] for us is that 
we’re not responsible for PCI (Payment Card Industry Data Security 
Standard) compliance, at least on the platform. ”

white-label apps with minor customization. Minor customization 
included changing the name of the app, color schemes, route informa -
tion, fare types, etc., as one interviewee described:

Before the white-label apps became widely available, some transit 
agencies collaborated with third-party vendors to build their transit 
apps. This In-house design vendor development model describes the 
partnership that transit agencies lead the product management and the 
feature scoping process, and the third-party vendors develop the soft-
ware accordingly. With this business model, transit agencies take the 
role of product managers and own the final products. One transit agency 
said they sub-licensed their transit app product to other agencies to 
generate revenue: 
“We develop new capabilities that those of them rolled out made available 
to other markets. Because we’re an agency, we are an expertise, we know 
how things actually need to be implemented, we know the teams that need 
to be involved in taking something for fruition.” 
The in-house development model applies to transit apps that are 
completely designed and developed by the staff at transit agencies. The 
transit agency hires project managers, designers, and developers, and 
provides office space and proper support to the team. Compared to other 
business models, in-house development usually gives transit agencies 
more control over the branding, configuration, and data. This model also 
allows transit agencies to implement cutting-edge technologies that 
have not been widely adopted by the industry. For example, an inter-
viewee said: 
“[Our App] will allow you to travel with that tap card now, using the NFC 
capabilities that’s in the phone. You don’t even have to open the app, you 
can just drop your phone down, and the reader will take your tap card. 
[The App] just sits there on the phone, knows what you have on your tap 
card, and is immediately good. It is pretty cool.” 
Correspondingly, the time and financial costs of app development 
are typically higher as the level of control increases. The associated 
expenses include personnel costs, server costs, and other fees (e.g., li-
cense fees for JavaScript libraries). 
The five business models discussed provide a spectrum of choices for 
transit agencies. Many small- to medium-sized transit agencies with 
limited resources found that the shared app is the best fit because this 
model typically takes a short time to launch, requires no upfront pay-
ments, and charges a percentage of transactions after sales (if the app 
allows fare payment). Over the past decade, an increasing number of 
medium and large transit agencies have chosen the white-label app 
model since the products in this industry have become more mature and 
standardized. Compared to shared apps, white-label apps allow for 
additional agency branding and customization. The time frame for 
launching a white-label app is usually shorter than developing the app in-house. Meanwhile, some large transit agencies still gravitate towards 
in-house design or development models because these offer more flexi-
bility and control. The costs of in-house development vary with the 
scope of work and team structure/management. 
4.5. Perceived benefits 
All interviewees agreed that their transit apps were valuable to both 
the agencies and their customers. Table 4 exhibits the representative 
interview quotes for perceived benefits of different functionalities in 
transit apps. 
Almost half of the transit agencies (eight out of 17) offer an all-in-one 
place single app for passengers that supports and advertises their 
multimodal transit operations (e.g., buses, light rail, Bus Rapid Transit, 
paratransit, on-demand transport, streetcars, and water taxis). By 
allowing passengers to pay fares, plan trips, contact customer services, 
and report suspicious activities, these transit apps enhance the digital 
experience of the riders, which can further encourage their transit use. 
One interviewee summarized, “[our app] is a one-stop shopping for transit 
in the palm of your hand.” 
Most of the reviewed transit apps (16 out of 20) enable riders to 
purchase tickets and manage their wallets through a password-protected 
account. This account-based fare collection system empowers transit 
agencies to implement fare capping programs and run targeted mar-
keting. One interviewee gave an example, “we can provide a promo code 
to the members of an upcoming convention. And so then we can isolate to see 
many passengers we gained from that event.” As a streamlined, contactless, 
and secure way for transit ticketing, mobile ticketing is widely adopted

Many interviewees considered the importance of transit apps as a 
channel to communicate with passengers. Trip planning and real-time 
vehicle information engage new riders and help existing riders plan 
their activities based on reliable data. As one interviewee said, 
“it was incredibly difficult to communicate to riders. You may have an 
email system that people subscribe to, but it’s not intuitive. So having a 
method through the mobile app to communicate directly to the customer 
and engage with them is really important.” 
Several interviewees highlighted the value of data they got from 
transit apps. By keeping track of transit users anonymously, transit 
agencies would be able to improve their services and feed the data back 
to the users. Collecting data on riders would also make it possible for 
transit agencies to provide contextually relevant information, such as 
offers, events, and service alerts, to targeted customers. 
4.6. Challenges 
First, choosing and working with third-party vendors can be chal-
lenging. Given the fact that the software application industry has been 
evolving rapidly, collaborating with credible and leading-edge vendors 
is critical for transit agencies. Most of the transit agencies interviewed 
are constantly updating their transit apps in terms of functions, designs, 
and service areas. Vendors need to ensure flexibility and scalability 
while making complex apps intuitive to users. One of the major chal-
lenges for transit agencies in working with vendors is to ensure that the 
app is developed within the planned time frame. Prolonged contract 
negotiations, excessive communication and coordination, and ineffi -
cient testing process could delay the deployment. Interviewees 
explained: 
“When you have a subcontractor that belongs to your prime contract 
vendor, you can tell the subcontractor what to do, but you have to work 
with you prime. So that can derive in challenges…there can be a lot of 
delays, just getting information and feedback.” 
“The biggest challenge is the need to accelerate the initial functionality 
delivery. There were project schedule rearrangements, finding testers, 
getting it tested. Making sure that it was developed within the time frame is 
challenging.” 
Second, quality issues with underlying data for app development are 
common hurdles to getting well-functioning transit apps. Small- and 
medium-sized transit agencies often find it difficult to hire staff with 
data management and application development expertise. Inaccurate 
trip planning and real-time vehicle information based on the poor 
quality of General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS)/GTFS-RT feed 
often mislead passengers. Many transit agencies interviewed faced 
challenges in producing high-quality, up-to-date data. One interviewee 
explained: 
“Sometimes the staff did not go through the process of data cleaning and 
updating; Sometimes the modems and bandwidth on board did not meet 
the requirements; Sometimes the onboard devices were not configured for 
the right vehicles.” 
Third, ineffective cross-department collaborations are common 
challenges reported by the interviewed agencies. The transit app 
acquirement, development, and deployment process involve many de-
partments within or across agencies. An interviewee noted, “making sure 
everything is moving forward in the same direction at the same time is a huge 
challenge.” Despite this, interviewees shared their experience, “[A suc-
cessful transit app project] requires a lot of commitment from the leadership 
side and the operation side.” “We have to compromise between different 
stakeholders.” 
Finally, some interviewees discussed the equity issues related to 
transit apps and provided their solutions. For instance, in some cities, bankless transit riders can load money into their digital wallets by 
paying cash at local stores. Most transit agencies (12 out of 17) manage 
to support multiple languages in their apps. A few transit apps offer 
voice-over features and a color-blind friendly palette for customers who 
are blind or color-blind. However, one interviewee mentioned some 
remaining difficulties, such as logistic challenges of synchronizing in-
formation on offline schedules after people with limited data plans 
download them on smartphones. 
5.Discussion 
5.1. Emerging trends 
While our interviews focused on transit agencies with transit apps 
that exclusively serve their local passengers, there are other types of 
transit apps. Aggregator apps are app-based platforms that offer multi -
modal transit information in different regions or countries. Google Maps 
for Transit provides trip planning and route information in more than 
100 countries (Google Maps Transit, 2022). The Transit App2 (Transit 
app, 2022) functions in over 170 metropolitan areas around the world, 
providing transit navigation, real-time arrival information, and some -
times fare payment features (Metro, 2022; Pittsburgh Regional Transit, 
2022). Unlike aggregator apps, regional apps serve transit systems in 
certain geographic regions. Some transit agencies in proximity join the 
same app programs to allow passengers to take transit across cities 
without downloading multiple apps. For example, 26 transit systems in 
the greater Los Angeles area participated in the TAP LA app project 
(TAP, 2022). The Clipper app works as a universal transit fare payment 
tool for 24 transit services in the San Francisco Bay Area (Metropolitan 
Transportation Commission, 2020). Since a smartphone-based ticketing 
system does not require infrastructure such as validators, it enables 
interoperability across agencies. 
One emerging trend identified from the interviews was that large 
transit agencies usually favor all-in-one local exclusive transit apps 
serving all local transit modes and providing all desired functions. Apps 
with multiple functions can provide a seamless and convenient experi -
ence for passengers. It underpins the importance of assessing apps as a 
united information technology. Large agencies believed these local 
exclusive apps have the potential to serve as efficient tools for two-way 
communications and data collection. In addition to providing transit 
information to passengers, 78.2% of agency branded transit apps 
allowed users to share feedback, ask questions, and contact customer 
services. Among the interviewed agencies, 95.0% of them offered a way 
for passengers to contact agencies, and 20.0% of them enabled the se-
curity report function in their apps. Having a transit app with the most 
extensive back-end control could assist the transit agency in under -
standing its passengers. For example, one interviewee stated, “We are 
always looking at how to improve our coverage, or our frequency of bus 
routes. So more data helps sort of fine-tune the system.” 
Many interviewees mentioned if transit agencies had access to peo-
ple’s travel patterns, they would be able to send passengers contextually 
relevant information such as serve alerts, offers, and events. Local 
exclusive apps can be enhanced in many ways to promote public 
transportation. Studies showed that personalization and user engage -
ment in smartphone apps are critical for travel mode changes (Durand 
et al., 2018; Andersson et al., 2018). Incentives and gamification ele-
ments, such as rewards, coupons, prizes, avatars, badges, leaderboards, 
competitions, leveling-up, and challenges, are promising features to 
promote transit use (Shaheen, 2016). For example, Transit apps could 
encourage transit use by awarding riders credit points that could be used 
towards their future fare. Transit apps can also visualize the impact of 
transit use by displaying the calories burned, gas saved, and carbon 
dioxide emission reduced.

operators prefer aggregator apps and would like to provide underlying 
data to third-party companies instead of acquiring or developing apps by J. Using APIs and SDKs to integrate fare payment systems for multimodal 
transportation options (e.g., train, bus, paratransit, bike-sharing,

“We’re a small city in central Florida. But on the other hand, we have 75

phone without loading a particularly LYNX based application. We want to 
make sure our data is in each and every one of the different applications 
so that the passengers can choose what’s the best transportation option for 
them.” 
5.2. Improve the quality of underlying data 
As reported in Section 4.6, interviewees stated that the reliability of 
underlying data is the key to a high-quality digital customer experience. 
Our interview results showed that most transit agencies have moved 
from self-made APIs to standardized GTFS feeds to support trip planning 
and real-time vehicle information features. However, transit agencies 
often find it time-consuming and challenging to clean and validate 
GTFS/GTFS-RT feeds. Although many educational and open-source 
tools are available (Watkins et al., 2021; MobilityData, 2022), more 
resources are needed to identify and troubleshoot problems in practice. 
Results from this study further suggest that reconfiguring the back- 
office system for real-time information displays on mobile platforms is 
needed. Standardizing and modernizing the transit data exchange pro-
tocols are critical for future executions. 
5.3. Vendor management 
To support and deploy transit apps, public transit agencies often 
partner with private-sector vendors for different tasks. For instance, 
transit operators work with companies like Trapeze (Trapeze Group, 
2022) for vehicle scheduling and assignment. Some transit agencies 
outsource vehicle predictions and real-time feed provision to vendors 
such as Swiftly (Swiftly, 2022). However, transit app technologies and 
transit services have been rapidly developing and will continue to 
transform. The fast-evolving nature of transit apps poses challenges in 
collaborating with vendors. The interview results highlighted the 
importance of efficient vendor management. 
The critical components of the vendor management process include 
vendor selection, contract negotiation, performance evaluation, and risk 
management (Cleary and McLarney, 2019; Costello, 2013). Practical 
recommendations for vendor management are discussed and shared by 
the interviewees. 
First, since predicting every single feature and function needed on 
transit apps has become increasingly difficult, an interviewee shared 
some advice for the RFP (Request for Proposals) writing when selecting 
vendors: 
“We actually kept [our RFP] to core technical specs instead of onerous 
long RFPs with hundreds and hundreds of requirements…so we described 
what we wanted, not necessarily as many details of what those features 
had to be, and we had a lot of bids. And I think that worked out really well 
for us.” 
Second, when negotiating and renewing contracts with vendors, a 
robust modification protocol is needed to potentially deal with en-
hancements of products, adjustments of services, and changes in the law. 
Since the data generated by transit apps are often desired by transit 
agencies for operation improvement, terms and conditions about data sharing need to be clarified before procurements. An interviewee re-
flected on this issue: 
“I think moving forward, a transit agency needs to think about what ways 
we can engage vendors to ensure that we get the data that we want. A lot 
of discussions and thinking about data really has to happen long before 
you even have a project.” 
Third, testing and QA/QC by transit agencies or pilot users is critical 
for the success of transit app projects. The responsibilities of each party 
should be specified for performance evaluation. An interviewee stated: 
“I would say even though [QA/QC] did take longer to get the app out the 
door, it absolutely was worth it. Because the app is rock solid, it works 
extremely well. And honestly, I would say probably one of the most liked 
things about our agency.” 
Considering the level of control and costs, Section 4.4 identified five 
business models for transit agencies to consider when collaborating with 
vendors: (1) private sector shared app, (2) white-label app with minor 
customization, (3) white-label app with major customization (4) in- 
house design vendor development, and (5) in-house development. The 
first three business models align well with those raised by Brakewood 
(Brakewood, 2020) – Shared App, White Label App, and White Label 
App with Validation Hardware – which describe the mobile fare 
collection application. The choice of models would depend on the 
agency’s operational context and available resources, as epitomized by 
an interviewee, “It really depends, agency by agency, in the comfort level. 
There are options out there for everyone.” 
6.Limitation and conclusion 
This study is subject to the following limitations. First, the in-
terviewees may have biases toward the values of their apps, for which 
they serve a leadership role. Their professional experience provided rich 
information and insights, despite such inevitable selection biases. Sec-
ond, this study targeted transit agencies that developed their own offi-
cial branded transit apps. Small-size transit agencies that do not have the 
capacity to build their own apps were excluded from the interviews. 
Further studies are needed to investigate the challenges and opportu -
nities for transit operators under limited resources. Particularly, how 
smaller transit agencies might collaborate with other agencies and 
regional/local governments to pool resources and jointly invest in their 
technological infrastructure. Third, the practice of transit apps has been 
evolving rapidly in recent years and is likely to continue its fast pace of 
development in the future, requiring continued efforts to bring up-to- 
date knowledge to the field. 
This study reviewed the transit apps provided by 295 transit agencies 
in the US. As of 2021, the majority of transit agencies provided smart -
phone transit apps to passengers in their service areas. Most transit apps 
(88.1%) offered more than one of the following functions: contact in-
formation, real-time vehicle information, fare payment, trip planning, 
marking favorites, and rider alerts. We interviewed 21 representatives 
from 17 transit agencies about their experiences with their agency- 
branded official transit app projects. Transit industry leaders view 
transit apps as a critical component of their account-based fare system 
and an essential means for two-way communications with passengers. 
Based on the costs and level of control, five business models for devel -
oping transit apps were identified: private sector shared app, white-label 
app with minor customization, white-label app with major custom -
ization, in-house design vendor development, and in-house develop -
ment. We have also identified two major trends: large transit agencies 
usually favor local exclusive transit apps, which could be enhanced to 
better serve their local passengers; and small and medium sized transit 
providers prefer to join aggregator apps that could serve hundreds of 
agencies in different regions or countries. By integrating multi-modal 
transportation information, aggregator transit apps have the potential 
to grow as Mobility-as-a-Service application platforms. However, to J.
