Understanding the in ﬂuence of absorptive
capacity and ambidexterity on the process ofbusiness model change –the case of on-
premise and cloud-computing software
Johann J. Kranz, *André Hanelt & Lutz M. Kolbe
Institute of Information Systems, Georg-August-University Goettingen, Platz der Goettinger
Sieben 5, Goettingen 37073, Germany E-mail: jkranz@uni-goettingen.de
Abstract. The increasing popularity of Software as a Service has strongly af-
fected the established business model of on-premise enterprise software. Software
as a Service has distinctive characteristics of disruptive innovations that typically
create several dif ﬁculties for incumbent ﬁrms, in particular with regard to adapting
business models. To date, however, little empirical understanding exists regarding
the dynamics of business model change –a topic of special importance to the
highly dynamic software industry, which is characterised by rapid and regular
emergence of disruptive innovations. As disruptive innovations require gathering
distant knowledge and experimenting with new ideas, this study addresses theo-retical gaps regarding the role of absorptive capacity and organisational ambidex-
terity in the process of business model change owing to the emergence of a
disruptive innovation. Drawing on evidence from multiple case studies of six incum-
bent vendors of enterprise resource-planning software and informed by a thorough
review of related secondary data, we investigate the pace and path of incumbents ’
business model adaptations. We propose a theoretical model that re ﬁnes prior lit-
erature on absorptive capacity and organisational ambidexterity, particularly with
regard to the process of business model change. This study identi ﬁes further tech-
nological factors that determine how and why incumbents change business
models. In addition, our study provides in-depth insights on the technological tra-
jectory of enterprise resource-planning software switching from on-premise to
on-demand software services. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

=== PÁGINA 2 ===
INTRODUCTION
Cloud computing, which has been characterised as a disruptive innovation (DaSilva et al.,
2013), ﬂexibly provides information technology (IT) resources and services to distributed users
through the internet. Around 15% of the global cloud-computing market comprises Software as
a Service (SaaS), an on-demand software delivery service that offers ubiquitous access to ap-
plications and shares a pool of common resources managed by SaaS providers (Armbrust
et al., 2009). Although the established license model for on-premise software still dominates
the market for business software, it is losing its supremacy to SaaS (PWC, 2013). This shift also
applies to the market for enterprise resource-planning (ERP) systems. Owing to the increasing
popularity of SaaS, incumbent ERP vendors have needed to adapt their long-established busi-
ness models, which has created several major challenges.
Disruptive innovations typically have this effect because they fundamentally change the rules
of competition in established markets, often leading to the incumbents ’failure as they react too
late or falteringly (Christensen, 1997; Govindarajan & Kopalle, 2006b). While early work on dis-
ruptive innovation focused on technologies (Christensen, 1997), recent research highlights the
role of business models that lead to commercialization of the innovation (Chesbrough, 2010;
DaSilva et al., 2013). Although business models are constantly adapted, especially in high-
velocity environments like the software industry, prior research has surprisingly examined busi-
ness models predominantly from a static perspective, yielding limited evidence on the dynamics
of business models (Kamoun, 2008; Aspara et al., 2011; Lambert & Davidson, 2012). Particu-
larly understudied are internal organisational processes regarding the change from a dominant
business model to another model in response to disruptive innovations over time (Govindarajan
& Kopalle, 2006b; Ansari & Krop, 2012).
Disruptive innovations challenge a ﬁrm’s dominant logic as embedded in established busi-
ness models, routines and organisational knowledge bases (Prahalad, 2004). Therefore, dis-
ruptive innovations force companies to revalue prior intellectual capital and to identify,
acquire, assimilate and exploit new knowledge to create new offerings –an organisational
capability referred to as absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George,
2002). However, as ‘future absorptive capacity is determined by the current absorption of
new knowledge ’(Todorova & Durisin, 2007, p. 783), absorptive capacity can be a double-edged
sword in that it limits a ﬁrm’s ability to acquire new knowledge from distant and loosely coupled
knowledge domains, from which disruptive innovations typically emerge (Yu & Hang, 2010).
While absorptive capacity has a positive impact on innovations primarily based on existing
knowledge (Helfat, 1997), its effect on disruptive change is less understood. Another gap in
the literature on business model dynamics is the role of organisational ambidexterity, under-stood as ‘an organisation ’s ability to be aligned and ef ﬁcient in its management of today ’s busi-
ness demands while simultaneously being adaptive to changes in the environment ’(Raisch &
Birkinshaw, 2008, p. 375) requiring organisations to be able to explore new alternatives and ex-
ploit existing competences at the same time (March, 1991). Owing to the multiple trade-offs
arising from simultaneously engaging in exploring with new business models and exploiting
existing ones, organisational ambidexterity is of particular value for understanding business
model change –especially in times of disruptive change (Markides, 2013). This study also478 J Kranz et al.

We conducted a multiple case study analysis comprising six incumbent vendors of ERP sys-
tems to study the phenomenon in depth. Our study contributes to the information systems (IS)
literature on business model change in three major ways. First, we re ﬁne the literature on
absorptive capacity. Our results imply that not only technology-related absorptive capacity but
also the absorption of market-related knowledge and the balancing of both sources of knowl-
edge play a critical role in fostering incumbents ’ability to correctly time the required business
model changes. Second, we ﬁnd that ﬁrms able to dynamically shift between exploitation and
exploration of business models dependent on external and internal conditions have changed
business models more successfully. Third, our study provides new insights into how and why
ERP vendors have changed business models, switching from on-premise to cloud-computing
software.
BACKGROUND
The rise of SaaS in the on-premise ERP market
ERP vendors ’traditional business model consisted of selling licenses and services for on-
premise software to typically larger organisations for automating enterprise-wide processes.
With the rise of SaaS in 2000, new business model designs have emerged, delivering single
ERP functionalities on demand through the internet (Stuckenberg et al., 2011). Using SaaS en-
ables companies to focus on core competencies, because they are relieved of the burden of ﬁ-
nancing, managing and maintaining their own IT infrastructures (Armbrust et al., 2009).
Because services are provided on a pay-per-use basis, SaaS allows for the shifting of capital
expenditures to operational expenditures. Furthermore, strategic ﬂexibility increases, as sunk
costs are lower and resources can easily be scaled up or down in response to short contract
periods. Hence, the key drivers for adopting SaaS solutions are that they are readily available,
reduce vendor lock-in and minimise capital expenditures, while the main inhibitors –especially
for larger ERP enterprises –are potential performance losses, con ﬁdentiality and security con-
cerns, unanticipated costs and IT know-how drain (Benlian & Hess, 2011b).Understanding the dynamics of business model change 479

=== PÁGINA 4 ===
It is important to note that the idea of remotely providing software ‘on-demand ’through the
internet is not new. In the mid-1990s, application service providers (ASPs) allowed customers
to outsource hosting and management of client –server applications (Valente & Mitra, 2007).
However, the business model innovation failed at the time owing to low broadband penetration
and inadequate software design. In addition, the single-tenant architecture of ASPs precluded
economies of scale. In contrast, SaaS builds on a multi-tenant architecture that permits cus-
tomers to share a single code base and data structures for an application, allowing economies
of scale (McAfee, 2011).
Theoretical background
Business model research in the software industry
This study conceives of a business model as ‘a template of how a ﬁrm conducts business, how
it delivers value to stakeholders (e.g. the focal ﬁrms, customers and partners) and how it links
factor and product markets ’(Zott & Amit, 2010, p. 222). A business model is therefore com-
posed of interlocking components that specify how a product or service offering creates value
(value proposition), which customer segments are targeted, how they are served and how the
relationship is managed (customer interface), which competences, resources and partners a
company needs (infrastructure management) and how products or services produce costs
and generate revenues ( ﬁnancial aspects) (Osterwalder et al., 2005; Al-Debei, 2010). Because
business models –especially in high-velocity markets –depend heavily on IS as an enabler and
trigger of innovation (Nambisan, 2013), they are a valuable unit of analysis for IS research (Rai
& Tang, 2014). A recent analysis of prior literature identi ﬁed three important pillars of business
model research in IS: business models in IT industries, digital business models and IT support
for developing and managing business models (Veit et al., 2014). In the ﬁrst category –which is
the focus of this study –the software industry is particularly relevant for studying business
model dynamics owing to the rapid technological shifts.
Until recently, business model research has been dominated by conceptual and theoretical
contributions de ﬁning and delineating the business model concept (e.g. Osterwalder & Pigneur,
2009) and relating it to adjacent ﬁelds, such as strategy, innovation and management (e.g.
Porter, 2001; Chesbrough, 2010; Teece, 2010). Although companies –particularly those in
turbulent environments –constantly need to adapt their business models to stay successful
(Wirtz et al., 2010), the literature does not re ﬂect how and why ﬁrms change business models
over time (Kijl & Boersma, 2010). In recognition of this gap, the focus of business model
research is shifting toward a more dynamic perspective (Burkhart et al., 2011). In this regard,
research has found organisational capabilities in identifying and reacting to discontinuous inno-
vations that are of major importance (Demil & Lecocq, 2010; Aspara et al., 2011). The extent of
business model change therefore depends on the ﬁrm-speci ﬁc perceived environmental pres-
sure to adapt (Linder & Cantrell, 2000; Madjdi & Hüsig, 2011) and varies in degree from slight
adaptations to major revisions or the creation of new or combined business models (O ’Reilly &
Tushman, 2008; Cavalcante et al., 2011).

Enablers and inhibitors of disruptive business model change from an incumbent ’s
perspective
Disruptive innovations are new technologies or services that are typically cheaper or of initially
lower quality or performance than existing market offers, however still good enough to satisfy
the needs of less demanding and least-pro ﬁtable customers at the low end of the initial value
network or to create whole new value networks (Christensen, 1997; Christensen & Raynor,
2003). Owing to the inferior quality, established ﬁrms often do not regard these innovations
as seriously affecting their competitive advantage. Furthermore, incumbents tend to incremen-
tally ‘ﬁne-tune ’existing products much faster than low-end customers can take advantage of.
Hence, these users are typically the ﬁrst to adopt disruptive innovations because they value
the non-standard performance attributes (Christensen & Raynor, 2003). However, over time
disruptive innovations ’performance increases, so that they can gradually satisfy the needs of
more mainstream customers. The theory of disruptive innovation is often misunderstood as
stating that disruptors are generally start-ups and incumbents as well as predominant business
models are inevitably replaced (Danneels, 2004). However, numerous examples exist of
incumbents that have either introduced disruptive innovations themselves (e.g. Intel ’s Centrino
chip set, IBM ’s personal computer and Charles Schwab ’s online trading) or continue to offer
their established business model in parallel to a disruptive one (e.g. Lufthansa ’s low-cost
airline, Allianz ’s online direct insurance and BMW ’s car-sharing service) (Markides, 2006).
Prior research suggests that the less an emergent disruptive technology destroys the value
of an incumbent ’s existing resources, skills, and expertise and those of its customers and
partners, the higher incumbents ’propensity to make use of the technology (Tushman &
Anderson, 1986; Afuah, 2000). Tripsas (1997) further provides evidence that the pressure
of technological shifts on incumbents is attenuated if incumbents have unique assets that
cannot be easily replicated and cause signi ﬁcant switching costs for existing customers.
However, disruptive business model innovations typically require more than adapting
established technological (sub-)systems as they affect key logics of how established ﬁrms
create and capture value (Khanagha et al., 2014). Therefore, they often have a broad
impact on all business model components leading to a devaluation of established ﬁrms ’assets
and resources (Markides, 2006).

Prior literature has identi ﬁed various organisational factors in ﬂuencing incumbents ’ability to
develop disruptive innovations (see Yu & Hang, 2010 for an overview). In this study, we focus on
absorptive capacity and organisational ambidexterity (see Table 2 for de ﬁnitions of the study ’s core
constructs) as literature suggests that both organisational abilities play a key role for incumbents in
sensing, understanding and responding to changing environmental conditions in order to success-
fully revise business models (Lucas & Goh, 2009; Markides, 2013; Khanagha et al., 2014) but
lacks rich empirical evidence on how they affect the process of business model change.
O’Reilly and Tushman (2008, p. 190) propose that ﬁrms need to develop ambidexterity ‘to
compete simultaneously in both mature and emerging markets –to explore and exploit ’to be
successful in the short term and to adapt to technological changes in the long run. Other
researchers however argue that disruptive innovations ’initially inferior performance inevitably
leads managers and existing customers to pay less attention to them than to incremental or
radical innovations (Christensen & Bower, 1996; Yu & Hang, 2010). Thus, Christensen (2006)
suggests that with regard to disruptive innovations, organisations cannot pursue both, explore
disruptive opportunities and exploit existing assets. Thus, he concludes that ﬁrms should set
up autonomous business units that are independent of the traditional business. These units
should be free to experiment and embrace an organisational culture that motivates employees
to take risks, develop creative ideas and behave like ‘in-house entrepreneurs ’(Govindarajan &
Kopalle, 2006a). Another much debated question is whether organisations should alternate
between explorative and exploitative periods (Nickerson & Zenger, 2002) or should simulta-
neously conduct explorative and exploitative activities (Tushman & O ’Reilly, 2002; Raisch &
Birkinshaw, 2008). To integrate both views, researchers recently have proposed to conceptual-
ise ambidexterity on a continuum between exploration and exploitation de ﬁning the focus of
ambidexterity as an intermediate point between the two ends of the continuum (Lavie &Rosenkopf, 2006; Luger et al., 2013).

Disruptive innovations require ﬁrms to accept that they devalue their prior intellectual capital.
Incumbent ﬁrms therefore need to develop absorptive capacity, which allows them to constantly
identify and acquire new knowledge and to assimilate and apply this knowledge (Cohen &
Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). For disruptive innovations, however, absorptive capac-
ity may be both a blessing and a curse as it strongly depends on prior related knowledge
(Roberts et al., 2012). Therefore, the acquisition of external knowledge is path dependent, in
that past experiences and decisions determine future absorptive capacity in a self-reinforcing
way (Todorova & Durisin, 2007; Sydow et al., 2009). Path dependency generally leads to ﬁrms
get stuck in a speci ﬁc‘corridor of limited scope of action ’(Sydow et al., 2009p. 704). Thus, while
absorptive capacity increases the depth of knowledge in domains in which the ﬁrm is already
active, it may also limit a ﬁrm’s ability to acquire knowledge from other domains, which would
help to increase the breadth of knowledge. As disruptive innovations typically originate from
distant and loosely coupled knowledge domains and require major changes in established
business models, the role of absorptive capacity is important, even though it is understudied
(Lane et al., 2006).
Prior research has largely neglected the fact that the concept of absorptive capacity encom-
passes not only technological knowledge but also market knowledge (Lane et al., 2006). Market
knowledge provides ﬁrms with insights on how to commercialise technological knowledge
(Teece, 2007) and increases the organisational capability to solve complex problems in other
domains (Weigelt & Sarkar, 2012), which is especially important for disruptive innovations
because they initially appeal to new customer segments. Hence, a unilateral focus of absorptive
capacity on technological knowledge, as well as on knowledge closely related to existing
intellectual capital, may harm an incumbent ’s ability to respond adequately to emerging
disruptive innovations. We follow Roberts et al. (2012) in conceptualising absorptive capacity
as a capability encompassing organisational routines and processes regarding the absorption
of knowledge and the ability to adjust those dynamically, whereas much of the prior literature
treated absorptive capacity as an asset (e.g. number of patents).
To summarise, literature suggests that to identify and assess the potential of disruptive
innovations, absorptive capacity is essential, while ambidexterity is a vital organisational ability
for integrating and reconciling exploratory and exploitative activities with regard to new and
established business models. The importance of the two constructs for the process of business
model change is indicated by Lucas and Goh (2009, p. 54) who state that regarding disruptive
innovation ‘the most important observation is that management has to recognise the threats
and opportunities of new information and communications technologies and marshal capabili-
ties for change ’. Our theory-based re ﬂections and their relationships are captured in Figure 1.

METHODS
We use a multiple-case study approach to investigate the complex temporal process of business
model change in depth. We chose a multi-case design because it allows study of complex
organisational processes ‘involving multiple actors and interacting sequences of events ’
(Bhattacherjee, 2012, p. 95) and comparison of the ﬁndings among ﬁrms, resulting in higher external
validity of the results (Yin, 2009). As we aim at extending theory, our research can be categorised as
a positivist exploratory case study (Benbasat et al., 1987; Paré, 2004; Yin, 2009). We used replication
logic to identify consistent patterns across cases , treating each case as an experiment that con ﬁrms
or discon ﬁrms emerging theory (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2003). Table 3 summarises how we ad-
dressed key methodological guidelines (Klein & Myers, 1999; Paré, 2004; Yin, 2009).
We chose the software industry as our research setting because its high-velocity nature
makes it an attractive context for the study of dynamic changes (Martin, 2011) and it is
frequently confronted with potentially disrupting digital technologies (Veit et al., 2014). We
speci ﬁcally focus on the market for ERP systems because it is one of the most established
segments within the enterprise software market, making it an interesting setting for examining
business model change.
Our research sample contains six software vendors (Table 4). Because we seek to examine
how and why business models changed, our sample did not include ERP vendors that did not
change business models. We selected the cases following a theoretical replication design (Yin,
2009) with the aim of increasing the variance regarding the observable timing and extent of
business model change (Bhattacherjee, 2012). In each case, we conducted interviews until
we reached a point of saturation such that additional interviews yielded no new ﬁndings with ref-
erence to our research questions (Paré, 2004).
Data collection
Our study relies on various data sources comprising interviews and follow-up emails with 26
informants along with internal sources (e.g. reports, blogs and presentations) and external
documents (e.g. ﬁnancial reports and market analyses) collected over a period of 14 years.
On the basis of these data, we were able to verify the consistency of interviewees ’statements
on past developments. Using multiple data sources allowed triangulation of the data to increase
internal validity and mitigate potential biases (Yin, 2009). We stopped conducting further
interviews if a comprehensive and consistent understanding of the cases could be established.
Drawing on prior theorising, we designed a semi-structured interview guide consisting of
open questions (Appendix A). Interviewees came from different organisational departments
and hierarchy levels and had signi ﬁcant experience in the ﬁeld of business software. The inter-
view length ranged from 48 to 97 min (median: 68 min), and all interviews were tape recorded
and transcribed usually within 24 h.

Change patterns of incumbents ’business models
All of the incumbents we investigated did not terminate their established on-premise business
model but instead began to additionally offer SaaS. We therefore analysed an incumbent ’s busi-

and the emerging SaaS business model. The temporal evolution of SaaS business models that
emerge from our data can be categorised into three stages as depicted in Figure 2. In the
following, we elucidate how and when our case companies changed their business models,
separately for each business model component.

Because SaaS users need only a computing device and a Web browser to use the software, in

Customer interface
According to our interviewees, SaaS initially attracted low-end customer segments and prior

For smaller ﬁrms, ERP was a solution from an ivory tower, too complex and costly to imple-

Then, lines of business with standardised processes across industries (e.g. human

to demand SaaS as it is easily accessible from anywhere at any time and more ﬂexible in terms

security considerably in ﬂuence SaaS adoption. Some interviewees noted that particularly,
customers from Western European are reluctant to use cloud software because of concerns
regarding data security, which were fuelled by recent revelations of international espionage.
Software as a Service has changed the signi ﬁcance of distribution channels through which
customers are reached. Interviewees observed that customers interested in SaaS now prefer
to gather pre-purchase information on the internet and test downloadable trial versions of
different providers. Thus, the internet gains weight as a direct distribution channel affecting
indirect distribution networks, which many of our investigated ERP vendors rely on heavily in
the on-premise ERP market.

adapting and building new competencies.

DISCUSSION OF FINDINGS
The ﬁndings of our case studies led us to re ﬁne our initial model as depicted in Figure 3. First,
we found that we need to differentiate the concept of absorptive capacity by accounting for the
type of knowledge involved. In our sample, we found signi ﬁcant differences regarding ﬁrms ’
ability to absorb market and technology knowledge. While the majority of case companies
had developed a suf ﬁcient absorptive capacity regarding market and technological knowledge
relevant to the ERP market, many ﬁrms lacked breadth in absorptive capacity concerning adja-
cent markets and general environmental trends. This lack limited their capacity to make sense
of and connect weak market and environmental signals, an ability crucial for correctly assessing
an innovation ’s contemporary and general disruptive potential (Weigelt & Sarkar, 2012). Be-
cause disruptive innovations often emerge from technology domains and customer needs dis-
tinct from a ﬁrm’s established base, possession of a broad sensing scope that includes a variety
of external sources is important (March, 1991). Firms in our sample with a narrow focus on iden-
tifying knowledge relevant to their established ERP business model failed to recognise emerg-
ing customer needs and technology opportunities. Their assessment of SaaS was strongly
biased by their focus on existing customers and partners –a restriction that resulted in ‘cogni-
tive lock-ins ’that limited their ability to sense potential new dominant business models in the
ERP market (Prahalad, 2004).
Beyond that, we identi ﬁed differences between ﬁrms regarding their ability to balance and
connect market-oriented and technology-oriented knowledge, which we refer to as balancing
capability. We found that an imbalance between market-related and technology-related absorp-
tive capacity results in ﬁrms either underestimating or overestimating a disruptive innovation ’s
market or technology potential, leading to misalignments regarding the required timing and ex-
tent of business model change. Only when market and technology knowledge are balanced
and combined, with accurate connections with each other, can value be created (Shane,
2000; Song et al., 2005). By drawing on the ability to absorb both market and technology knowl-
edge of the ERP and other software markets from a large variety of sources, GammaCo could
balance the two sources of knowledge, allowing the company to sense when to put emphasis
on exploration. In contrast, the technology-biased companies such as AlphaCo and ZetaCo
lacked adequate market knowledge. Their assessment of SaaS was strongly technology

IMPLICATIONS
Theoretical implications
This study addresses theoretical and empirical gaps in the IS literature on business model
dynamics. Overall, the ﬁndings of our study contribute re ﬁned constructs and theory that
explain capabilities of business model change and the relationships among them. We now
address the research questions outlined earlier, elucidating the major elements of the revised
theoretical framework (Figure 3). This study contributes to the IS literature on business models
in three major ways. First, our study re ﬁnes theory on absorptive capacity regarding business
model change resulting from the emergence of disruptive digital technologies. Prior literature
conceptualised absorptive capacity predominantly as technological or scienti ﬁc knowledge
(Lane et al., 2006). However, disruptive innovations often build on inferior technologies and
initially attract non-users or low-end customers (Christensen, 1997). Hence, a single-sided
focus on technology knowledge can have negative consequences. As our ﬁndings indicate,
especially for disruptive innovations, the ability to absorb knowledge from various sources on
the target and adjacent markets as well as general environmental trends is essential for the
right timing and extent of business model change. Therefore, regarding the debate about
market-orientation vs. technology-orientation (Govindarajan & Kopalle, 2006b), our ﬁndings502 J Kranz et al.

external environment demands business model adaptations, which can be regarded as a
dynamic capability (Teece et al., 1997).

Limitations and further research
In this study, we have proposed a tentative model of factors in ﬂuencing the process of business
model change based on the evidence of six case studies with established ERP vendors. As is
the case for any theoretical model, our model simpli ﬁes some of the complex nonlinear relation-
ships between social and psychological variables. However, as we checked for face validity and
consistency in our data, the relationships accurately re ﬂect the interviewees ’beliefs and attitudes.
Nevertheless, generalisation of the ﬁndings requires quantitative approaches that test the model.
Future research should further study business model change from different theoretical perspec-
tives focusing on other in ﬂuencing factors such as institutional logics or strategic orientation.
Further research could also investigate the transferability of our model to different contexts
because the software environment is a high-velocity environment in which change occurs
