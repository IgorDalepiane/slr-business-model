FEATURE ARTICLE
Software as a service: Lessons from the video game industry
Fanny Vaudour | Aleksej Heinze
Digital transformation has prompted some organizations to use a service busi-
ness model that does not require the purchase of a product but, rather, use of
their services. Software-as-a-service (SaaS) is a method by which service pro-
viders meet the needs of their clients via an internet server. A look at con-
sumers' reactions to the games-as-a-service (GaaS) business model used in the
computer games industry illustrates how the model has transformed the indus-try. It also highlights the importance of timeliness, transparency, and creativity
in any SaaS business's efforts to attract and retain customers and optimize
profitability.
1|INTRODUCTION
A business model describes how an organization creates,
delivers, and captures value (Osterwalder & Pigneur,2010). Software-as-a-service (SaaS) is a method used by
service providers to distribute software-based services to
clients via a single server, which may or may not beowned by a third party. Some prominent examples of
SaaS are Microsoft Office 365, Google Apps, Dropbox,
and Amazon Web Services. Concentrating the provisionof software on a centralized server guarantees synchro-nous distribution, saves time and money, and eases the
problems of scalability and compatibility that occur when
software is distributed via physical media, such as CDs.SaaS is one of the main applications of cloud computing,
and business models based on SaaS have been adopted by
several industries —most notably, companies that develop
and publish computer games.
The provision of multiplayer and standalone games
via the internet —essentially a variant of SaaS —has led to
the growth of a distinct games-as-a-service (GaaS) busi-
ness model, where all the software needed to play the
game is located on an external server (Ali, Sultan,Ghani, & Zulzalil, 2019). Although GaaS business modelshave become increasingly popular in the games industry,
the topic is relatively new and deserves further study
(Choi & Chen, 2019).
One of the first GaaS video games, World of Warcraft,
was released in 2004. GaaS-based video games becamevery popular in the following years, and sales have con-
tinued to grow. Video game revenues in the United States
have nearly doubled, from USD 17.5 billion in 2010 to
USD 29.1 billion in 2017. Compared to the sales of physi-cal media, digital sales —including subscriptions, full dig-
ital games, downloadable content, mobile apps, and
social network games —have more than doubled. Gaming
software delivered via physical media, which accounted
for two-thirds of sales in 2010, has now largely been rep-
laced by digital delivery (Entertainment Software Associ-ation, 2018).

2|THE GAMES-AS-A-SERVICE
BUSINESS MODEL
The main business model initially used in the video
games industry initially was “one price, one service. ”
Essentially, players bought a game that could be com-pleted from beginning to end in a matter of days, weeks,
or months. When the game was over, there was nothing
left to do but replay the same game. The adoption of theSaaS approach to software distribution led to a significant
change in players' habits. The instant availability of digi-
tally delivered games meant that players began to moveaway from traditional boxed products toward online ver-
sions. The cost was the same, and they were available for
play immediately after being downloaded. Consumersdid not have to bother with finding a retailer, or sufferthe disappointment of being told that the game they were
hoping to purchase had sold out. If they wanted to buy a
game at 3:00 a.m. on a Sunday, they could.
Digital delivery also changed the dynamics of the
games market in other ways. Previously, developers
would create a sequel to a popular game to generate addi-tional revenue. With the GaaS model, they can update a
game on the fly —that is, release as many updates as they
want, whenever they want —allowing all players to
receive them as soon as they connect to the server. With
GaaS-based games, players never really finish a game
because it keeps evolving.
Developers and publishers can extend the life of a
game by continually adding new content —commonly
referred to as downloadable content, or DLC —as
required. DLCs can take many different forms, and maybe free or need to be purchased. Some examples include
new skins (changing the appearance of a character),levels and quests (to improve the game's storyline), maps
and characters, and gaming modes.
Now conducted year-round, game development has
become an ongoing rather than a discrete activity. Game
developers and publishers realize, however, that gamerswill continue to play the game and remain loyal only for
as long as their expectations are met (Sinclair, 2017).
Consequently, if a game is to be successful, developersneed to keep players engaged and continue to satisfy
their demands over periods of months or possibly even
years.

To be effective as a business model, GaaS must generate
revenue. Games developers have implemented several
different monetization methods and, as their colleaguesin other industries have done, often incorporate more
than one technique into a single approach.
Most GaaS business models rely on powerful cus-
tomer relationship management (CRM) systems to seg-
ment users and to follow up on gamers' behavior
(NoBlue, 2018). In addition to monitoring gamers' behav-ior, these systems are also used to manage and approverevenue-generating transactions, such as those described
below.
Most GaaS business models
rely on powerful customerrelationship management.
Microtransactions —These are micropayments made
online to purchase virtual goods in a game. Apart from
being a source of revenue, microtransactions increase
players' engagement by offering them attractive items orin-game features.
For example, one popular form of microtransaction
offers users a way to skip parts of the game. Gamers canbuy access to new content without having to invest time
normally spent in building their game characters or
working their way through the lower levels of the game,colloquially referred to as “the grind. ”Instead of grind-
ing, gamers can reach a certain level directly by purchas-
ing it through a microtransaction. The timing of suchmicrotransactions in the game is critical, however: Ifthey interrupt the gaming process, they can lead to a loss
of earnings for the game developer and publisher
(Tomi /C19c, 2019).

6.1 |Is the GaaS business model
sustainable?
GaaS has been a game changer in the games industry,
but at what cost? With the emergence of this new busi-ness model, players' expectations have risen: They now
want more content and a better gaming experience for
less money.
Microtransactions allow
players to trade money for
gaming time, and can destroyany sense of skills-based
competition.
This has encouraged some developers and publishers
to push boundaries and make what could be perceived as
false claims for their games. For example, it was impossi-
ble to play EA's entire Battlefront II game for free; toenjoy all its features, it was necessary to pay at somepoint. Such requirements strengthen the view among
gamers that certain publishers are only concerned with
making money. Although most gamers understand thatgames developers and publishers are commercial enter-
prises, a game's perceived value is critical to its success.
As illustrated in Exhibit 8, cost is the main reason why agamer stops playing a game.
For some gamers, the revenue-generation aspects of
the GaaS business model seem exploitative. For example,several games have slowly drifted into a “pay-to-win ”
mode (Moeller, 2018), which gives an advantage to
players with greater purchasing power. Microtransactions
allow players to trade money for gaming time, and candestroy any sense of skills-based competition. Players can
skip some parts of the game and reach the top directly,
but ultimately will know less about it than those whospend the time grinding to discover all its secrets
(Makuch, 2016). In addition, such tactics may demotivate
top players who face unfair competition from playerswho have paid to win (Klubnikin, 2018).
Related to these concerns is the debate concerning
whether opting for loot boxes should be considered gam-bling. It has been shown that loot boxes have the capacityto manipulate players' behavior and can result in a signif-
icant outlay of time and money. In the long term, they
can leave players with a sense of regret and frustration(Wiltshire, 2017).7|BEYOND GAMING
Looking at the experiences of the gaming industry, argu-
ments can be made both for and against GaaS. Extrapo-
lating beyond gaming, there are three key implicationsfor other business models based on SaaS.
Offer minimum viable service to foster goodwill —It
seems clear that a software service needs to provide satis-faction for its users if it is to be used. The key point here
concerns building a minimum viable service (MVS) —that
is, a service with enough features to satisfy enough cus-tomers to justify its future service development.
Although free-to-play games are readily available and
allow gamers to play without having to buy anything,
most GaaS games ultimately require some form of pay-ment if they are to be enjoyed to the full, and this can
leave some players feeling cheated. When a games com-
pany first launches an “unfinished ”service, it needs to
reach its MVS quickly to avoid losing users' goodwill.
Similar considerations apply to SaaS, particularly when
launching a new service. For example, when Dropbox, acloud-based file storage and file-sharing app, was
launched, it needed to offer enough functionality to meet
the needs of users without driving them away before con-
verting them into paying customers.
Ensure transparent cost structures to maintain existing
customers —For SaaS to work, there needs to be a trans-
parent cost structure that clearly states the level of invest-ment needed to fully benefit from the software.
The results of this survey showed that some gamers

bought DLCs. In the same way that failing to provide
MVS in a new offering can turn away users before theybecome paying customers, failure to provide a transpar-ent pricing structure can turn away existing customers.
For example, Microsoft Office 365 has been criticized for
having hidden costs for companies that migrate fromstandalone Office applications to the cloud-based
Office 365.
