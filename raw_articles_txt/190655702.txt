Stefano Colombo ·Luca Grilli ·

Accepted: 11 May 2013 / Published online: 2 June 2013

Abstract This paper proposes a formal model that analyzes the degree of openness
chosen by start-ups when entering the software industry. In line with the literature, welabel as degree of openness the extent to which software start-ups mix open source
(OS) and proprietary solutions in the portfolio of software products they offer. We
relate the choice of the degree of openness to two key characteristics of the marketsegments in which software start-ups operate: the strength of the network externalities
and the competitive advantage of the incumbent. Speciﬁcally, by modelling (price)
competition between an incumbent and an entrant in two ways, i.e., the entrant is price-
setter or price-taker, we derive the necessary condition(s) in terms of the strength of
network externalities for observing the adoption of a business model that comprisesthe offering of both proprietary and OS solutions by the entrant (i.e., hybrid business
model). Then, we highlight that, if a hybrid business model is the choice, the degree of
openness chosen in equilibrium increases along with both the strength of the networkexternalities and the competitive advantage of the incumbent. This result holds indif-
ferently whether the software start-up is modelled as a price-setter or a price-taker. An
empirical test run on a sample of European start-ups in the software industry supportsthese theoretical predictions.

1 Introduction
Although open source (OS) was born as an ideological movement, it has rapidly
evolved into a business opportunity for software ﬁrms, which often rely on the codeand knowledge created by the OS community to run their business ( Fitzgerald 2006 ).
Due to the collective good nature of OS software ( Johnson 2005 ;Bitzer and Schröder
2005 ), the involvement of for-proﬁt ﬁrms in the OS realm has puzzled economics and
management scholars ( Lerner and Tirole 2002 ;Bonaccorsi and Rossi 2003 ). Accord-
ingly, since the last decade, increasing research effort has been devoted to inquire themulti-faceted linkages between software ﬁrms and the OS world. Researchers have
extensively discussed why andhow software ﬁrms get involved in the OS movement,
backing their speculations with rich qualitative and quantitative evidence (see von
Krogh et al. 2012 , for a recent review of this literature).
Investigations on how software ﬁrms build up business models which entail the
offering of software solutions based on OS have been at the core of the research streamon ﬁrm involvement in the OS world (e.g., Hecker 1999 ;Krishnamurthy 2006 ). In this
framework, Bonaccorsi et al. (2006 ) have shown that the majority of the software ﬁrms
that provide OS-based solutions mix the offering of OS and proprietary software. Theauthors have referred to these ﬁrms as hybrid ﬁrms (i.e., ﬁrms that adopt a hybrid
business model ). Moreover, they have empirically analysed the determinants of their
degree of openness , which they deﬁned as a multi-dimensional construct reﬂecting the
extent to which ﬁrms mix OS and proprietary solutions in their product portfolio.
1
However, the work of Bonaccorsi et al. (2006 ) represents an exception and the study
ofhybrid ﬁrms and of what determines their degree of openness has gone rather under-
remarked. This is fairly surprising in the light of the real-world relevance of hybrid
ﬁrms . Basing on a large scale survey taken on a wide range of countries, Lerner and
Schankerman (2010 ) have documented that many software ﬁrms offer both proprietary
and OS software. The authors have extensively speculated on the reasons why these
hybrid ﬁrms exist and have empirically showed that they comingle OS and proprietary
software along several dimensions of their business model. Nevertheless, theorizing
on what determines hybrid ﬁrms ’ degree of openness is still lagging behind.
To start ﬁlling this gap, the present paper builds theory on how the offering of OS
solutions by hybrid ﬁrms relates to the characteristics of the market in which these
ﬁrms operate. Speciﬁcally, we develop a formal model where the choice of the optimal
degree of openness is studied in the context of an entry model, where a new entrant
ﬁrm (i.e., software start-up ) faces a well-established incumbent in a competitive envi-
ronment characterized by network externalities. For sake of simplicity, we assume that
customers are not hostile to OS solutions. This is not unrealistic. OS is diffusing veryrapidly: several OS products (such as the Linux operating system or the Apache Web

on a sample of European start-ups in the software industry supports these theoretical
predictions.

2 Network Externalities, Incumbent’s Competitive Advantage, and the Choice
of the Degree of Openness by Software Start-Ups
Section 3reports our formal model on the choice of the degree of openness by a soft-
ware start-up that faces a well-established incumbent in a competitive environment
characterized by network externalities. Accordingly, this section frames the impor-
tance of network externalities and incumbent’s competitive advantage for the choice
of the degree of openness by software start-ups,4thus speaking in favour of the rele-
vance of our theoretical analysis.
The leading role that network externalities (Katz and Shapiro 1986 ;Farrell and
Saloner 1986 ) play in the software industry has been extensively acknowledged by
scholars ( Shapiro and Varian 1998 ). It is common wisdom that because of network
externalities the market success of a software solution strictly depends on its installed
base ( Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996 ). A potential adopter would prefer to choose
the most diffused software solution so as to join the widest users’ network ( Gandal
1995 ). In so doing, she would be able to exchange ﬁles and know-how with a large
pool of users and to beneﬁt from the rich availability of compatible applications.
Due to the novelty of the OS software phenomenon, the aforementioned seminal
contributions in network externality literature have neither explicitly nor formally
addressed how the strength of network externalities shapes the choice of the degree of
openness by ﬁrms operating in software markets. More speciﬁcally, in this early liter-
ature, the notion of openness resembles the concept of compatibility and the strategic
choice between openness versus closeness echoes the one of compatibility versus non
compatibility (e.g., Katz and Shapiro 1992 ). In this perspective, the importance for a
ﬁrm operating in a market characterized by network externalities of getting the correct
“rights management” strategy for the products it offers equates to the need of being
sufﬁciently “open” to the external world, i.e., consumers and/or other ﬁrms, and not
too greedy for (short-run) proﬁts. Accordingly, the traditional literature on network

Nevertheless, it is worth remarking that in case of OS software, the notion of openness
has a different connotation, contemplating a juridical rather than a technical meaning.
The concept of openness in the OS realm does not equate the one of compatibility.
Indeed, a software can be OS (i.e., protected by IPRs that favor instead of forbidding

OS software arena.

As to the second market-related factor here analyzed, contributions on hybrid ﬁrms
have left largely under-researched the role that incumbent ﬁrms play in shaping the
choice of the degree of openness by new entrants. Investigating this role is undoubtedly
worthy. The OS movement was born as a reaction against the monopoly power of largeplayers in the software industry. This initial footprint has not vanished since OS has
gone closer to the business arena (Raymond 2001).

Indeed, evidence exists that users of OS solutions usually differ from the average user of
proprietary software ( DiBona et al. 1999 ). In general, OS users are skilled in computer
science (e.g., system administrators or software developers). They behave as advanced
users and use OS as an input to their own production processes—i.e., the developmentof other software solutions ( Bogers et al. 2010 ). Conversely, users of proprietary
solutions are normally end-users, who use the software to satisfy their personal needs
(e.g., making calculations with a spreadsheet or writing a document with a wordprocessor). Thus, “going open source” and offering OS solutions may allow start-ups
to capture a residual demand which is not fully covered by proprietary incumbents.

the offering of OS solutions as a way to be independent from the pricing and license
strategies of large proprietary incumbents in the software industries.
To sum up, basing on the discussion above, it is reasonable to expect that both the
strength of network externalities and incumbent’s competitive advantage do play a
signiﬁcant role in the choice of the degree of openness by software start-ups. Accord-ingly, the formal model we develop in the next section centres on these two prominent
aspects.

variable was multiplied by C1 to obtain a proxy of the competitive disadvantage expe-rienced by the ﬁrm in that market segment. For each ﬁrm, the products of the seven
dummy variables with the corresponding C1 indexes were summed and this sum was
divided for the number of market segments where the software start-up is present,in order to obtain a synthetic measure of the structure of competition faced by the
start-up.
We insert in the model a series of control variables. PRICE is an ordinal variable
assuming value 1 if, according to the respondent ﬁrm, its customers are rather insen-
sitive to price when choosing a software, value 2 if they attach a medium level of
importance to price, and value 3 if they attach high importance to price. SIZE is thetotal staff of the ﬁrm, as measured by the number of employees, working partners, and
freelancers. AGE is the age of the software start-up, as measured by the time elapsed
since the ﬁrm’s foundation, as of 2004 (the year of the survey). Country dummies arealso inserted.

5 Concluding Remarks
This paper investigates the impact of network externalities and incumbent’s competi-
tive advantage on the degree of openness that software start-ups choose when entering
the software industry. Speciﬁcally, we model competition between an incumbent and a
new entrant and highlights that strong (weak) network externalities determine the nec-essary condition for observing entrance of a price-setter (price-taker) software start-up
in the market through a hybrid business model. Conversely, when network externali-
ties are sufﬁciently weak (strong), a price-setter (price-taker) software start-up’s best
123

strategy is always to pursue the maximum degree of openness and constitute itself
as a pure OS ﬁrm. Finally, we show that when the entrant chooses a hybrid business
model, its degree of openness increases along with both the strength of the networkexternalities and the competitive advantage of the incumbent. This latter result holds
irrespectively of whether the entrant competes in price with the incumbent or not,
thus being price-setter or price-taker. An econometric exercise run on a sample ofstart-ups adopting a hybrid business models brings empirical support to the results of
the aforementioned formal analysis. Namely, it shows that the degree of openness of
hybrid software start-ups is positively related to the strength of network externalities
and the competitive advantage of the incumbent.
The paper contributes to the literature studying the economic and managerial aspects
of the OS phenomenon. In line with the works by Lerner and Tirole (2002 ) and Bonac-
corsi and Rossi (2003 ), our paper corroborates the idea that the open source puzzle
can be traced back to the mainstream economic discourse. Indeed, our model takesshape from the consideration that for-proﬁt ﬁrms make choices about their engage-
ment in the OS world (i.e., they participate in the private provision of a collective
good) based on proﬁt-maximisation considerations. In other words, the mix (to dif-ferent extents) OS and proprietary solutions turns out to be a rational strategy for
a proﬁt-maximising ﬁrm in response to certain market conditions. Furthermore, the
paper heeds the call for more theorising in OS research ( Dalle et al. 2007 ), thus diverg-
ing from the phenomenon-based approach that characterises academic research on OS
(von Krogh et al. 2012 ).
22Finally, the research strand that has studied how OS shapes
competition in the software industry (von Krogh and von Hippel 2006 ) has mainly

While the proprietary incumbent only sells proprietary solutions, the OS community

so common in the real world. Few recent contributions have overcome this deﬁciency
and considered the possibility of hybrid strategies by for-proﬁt ﬁrms. These works
have focused on the incumbent’s choice of adding OS solutions to its proprietary
offering or have analysed the choice of the degree of openness in relation to speciﬁc
software characteristics ( Casadesus-Masanell and Llanes 2011 ;Comino and Manenti
2011 ). Conversely, the locus of investigation is here on the choice of the degree of
openness by new entrants depending on important characteristics of the competitive
environment in which they operate.
The study has limitations that open up avenues for future research. In developing
our model, we have assumed that customers are not hostile to OS. This is undoubtedly
true as regards to advanced users (see the Introduction). However, such an assumptionmight be challenged when considering non-advanced users, who are scared by (per-
ceived) incompatibility between OS and proprietary products and by switching costs
in jumping between the two standards. More importantly, our paper is not intended

Its scope is limited to offering a stripped-down theoretical model for weighing vari-

are aware that in the real world the choice of the degree of openness by OS start-
ups also depends on a plethora of company-speciﬁc, idiosyncratic motives and, mostimportantly in this context, by other market factors. First, start-ups which offer OS
solutions can develop software at lower costs as they rely on the community of OS
programmers for code writing and debugging. How different costs in software devel-opment between entrant and incumbent shape their competition is left aside from the
model, but it could be another important element to be considered when studying the
determinants of start-ups’ degree of openness. Second, hybrid business models are
increasingly adopted also by large software incumbents (e.g., IBM, see Campbell-
Kelly and Garcia-Swartz 2009 ) and this strategy clearly impacts new entrants’ degree
of openness. In our model, we have considered a “pure proprietary” incumbent and
the choice of the degree of openness when a start-up enters the software market. How-
ever, ﬁrms update choices about openness during their life cycle in response to changesof the competitive environment. Accordingly, a proprietary incumbent can decide to
include OS solutions in its offering, while a start-up can modify its degree of open-
ness as it ages. Finally, it is worth noting that OS ﬁrms may supply highly specialisedproducts and face the competition of other ﬁrms doing business with OS in market
niches. From this perspective, how choices of the degree of openness are taken in a
more fragmented market landscape represents another interesting development of ouranalysis.
Despite these limitations, the model offers interesting insights to perspective entre-
preneurs who aim to enter the software industry. OS is currently so widespread thatmost new entrants face the problem of choosing the “right” degree of openness. Despite
the allegedly heterogeneity of the start-ups that prospective entrepreneurs are going
to set up, the challenges posed by incumbent’s presence and by network externalities
are ubiquitous across software market segments.
